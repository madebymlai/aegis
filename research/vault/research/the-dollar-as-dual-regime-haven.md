# The Dollar as a Dual-Regime Haven

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> UUP is the most regime-robust haven in our universe because the dollar smile makes it gain in both global risk-off shocks and US rate shocks, but that hedge rests on the dollar's reserve-currency convenience yield and breaks precisely when the US is the source of stress or the Fed is easing - so the design the evidence supports is a carry-conditioned UUP floor, held cheaply when US short rates top the foreign basket and backed off when they do not.

The aegis universe contains one instrument that gained in every crash of the test window. In 2018, when SPY fell 4.56%, TLT fell 1.61%, GLD fell 1.94% and the slow-trend champion lost double digits, the dollar finished the year positive - the only haven that worked.[^bespoke] It spiked in the March-2020 dash for cash and climbed through the 2022 rate shock. The universe-mapping article flagged UUP as the dual-regime standout and left a hypothesis open: that UUP-heavy weighting is the most consistent crash-gainer across both growth and inflation shocks.[^universe] This article supplies the mechanism behind that observation, names the regimes where it fails, and resolves the design question the runs need answered - whether to hold the dollar always or only on a trigger.

## Why the dollar gains at both extremes: the smile

The organizing idea is Stephen Jen's "dollar smile," from around 2001: the relationship between the dollar and US growth is non-monotonic, so the dollar strengthens at both ends of the growth distribution and weakens in the middle.[^jen] The framework is a practitioner construct from the author's own firm and carries a clear conflict of interest, but its three regimes each rest on independent, higher-grade evidence, so the components are worth more than the brand.

The **left side** is risk-off. In a global growth or liquidity shock, capital deleverages into the safest, most liquid asset - dollar cash and US Treasuries - regardless of US fundamentals, because the dollar is the world's funding and reserve currency. Avdjiev, Bruno, Koch and Shin establish the dollar as a global risk factor: a stronger broad dollar tightens global financial conditions, cuts cross-border bank flows and depresses real investment, operating through borrower and lender balance sheets rather than through trade.[^avdjiev] Lustig, Roussanov and Verdelhan supply the asset-pricing counterpart - the dollar carry trade earns a premium for being long the dollar in bad global states, which is to say the dollar predictably appreciates when global risk spikes.[^lustig] The cleanest live demonstration is March 2020: in the dash for cash, sovereign dollar-reserve sales ran above 80% of total reserve sales against the dollar's roughly 60% reserve share, and the offshore dollar funding cost (the FX-swap basis) blew out from about 20 basis points in January to roughly 150 in mid-March.[^nyfed]

The **right side** is a US rate shock. When US growth and inflation run hot and the Fed tightens, widening real-yield differentials pull capital into the dollar. 2022 is the case in point: from early March the advanced-economy dollar index rose about 13% over six months as hiking turned synchronous and risk aversion rose, and the broad dollar peaked up roughly 20% before finishing the year up 8.2%.[^dallasfed][^macrosights] This is the regime where the Treasury hedge fails - bonds fell with stocks - and the dollar is the haven that still pays.

The **middle** is the cost. In synchronized global growth with no crisis and a US current-account drag, capital rotates out of the dollar into higher-yielding non-US assets, and the dollar drifts down - the 2003-04 and 2010-14 stretches, when the broad dollar fell on the order of 9%.[^jen] An always-on dollar position pays this drift as its calm-market drag, which is the crux of the floor-versus-trigger question below.

## When the dollar does not rally: the failure regimes

The dual-regime case is real but conditional, and the conditions are where the runs are most likely to be surprised. The dollar's haven status rests on its reserve-currency convenience yield - the liquidity and safety premium foreigners pay to hold dollar assets - and it fails whenever that premium is the thing under attack. Maurer, Tô and Tran put the counter most sharply: the dollar is *not* a traditional funding-currency safe haven like the yen or Swiss franc; it appreciates only temporarily in ordinary risk-off, and the United States' large negative net-foreign-asset position since the global financial crisis sits awkwardly with durable safe-haven status.[^maurer] Habib and Stracca reach a milder version of the same point - the dollar qualifies as a haven but messier than the yen or franc, whose strength is better explained by funding-currency unwind.[^habib]

Three failure regimes follow, each with episode evidence.

When the **US is the source of the stress**, foreigners sell dollar assets rather than buy them. April 2025 is the clean modern break: in the tariff shock the dollar *depreciated* about 3% while Treasury yields *rose* - the inverse of normal risk-off - with roughly 47 billion dollars of long-Treasury outflows in a month that typically sees comparable inflows, a move the ECB and others read as a fall in the convenience yield itself.[^ecb] This is the regime a dollar floor cannot hedge, because the dollar is the asset losing value.

When the **Fed eases aggressively** and the rate differential collapses, the right side of the smile inverts. The dollar weakened into and after 2008-09 as the Fed cut to zero and ran quantitative easing, and the Plaza Accord episode of 1985-87 saw the dollar fall roughly 40% on a major-currency basis - driven, on the standard practitioner reading, mainly by Fed easing from 12% to 6% rather than by the coordinated intervention it is remembered for.[^plaza] A floor held into an easing cycle pays carry to hold an asset that is also losing on price.

And even when the left-side spike is real, it can be **policy-capped**. The March-2020 dollar surge was explicitly reversed within weeks by Fed swap lines and the FIMA repo facility, which exist precisely to lean against dollar-shortage spikes.[^nyfed][^dallasfed2] The haven rally is genuine but bounded by a central bank that will fight its own scarcity.

## Floor versus trigger: the carry decides

The runs need to know whether to hold UUP always (a floor) or only when a signal fires (a trigger). The answer turns on carry, and here the dollar is structurally different from an options tail hedge. UUP is futures-based: the holder earns roughly the US-minus-foreign short-rate differential plus the Treasury-bill yield on collateral, minus the 0.75% fee, so its recent ~3.3% distribution is mostly bill income.[^uup] That means the dollar's "hedge premium" is the rate differential, which is frequently zero or positive - unlike a protective put, which bleeds 2-5% a year regardless and which the tail-hedging literature finds is, as a standing position, a money-loser.[^tailhedge] When US short rates exceed the foreign basket - true across 2018-19 and 2022-24, with the Fed at or above the ECB, BoJ and SNB - holding UUP is paid, not a drag, and the dual-regime hedge comes nearly free. The carry turns against the holder only when US rates fall below the basket, as under post-2009 zero-rate policy.

This maps cleanly onto the failure regimes, which is the useful part. Positive dollar carry coincides with the regimes where the dollar *works* (tightening, risk-off with the Fed not yet easing); negative carry coincides with the easing regime where the dollar tends to *fail*. So a fixed floor is well-supported when carry is positive and doubly penalized when it is negative - it pays to hold an asset that is also falling. The synthesis the evidence actually supports is therefore a **carry-conditioned floor**: a minimum UUP weight that scales with the sign and size of the US-foreign short-rate differential, on cheaply through 2018/2020/2022 and backed off in easing and de-dollarization regimes.

One piece of this is already handled by the data contract: UUP's Close is dividend-adjusted total return, so the carry the fund actually earns (its Treasury-bill collateral income net of fee) is banked into the price series, and a backtest that holds UUP captures the floor's true cost and benefit natively with no carry correction. What is *not* in the feed is the rate differential as a forward-looking *signal* on which to condition the weight - that is a macro series outside the OHLCV contract. The cleanly testable version is therefore the unconditional floor, with the carry-conditioned ideal noted as the better-supported design that needs one extra input, or approximated by a price-only proxy such as UUP's own trailing trend, which embeds the realized carry-plus-price drift but conflates the two and should be treated as a weak stand-in, not the real signal.

## Limitations

- The dollar-smile theory has only practitioner, conflicted origin sourcing; its empirical content is borrowed from independent academic and central-bank work, so the components are well-supported even though the named theory is not. Confidence on the dual-regime regularity: medium-high. Confidence on the smile as a *mechanism* versus a relabeled set of correlations: lower.
- The strongest counter is structural and forward-looking: if the convenience yield is the load-bearing pillar, secular de-dollarization or any US-origin confidence shock removes the exact thing that makes UUP a haven, and April 2025 is one realization, not a one-off.[^maurer][^ecb] A floor sized on 2018-2024 history is implicitly betting the convenience yield holds.
- Three crash episodes (2018, 2020, 2022) all sit in a positive-carry, dollar-friendly window, so our sample cannot distinguish "the dollar is a dual-regime haven" from "the dollar had favorable carry in every crash we happened to test." The carry-conditioned framing is partly an attempt to guard against exactly that confound; it remains untested here.
- The floor-versus-trigger verdict leans on a carry argument plus a tail-hedging analogy, not on a head-to-head backtest; treat it as a motivated prior for the runs, not a result.

The positive-carry confound (limitation 3) remains undistinguished.*
