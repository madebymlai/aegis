# Detecting Fast Crashes from Price

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> No daily signal *predicts* a fast V-shaped crash; the fastest honest signals live inside the daily bar - a Yang-Zhang range volatility and the raw overnight gap react a day or more before close-to-close vol and weeks before a 252-day trend - so the realistic edge is a *coincident* throttle that fires on day 0-1 of the shock, the VIX earns at most a place as a cleaner coincident confirmer rather than a lead, and the binding design constraint is the false-positive bill in calm years, with de-risking to cash better evidenced than rotating to a haven.

The aegis family is a working *sustained-bear* sleeve: a slow 252-day trend core plus a haven rotation that gains through the 2022 grind. Its hole is the fast V-shaped shock. On extended history the champion loses -1.65 Sharpe, -11.7%, a 13.4% max drawdown in 2018, a year that stacked two fast reversals (February's volatility-ETP unwind, the Q4 selloff) on a rate shock where every haven except the dollar fell.[^bespoke] The slow trend core entered 2018 long from 2017's melt-up and was whipsawed before it could turn; the haven sleeve had nothing trending to rotate into. The question this article settles is narrow and falsifiable: is there a *daily-computable* signal that fires early enough in such a shock to cut the loss, and if so, what does "early" actually buy against a 252-day trend that lags by weeks?

The answer is sobering but usable. The early-warning literature, read honestly, says nothing on a daily horizon *predicts* a fast crash. What the data does allow is *faster detection* - and the speed gradient is real: the overnight gap and the daily high-low range register a shock before the day's close does, and any of these beats a 252-day trend by weeks. The edge on offer is reaction latency, not prediction, and the cleanest place to harvest latency is the open-high-low-close-volume bar itself.

## The prediction ceiling: price-based early warning does not time crashes

The cleanest test of the whole early-warning paradigm is Guttal and co-authors, who run the standard instability indicators across 115 years of the Dow and multiple indices.[^guttal] Their result is two-edged and decisive. Rising *variance* is a genuine leading signal: it preceded every major crash in the sample by at least three months with no false negatives. But it threw seven false alarms, and - critically - it "does not forecast *when* a crash might occur." The companion critical-slowing-down indicator (rising lag-1 autocorrelation), which works for ecological and climate tipping points, shows no usable trend before financial crashes; the authors conclude meltdowns are not forecastable critical transitions but stochastic shocks. The lesson transfers directly: a price-only signal that *leads* (rising variance) cannot time the crash and fires often and wrong, while a signal precise enough to act on does not lead. This is the same trap the whipsaw article found for regime filters - lagging, overfit, and whipsaw-prone - now shown to be a property of the price series itself.[^whipsaw]

That sets the ceiling. The realistic goal is not to predict the crash but to *react inside it faster than a 252-day trend does*, and to pay an acceptable false-positive cost for that speed. The rest of this article is a speed ranking of the signals a daily bar can produce, from the fastest (inside-the-bar range and gap) to the slowest, and a verdict on whether the VIX, now conditionally allowed as a signal, earns its keep against them.

## The fastest signals are inside the bar: range and gap

A close-to-close return throws away everything the session did between the prints. The daily bar does not. Two constructions extract a faster read of stress from the open, high, low and close that a single bar already contains, and they should be the first toolkit tried because they are the fastest signals daily data gives.

The first is a **range-based volatility estimator**. Parkinson uses only the high-low log range and is about five times more efficient than squared close-to-close returns; Garman-Klass adds the open and close for roughly seven times; Rogers-Satchell makes the estimate independent of drift.[^parkinson][^garmanklass] The efficiency gain is the point: a regime shift crosses a detection threshold *earlier in calendar time* with a range estimator than with close-to-close, because each bar carries far more information, so a short lookback suffices where close-to-close needs a long noisy one. But there is a caveat that is fatal if ignored and is exactly a fast-crash signature: all three estimators are computed from the *intraday* range and assume continuous trading, so they systematically *understate* volatility when the move arrives as an overnight gap - the jump from yesterday's close to today's open lands outside the day's high-low range and is never measured.[^rangevol][^yangzhang] Fast crashes are delivered largely through overnight gaps (March 2020 opened down roughly 7% repeatedly, tripping circuit breakers), so the fastest range estimators are blind to the fastest channel unless corrected. The fix is **Yang-Zhang**, the one estimator that adds an explicit overnight (close-to-open) variance term and is therefore robust to opening jumps while keeping eight-to-fourteen-fold efficiency.[^yangzhang][^rangevol] Yang-Zhang is the primary fast-vol trigger the runs should try first.

The second is the **overnight gap** itself, read raw: the magnitude of the close-to-open move, `|Open_t / Close_{t-1} - 1|`. It is mechanically the *first* observation a daily bar offers of an overnight shock - you see the gap at the open, before the day's range or close has formed - which makes it at least coincident and plausibly the earliest daily read available. The return-decomposition literature establishes that overnight and intraday returns are distinct, often opposite-signed components, and that overnight information carries incremental downside-risk content, though it stops short of proving a gap *predicts* a crash rather than registering one.[^lps][^overnighttail] Two practical cautions for our setup. At the ETF and index level, idiosyncratic single-name earnings gaps wash out, so calm-year false positives are lower than the single-stock gap literature implies. But our Close is dividend-adjusted (total-return), which injects spurious gaps on ex-dividend days into a naive `Open / Close_{t-1}` comparison; the gap must be computed on a consistent adjustment basis or ex-dividend days screened, or the trigger will fire on dividends.

Both signals beat close-to-close realized vol on speed, and all of them beat the 252-day trend by weeks. Neither predicts; both react fast. That is the most the bar can give.

## Cross-asset detectors, and what to discard

A single instrument's bar misses the coordination that defines a risk-off day - the moment when everything moves together. The **financial turbulence index** of Kritzman and Li captures it: the squared Mahalanobis distance of the day's cross-sectional return vector from its historical mean and covariance, `FT_t = (r_t - mu)' * inv(Sigma) * (r_t - mu)`.[^kritzmanturb] Computed from the ten-ETF return cross-section, it scores a day high when moves are jointly unusual in size *or* correlation, even if no single asset's move is extreme. Its one documented timing property is persistence: turbulence stays elevated for about two weeks after the first spike, so the usable play is react on day 0 and stay defensive through the cluster.[^poturb] The honest framing is that turbulence is a *coincident* detector, not a predictor, and its persistence backtests flag whipsaw on the sharp recovery (a turbulence throttle sat in cash through the COVID rebound); the originating authors market turbulence products, a conflict worth noting.[^poturb2] Turbulence pairs naturally with Yang-Zhang: the range estimator times the equity leg's stress, turbulence times the cross-asset coordination, and a fast crash usually lights up both.

Two cross-asset constructions look promising and should be discarded. A **correlation spike** - rising average pairwise correlation across the ten ETFs - looks like a regime warning but is largely a statistical artifact: Forbes and Rigobon show measured correlation is biased upward exactly when volatility is high, and correcting the bias makes the apparent spikes of 1987, 1994 and 1997 vanish.[^forbesrigobon] A raw correlation gate fires *because* volatility already rose; it is a downstream symptom, not a lead. (Turbulence escapes this because it weights the full inverse covariance rather than reading a raw correlation level.) And a **volume spike** is not a fast-onset trigger at all. Karpoff's survey establishes that volume and volatility share a latent information driver and move together, and the Granger-causality evidence runs from volatility *to* volume, not the reverse, so volume cannot lead price; capitulation volume marks *bottoms*, identifiable in hindsight, not onsets.[^karpoff][^grangervol] Keep a volume z-score as a coincident confirmer if at all, never as the leading edge.

The much-cited **absorption ratio** - the share of variance in the top principal components, claimed to spike about a month before declines - fails our use for the same reason it failed the original price-only sweep: its lead claim rests on a single conflicted source, an independent replication found the ratio *decreasing* before crashes, and it runs on a 52-week weekly covariance window built for slow regime decay, not a one-week February-2018 unwind.[^kritzmanar][^poar] It is not a fast-crash trigger.

## Flight-to-safety days: a coincident risk-off flag, regime-hardened

The flight-to-safety literature supplies a different and more interpretable coincident signal: a daily flag for equities falling while havens rally. Baele, Bekaert, Inghelbrecht and Wei identify flight-to-safety days across 23 countries using only daily bond and stock returns, through a threshold rule that is cleanly computable from our tickers: a day qualifies when the bond return exceeds a positive volatility-scaled threshold *and* the stock return falls below the symmetric negative one, `r_bond > k*sigma_bond AND r_stock < -k*sigma_stock`.[^baele] Calibrated at `k = 1.5` these days are about 0.8% of the sample yet carry an average bond-minus-equity differential of 4.26%; the published headline rounds to under 2% of days and a 2.7% differential.[^baele] In our universe the flag is `SPY < -k*sigma_SPY AND TLT > +k*sigma_TLT`, with `k` near 1.5 for rare-event flagging or nearer 1.0 for a more frequent sizing signal, and the volatilities can come from the same range estimators above rather than close-to-close.

The decisive property is that this flag is **coincident, not leading**. The paper's own evidence is unambiguous: the bond-minus-equity impact is already -3.16% on the *first* day of a flight-to-safety spell, and the day before an isolated one the impact is essentially zero - there is no measurable pre-move, because the flag is a same-day co-exceedance by construction.[^baele] Spells are short, 89% lasting three days or fewer. So it cannot front-run a crash; it confirms one as it starts, which is still useful for scaling exposure down once a cluster begins.

The flag inherits the weakness of its haven leg. A Treasury-up requirement breaks precisely in inflation shocks: in 2022, the first year since the late 1970s that stocks and bonds both fell, TLT declined *with* SPY for months, so a TLT-up flag would have stayed silent through the decade's worst equity drawdown.[^cheema][^statestreet] The fix the cross-asset evidence supports is to make the haven leg an *or* across the three havens whose failure regimes differ: `(TLT up) OR (UUP up) OR (GLD up)`. The dollar is the most regime-robust leg - it held in 2022 and spiked in the March-2020 dash for cash - gold is second, and Treasuries are the most fragile.[^cheema][^baurlucey] This is the complementarity the divergent-vs-defensive article found at the edge level, pushed into the trigger definition, and it connects to the dollar's dual-regime role.[^divergent][^dollar]

## VIX: a conditionally-allowed confirmer, not a lead

The VIX is now admissible as a signal-only input (it is not tradable here), but only if it earns the extra data dependency by leading the best in-bar trigger. The evidence says it does not clear that bar, and the reason is mechanical. The VIX *is* the implied volatility of S&P 500 options, so it spikes *as* the index falls; the contemporaneous VIX-change-to-return relation is strongly negative and asymmetric, which is precisely why the VIX is a same-day mirror of the drop, not a precursor to it.[^whaley] Giot tested the leading-indicator question directly and found the relation dominated by that contemporaneous link, with the only forward signal a *contrarian* one - extreme-high VIX predicts positive forward returns (oversold mean reversion), the opposite of a crash-onset warning.[^giot] For a fast V-shaped shock the VIX spike and the price drop are essentially simultaneous, so the VIX offers no head start over a same-day Yang-Zhang range spike that needs no extra feed.

The VIX does carry genuine forward content, but at the wrong target or horizon for this problem. The one clean *lagged* predictive result is Connolly, Stivers and Sun: a high lagged VIX raises the probability of a negative stock-bond correlation over the next month (a VIX above 25 implies a 36.5% chance against a 15.6% unconditional baseline and 6.1% when the VIX is below 20).[^connolly] That predicts the flight-to-quality *correlation regime*, useful context for whether the haven leg will work, not the equity drawdown's timing. The variance risk premium (the VIX squared minus realized variance) predicts aggregate returns, but at a *quarterly* horizon, it leans on intraday realized variance a daily sleeve approximates poorly, and its coefficients are unstable out of sample.[^bollerslev][^pyun] None of these is a faster crash-onset trigger than the bar.

What the VIX plausibly adds is *signal quality*, not lead time: as an S&P-wide measure of option demand it captures fear that an equal-weighted read of ten ETF ranges might understate, so it can clean up a coincident throttle rather than fire ahead of one. The parity-safe construction, since the VIX3M term-structure feed is unverified on the live broker, is the VIX against its own moving average with an absolute floor: `VIX_t > k * MA_50(VIX) AND VIX_t > floor` (floor around 20-25), optionally requiring two consecutive closes to cut whipsaw. The only supporting evidence for the crossover's lead and false-positive rate is practitioner-grade and unverified, so it must be benchmarked head-to-head against the Yang-Zhang trigger, not trusted on the vendor's numbers.[^vixma] Term-structure inversion (the VIX above the VIX3M) is coincident-to-capitulation, and the only variant claiming a one-to-two-week lead relies on VIX *futures*, which are out of scope. The honest pre-registration is therefore that a VIX hypothesis is *expected to fail* the lead-time bar and is worth running only to confirm it adds confirmation quality, not timing.

## Latency versus false positives, and what to do when it fires

Two design facts decide whether any of this helps. The first is that the relevant comparison is not trigger-versus-foresight but trigger-versus-the-252-day-trend-we-already-run. A slow trend does not flip until a move is weeks old; a Yang-Zhang range spike, an overnight gap, a turbulence spike or a flight-to-safety flag fires on day 0-1. Against that baseline a coincident detector *is* an improvement in fast shocks, which is the whole point. The second fact is that the improvement is bought with false positives in calm years, and the whipsaw article's lesson is that a trigger-happy or lagging filter forfeits the convex payoff it was meant to protect.[^whipsaw] The turbulence throttle's documented failure - sitting in cash through the violent post-COVID recovery - is exactly this.[^poturb] Every trigger must therefore be judged on its calm-year drag and its re-entry whipsaw, not only on the crash it catches; the right metric is the held-out tradeoff between 2018-style protection and 2019/2021-style false-positive cost. The in-bar signals have an edge here too: because they are more efficient, they can run a shorter lookback and a higher threshold for the same detection speed, which is the lever that trades latency against false positives.

On the *action* once a trigger fires, the evidence is thinner than the detection evidence and points, modestly, to cash. The published turbulence and absorption-ratio applications scale equity exposure toward cash by signal percentile; none of the in-scope sources establishes that rotating into a specific haven beats de-risking, and the haven legs are themselves regime-unstable.[^poturb][^kritzmanar][^cheema] The dollar is the one haven with a dual-regime case for being the rotation target, but that case is made in its own right, not by the trigger literature.[^dollar] The conservative reading is that the better-supported response to a fast-crash flag is to cut gross exposure to cash, and any haven tilt is a separate, weaker bet to test on its own.

## Limitations

- The honest ceiling is low. The strongest neutral source says price-based early warning cannot time crashes and throws frequent false alarms;[^guttal] everything here is a *coincident* detector, and its value over the existing trend core is reaction latency, not prediction. If even a fast in-bar throttle whipsaws more than it protects on held-out calm years, the fast-crash hole may be uncoverable on daily data, which is itself a finding worth establishing.
- The Feb-2018 shock specifically may be structurally invisible. Its mechanism - a short-volatility-ETP unwind and dealer hedging - lives in positioning and options data a daily sleeve cannot see, so a daily trigger can react to the resulting move but never anticipate its cause.
- The range-estimator speed claims are strongly sourced (multiple peer-reviewed estimators plus the gap-understatement caveat), but the *gap-leads-crash* and *VIX-MA-leads* claims are weaker: the former rests on a decomposition result plus mechanism, the latter only on unverified practitioner backtests. Treat both as priors to test against Yang-Zhang, not results.
- The *action* evidence (cash versus haven rotation) is weak and largely practitioner-grade; treat cash-beats-rotation as a prior to test.
- Conditionally in scope, with the bar stated: `^VIX` as a signal-only input that must demonstrate lead over the best in-bar trigger (the evidence predicts it will not); `^VIX3M` and term-structure ideas only as a variant alongside the parity-safe VIX-vs-own-MA fallback.
- Out of scope, and not seeded: options chains, VIX *futures* (the only term-structure result with a genuine lead needs them), intraday and high-frequency data, order flow and ETP positioning, credit spreads, and any macro or fundamental series. Several of the strongest early-warning and variance-risk-premium results depend on these and are untestable here.

## Strategy hypotheses this could seed

- [x] **Killed both halves** ([[runs/aegis/2026-06-10#20260610T205352247752Z_etf_aegis_v11_yzthrottle|v11]], [[runs/aegis/2026-06-10#20260610T205502094969Z_etf_aegis_v12_ccthrottle|v12]]): A Yang-Zhang range-volatility throttle (per-bar OHLC estimate on the equity leg, z-scored against a short lookback, scaling gross down past a threshold) cuts the held-out 2018 fast-crash loss and crosses its threshold earlier than a close-to-close realized-vol throttle at equal calm-year false positives - the test of whether the in-bar speed gain is real on our data. *Outcome: best 2018 improvement +0.01 Sharpe (vol-target design, fv_target x window grid on the pinned champion); the trigger is structurally post-shock (Feb-2018 cut lands 4-5 days after the bottom), and close-to-close beat Yang-Zhang at matched windows - the prediction-ceiling section called it.*
- [ ] An overnight-gap trigger (`|Open_t / Close_{t-1} - 1|` on an adjustment-consistent basis, ex-dividend days screened) fires on day 0 of the 2018 and 2020-Q1 gap-downs at least as early as the range throttle, and is the earliest daily read of a fast shock - confirm it leads close-to-close and watch the dividend-artifact false positives.
- [ ] A multivariate turbulence throttle (Mahalanobis distance of the ten-ETF return vector) adds protection beyond the single-leg range throttle by catching cross-asset coordination - test whether the two together beat either alone, and whether turbulence's re-entry whipsaw costs more than it saves.
- [ ] A daily flight-to-safety flag (`SPY < -k*sigma_SPY AND TLT > +k*sigma_TLT`, k near 1.5) is coincident, not leading: it fires on day 0-1 of the 2018 and 2020-Q1 drops but gives essentially zero lead - confirm it sizes but does not time; and replacing the TLT leg with an *or* across `TLT / UUP / GLD` restores firing in 2022.
- [ ] A `^VIX` signal (VIX above its 50-day MA with an absolute floor) does *not* lead the best in-bar trigger on the three reference episodes - the pre-registered expectation; worth running only to test whether it improves confirmation quality (fewer false positives at equal protection), not timing.
- [ ] A volume z-score does not lead and adds nothing as a standalone fast trigger - the pre-registered negative control, since the price-volume evidence says volatility drives volume, not the reverse.
- [ ] Routing a fired throttle to cash beats rotating the throttled budget into UUP or short-duration havens on held-out fast-crash splits - the action control the trigger literature implies but does not prove.

[^whaley]: Whaley, R., "Understanding the VIX", Journal of Portfolio Management 35(3):98-105, 2009. The VIX is the model-free 30-day implied vol of S&P 500 options; the contemporaneous VIX-change-to-return relation is strongly negative and asymmetric, the mechanical reason the VIX is near-contemporaneous with equity declines. Whaley authored the VIX methodology (mild COI on the construction facts). https://jpm.iijournals.com/content/35/3/98
[^giot]: Giot, P., "Relationships Between Implied Volatility Indexes and Stock Index Returns", Journal of Portfolio Management 31(3):92-100, 2005. Directly tests whether implied-vol indexes are leading indicators: the relation is dominated by a contemporaneous negative-asymmetric link, and the only forward signal is contrarian (extreme-high VIX predicts positive forward returns) - the opposite of a crash-onset warning. Peer-reviewed, no COI - the strongest counter to VIX-as-leading-indicator. https://jpm.pm-research.com/content/31/3/92
[^connolly]: Connolly, R., Stivers, C. & Sun, L., "Stock Market Uncertainty and the Stock-Bond Return Relation", Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 40(1):161-194, 2005. Lagged VIX predicts the probability of a negative stock-bond correlation next month: VIX>25 -> 36.5%, VIX<20 -> 6.1%, unconditional 15.6%. The one clean lagged-VIX predictive result, but the target is the flight-to-quality correlation regime, not the equity drawdown's timing. Peer-reviewed, no COI. https://cdr.lib.unc.edu/downloads/gf06gc22x
[^bollerslev]: Bollerslev, T., Tauchen, G. & Zhou, H., "Expected Stock Returns and Variance Risk Premia", Review of Financial Studies 22(11):4463-4492, 2009. The variance risk premium (VIX^2 minus realized variance) predicts aggregate returns, peaking at the quarterly horizon - but the headline result leans on intraday realized variance and the horizon is wrong for fast-crash onset. Peer-reviewed. https://public.econ.duke.edu/~boller/Published_Papers/rfs_09.pdf
[^pyun]: Pyun, S., "Variance risk in aggregate stock returns and time-varying return predictability", Journal of Financial Economics 132(1):150-174, 2019. The variance-risk-premium predictive coefficients are time-varying and unstable, "too unstable to use for out-of-sample forecasts" - the strongest peer-reviewed counter to relying on the VRP out of sample. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X18302782
[^vixma]: Practitioner regime-detection backtests (e.g. Volatility Box, "Volatility Regime Detection"). Claim a VIX-vs-moving-average crossover preceded major regime shifts 2005-2025 (including Feb-2018, Q4-2018, Mar-2020) with a short lead and ~18% false-positive rate. Vendor-grade, unverified, no peer-reviewed corroboration of the lead/false-positive figures - benchmark against the in-bar trigger rather than trust the numbers. https://volatilitybox.com/research/volatility-regime-detection/
