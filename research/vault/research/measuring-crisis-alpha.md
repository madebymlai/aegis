# Measuring Crisis Alpha

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> Full-sample Sharpe rewards calm-market smoothness; to rank a sleeve on stress responsiveness use a drawdown-aware objective (Ulcer Performance Index or moderate-alpha CDaR), and reward actual crash gains only through capture or multi-window measures - never by optimising on a single worst episode.

A ranking metric is a statement about what you want. Full-sample Sharpe says "high average return per unit of average volatility," which is satisfied best by a strategy that is smooth in the years that dominate the sample - the calm ones. A sleeve whose entire purpose is to gain during rare equity crashes is therefore systematically *mis*-ranked by Sharpe: its crash-time payoff is a few percent of the observations, swamped by the bleed it pays the rest of the time. Selecting such a sleeve needs an objective that weights the stress windows more heavily. The catch is that the metrics which most directly reward crash performance are also the most fragile, because crashes are rare and the relevant statistics are dominated by single paths. This article lays out the menu and argues for the robust middle.

## What the candidate metrics actually reward

The drawdown-aware family measures the shape of the equity curve's underwater periods rather than its return volatility. The **Ulcer Index** is the root-mean-square of percentage drawdowns from the running peak, so it penalises both the depth and the *duration* of being underwater, and squaring means deep dips are punished harder than shallow ones; the **Ulcer Performance Index** (Martin ratio) divides excess return by it.[^ulcer] **Conditional Drawdown at Risk** generalises this: it is the mean of the worst (1-alpha) fraction of drawdowns, tunable from average drawdown (alpha near 0) to maximum drawdown (alpha near 1), and it is convex and tractable for optimisation.[^cdar] The classic ratios - **Calmar/MAR** (return over maximum drawdown), **Sterling**, **Burke** - sit at the fragile end because they lean on the single worst decline.[^bacon]

The downside-risk family conditions on a return threshold. The **Sortino ratio** divides excess return by downside deviation, the volatility of returns below a minimum acceptable return, so it rewards strategies whose downside is small - but its ranking shifts with the arbitrary threshold choice.[^sortino] **Conditional Value at Risk** (expected shortfall) is the mean loss in the worst (1-alpha) tail, a coherent risk measure that is linear-programming tractable.[^cvar] Neither directly rewards *gaining* in a crash; they reward losing little.

The conditional family is the only one that rewards crash gains directly. **Up- and down-capture ratios** measure a sleeve's compounded return in the benchmark's up and down months as a fraction of the benchmark's - a defensive sleeve wants down-capture below 100, and a true crisis-alpha sleeve wants it *negative* (it gains when the benchmark falls).[^capture] The **stress-window conditional return**, the "crisis alpha" of Greyserman and Kaminski, is simply performance measured inside defined equity-crash windows.[^kaminski] This is the thing we actually want - but it is also the most exposed to the central pitfall.

## The pitfall: rare crashes make the best-aimed metrics the least stable

The metrics that most directly target crash performance are single-path order statistics over a handful of events, and they do not generalise. Magdon-Ismail and Atiya derive the expected maximum drawdown for Brownian motion with drift and show it grows with the length of the track record and carries large sampling error - so a backtested maximum drawdown (and any Calmar or MAR built on it) is a biased, noisy number, and optimising a ranking objective directly on it overfits to the one worst historical episode.[^magdon] The tail measures fail the same way from the other direction: Varga-Haszonits and Kondor prove that the estimation error of VaR, expected shortfall, and semivariance *diverges* as the sample size approaches the number of parameters, so CVaR-based optimisation is unstable precisely when crash data is scarce.[^vargakondor] A stress-window conditional return computed over three or four crashes is the limiting case - it is exactly as overfit as the number of windows is small.

This is the crux for designing a ranking objective. The closer a metric gets to "did you make money in *this* crash," the more it rewards a candidate for fitting that crash's idiosyncrasies. With only a few crash episodes in any realistic sample, optimising on the sharpest crisis metric selects for luck on those specific episodes, not for a repeatable crash-time edge.

## The robust middle

The resolution is to rank on a metric that integrates the whole equity curve while still rewarding shallow, short drawdowns, and to bring in the direct crash-gain signal only in regularised form. Two properties separate the robust objectives from the fragile ones: they use *many* observations rather than one path, and they have no arbitrary threshold. By that test the **Ulcer Performance Index** and **CDaR at moderate alpha** (roughly 0.05 to 0.2) are the best smooth, stress-aware ranking objectives - both reward crisis shallowness without collapsing onto the single-path maximum drawdown, and both are well-behaved for optimisation.[^ulcer][^cdar] To additionally reward *gaining* in stress rather than merely losing little, blend in a **down-capture** term or a **multi-window** conditional return averaged or bootstrapped across many stress windows - never a single one.[^capture][^kaminski] What to avoid as a direct optimisation target is the fragile end: raw Calmar/MAR and any single-window crisis return, which the sampling-error results show will not generalise.[^magdon][^vargakondor]

## Rewarding crash gains, not just shallow drawdowns

The drawdown-aware objectives have a known blind spot, and our own runs hit it: UPI and CDaR both selected the same heavy-core slow-trend candidate with shallower drawdowns, but they got there by rewarding *smoothness*, trading away the 2022 crash-time gain that is the whole point of a signal sleeve (the UPI pick took 2022 at +0.47 against the champion's +0.82; see [[runs/aegis/2026-06-10|run diary]]). Drawdown-aware ranking rewards losing little; the allocator's true objective is a sleeve that *gains*. Two terms target the gain directly, each reducible to one number per equity curve.

The first is **down-capture**. Over the set of benchmark-down periods `D = {t : b_t < 0}`, the Morningstar form is the ratio of compounded sleeve return to compounded benchmark return inside that set, `DownCapture = 100 * (prod_{t in D}(1+r_t) - 1) / (prod_{t in D}(1+b_t) - 1)`.[^downcap] Below 100 is defensive; *below zero* means the sleeve gained while the benchmark fell - true crisis alpha - so the ranking objective is to minimise it. The sharper variant is the **capture spread**, up-capture minus down-capture, which is a single number that rewards a sleeve for keeping upside while protecting on the downside and so penalises the degenerate "smooth and dead" solution that pure down-capture would accept.[^spread] Down-capture's known weaknesses are real and must be controlled: it is benchmark-dependent (fix one benchmark, SPY), it confounds with beta (the spread form mitigates this), and it is unstable when the benchmark return is near zero (exclude a small band `|b_t| < epsilon`).[^capturecrit]

The second is the **multi-window conditional return** of Greyserman and Kaminski: define crisis windows from price as the maximal intervals where SPY is in a drawdown deeper than some threshold X (peak-to-trough-to-recovery episodes), compute the compounded sleeve return inside each window `g_k = prod_{t in W_k}(1+r_t) - 1`, and aggregate across windows.[^kaminski][^kaminski2] The aggregation is where the few-window pitfall bites: with only three episodes (2018, 2020, 2022) the *mean* is dominated by one lucky window, so the robust reduction is the **median** of the `g_k`, which is the formula-level version of "averaged across many windows, never a single one."

Neither term is safe raw, because both are statistics over a handful of episodes. Three regularisations have the strongest support. *Bootstrap* the in-window returns with a stationary block bootstrap (random block lengths preserve autocorrelation, with automatic block-length selection) and rank on a lower confidence bound rather than the point estimate, so a term resting on one lucky path is shrunk toward its own uncertainty.[^politis][^heavytail] *Penalise dispersion* across windows - rank on `median_k(g_k) - lambda * IQR_k(g_k)` - to reward consistency rather than one standout episode, the same deflation logic that discounts a result obtained from many trials.[^bailey] And *shrink toward the full-sample objective*: rather than replace UPI/CDaR, blend, `Final = (1-w) * UPI_or_CDaR + w * CrisisGain`, with a small-to-moderate w, because with three windows the crisis term should *tilt* a robust full-sample objective, not stand alone.[^tailwarn][^magdon] This is the most defensible move the small sample permits - the bootstrap widens the error bars but cannot manufacture information that is not there.

The two pre-registered negative controls follow from the same statistics, and their predicted behaviour is now sharp. **Calmar/MAR** (return over maximum drawdown) should select visibly more overfit candidates because its denominator is a single-path order statistic with sampling dispersion scaling like the square root of the track length; a single catastrophic drawdown suppresses the ratio for years and the metric is not even comparable across unequal windows.[^magdon][^calmar] A **single-window 2022-only objective** should be maximally overfit, high in-sample and near-zero out-of-sample, because optimising on one episode is the degenerate, undeflated case of backtest overfitting - the probability of selecting a fluke grows with the number of trials and is unbounded when the objective rests on a single path.[^bailey][^deflated] Both controls are expected to select candidates that fail to repeat their crash gain out of window, against which the bootstrapped median-multi-window term is the robust comparison.

## Limitations

- The Ulcer Index primary source (Martin and McCann, 1989) could not be retrieved directly; the formula is verified across independent secondary sources and is not in dispute, but the grade on the primary is capped.
- The sample-dependence pitfall is strongly established for maximum drawdown and for CVaR by two independent peer-reviewed sources; the claim that it extends to a few-window crisis return is our own extrapolation from the same statistics, not a separately published result.
- The crash-gain terms collapse a conditional, path-dependent payoff into one number, and the strongest practitioner treatment of crisis-hedge measurement argues against exactly that: it holds that compounded-return metrics misrepresent conditional payoffs and that a hedge should be judged on the reliability of its stress payoff and the liquidity value of its proceeds, viewed jointly, not reduced to a scalar.[^longtail] Our one-number terms are a tractable ranking *proxy*; they undercount the liquidity-and-reliability value that source insists on. The source is a tail-fund product paper (high COI), but the methodological caution is independent of the sales pitch.
- These are *ranking* arguments. Which metric, in our data and splits, actually selects more stress-responsive candidates than Sharpe is an empirical question this article only frames.

## Strategy hypotheses this could seed

- [x] Ranking candidates on the Ulcer Performance Index instead of full-sample Sharpe selects sleeves with shallower crash-window drawdowns at acceptable full-sample cost - the first custom ranking Metric to register. **Supported 2026-06-10**: on the identical crisis-blend grid, UPI ranking selected a different candidate (heavier trend core, slower vol window) with shallower held-out drawdowns (5.54% vs 5.74% mean max_dd) at zero Sharpe cost (+1.007 vs +1.008) - and, as predicted, traded crash-time gain (2022 +0.47 vs +0.82) for smoothness. Registered as the opt-in `ulcer_performance_index` Metric. See [[runs/aegis/2026-06-10|run diary]].
- [x] A CDaR-at-moderate-alpha ranking objective selects similar candidates to UPI (robustness cross-check), while Calmar/MAR selects visibly more overfit ones - confirm the fragile-vs-robust split on our data. **Half supported, half refuted 2026-06-10**: CDaR converged with UPI (v8), but Calmar did *not* select a fragile candidate (v20) - see negative control A below.
- [x] A down-capture term against SPY (compounded sleeve return over benchmark-down periods, near-zero-benchmark days excluded), minimised as the objective, shifts selection toward sleeves with negative or low down-capture - those that *gain* when SPY falls rather than merely lose little - recovering the 2022 upside UPI traded away. The metric closest to the allocator's true objective. **Killed as a standalone selector 2026-06-10** (v19, registered as the opt-in `neg_down_capture` Metric): it selects a deadened book (breadth-throttled to a 0.27 mean Sharpe), exactly the degenerate solution the next hypothesis predicts. See [[runs/aegis/2026-06-10|run diary]].
- [x] The **capture spread** (up-capture minus down-capture) selects a less degenerate sleeve than down-capture alone, which can be minimised by a smooth, dead book - confirm the spread penalises "smooth and dead" and keeps upside. **Supported 2026-06-10** (v18 vs v19, opt-in `capture_spread` Metric): the spread pick is alive (mean 0.47) and recovers the 2022 upside (+0.60 vs the sharpe pick's +0.17) - but it pays in 2018 and chop, so as a *standalone* objective it still loses on the crash-year minimum; the blend below remains the live design.
- [ ] A **multi-window** conditional-return objective (median compounded sleeve return across SPY drawdown-deeper-than-X windows), bootstrapped to a lower confidence bound and blended with UPI/CDaR, beats either the raw crisis return or the drawdown-aware metric alone at selecting sleeves that gain across *all* in-sample crashes, not one. *Still open - the only live metric hypothesis after 2026-06-10: standalone crash-gain terms killed (v18/v19), so the tilt-a-robust-objective design is what remains to test.*
- [x] **Negative control A**: ranking on Calmar/MAR selects a visibly more overfit candidate (deeper held-out drawdowns / lower held-out Sharpe) than the robust drawdown-aware metrics - the fragile-end half of the fragile-vs-robust split, still untested after v8. **Refuted on our grid 2026-06-10** (v20, opt-in `calmar_ratio` Metric): the Calmar pick lands in the same UPI/CDaR family with identical held-out drawdown (5.54%) and Sharpe inside the 0.1 band. Diagnosis: the fragility statistics assume a search space wild enough to contain flukes; the crisis-blend grid is regularized enough that every objective lands in the same robust basin.
- [x] **Negative control B**: optimising directly on a single-window (2022-only) crisis return overfits and degrades on held-out crashes - the pre-registered single-episode control. **Refuted on our grid 2026-06-10** (v21, diluted to a two-window bear-era selection by a pipeline >= 2-splits constraint): the bear-era pick generalises to composite 0.154 vs the champion-params 0.151 on the extended window. Same diagnosis as control A - the grid's own regularization is the binding protection.
