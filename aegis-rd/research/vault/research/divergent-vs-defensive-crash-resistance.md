# Divergent vs Defensive Crash-Resistance

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> "Crash-resistant" hides two different payoffs - edges that *gain* in a crash (flight-to-safety, trend-following crisis alpha) and edges that merely *lose less* (defensive low-beta equity) - and only the first kind can serve as a sleeve whose rising return is read as a bearish signal.

A sleeve meant to *tell* an allocator that a crash is coming has to do more than survive one. It has to make money while the rest of the book bleeds, because the allocator infers the regime from the sleeve's own rising returns. That requirement splits the crash-resistance literature cleanly in two. Some edges are **divergent**: they produce positive returns precisely when equities fall. Others are merely **defensive**: they fall less than the market but still fall. Both reduce drawdown; only the divergent ones produce the upward swing in relative performance that a regime detector can read. This article grades the three best-documented edges on that axis - flight-to-safety, trend-following crisis alpha, and the low-risk equity anomaly - and names the failure mode of each, because a sleeve that is supposed to signal danger is worse than useless if it goes quiet in the exact crash it was built for.

## Flight-to-safety: the cleanest crash-gainer, and its regime trap

When equities sell off on a growth or risk shock, capital does not sit in cash - it flees to a short list of safe havens: long-dated Treasuries, gold, and the US dollar (plus the yen and Swiss franc in FX). The defining feature is a *correlation regime shift*. Assets that are weakly or even positively correlated to equities in calm markets turn negatively correlated in stress, so the hedge appears exactly when it is needed. Baur and Lucey gave the field its vocabulary here: a *hedge* is uncorrelated or negatively correlated to equities on average, while a *safe haven* is uncorrelated or negatively correlated specifically in a market crash - the extreme tail.[^baurlucey] Gold qualifies as a safe haven for US and major European equities, though the protective effect is short-lived (on the order of fifteen trading days) and does not hold for every market.[^baurlucey][^baurmcdermott]

The mechanism is well-theorised. Vayanos models how rising volatility raises fund managers' effective risk aversion and liquidity preference as they approach redemption thresholds; they dump risky and illiquid assets, mechanically pushing risk-asset correlations toward one while bidding up the highest-quality, most-liquid instruments.[^vayanos] Connolly, Stivers and Sun supply the empirical conditioning variable: the stock-bond return relation is negatively related to implied volatility, so the flight-to-quality hedge is concentrated in high-VIX states rather than present on average.[^connolly] Baele and co-authors, identifying flight-to-safety days across 23 countries, show how rare and episodic this is - such days are under 2% of the sample yet carry an average bond-minus-equity return differential of about 2.7%, with equity-fund outflows feeding government-bond and money-market inflows.[^baele]

The trap is that the Treasury hedge is *regime-conditional, not structural*. The negative stock-bond correlation that investors leaned on for two decades is a post-2000, low-and-stable-inflation phenomenon. It was positive across the high-inflation 1970s and 1980s, and it turned positive again in 2022, when an inflation and rate shock - not a growth shock - drove stocks and Treasuries down together. The 60/40 portfolio had its worst year since the 1930s, and the bond hedge failed at the moment it was supposed to work.[^aqrcorr][^callan] AQR's framework attributes the sign of the correlation to the relative volatility of growth versus inflation and their comovement, explaining roughly 70% of the long-run US stock-bond correlation; the lesson is that *the shock type, not the presence of a crash, decides whether bonds hedge*.[^aqrcorr] Gold carries a related but milder unreliability: in the March-2020 dash-for-cash it sold off with everything else before recovering, because in a liquidity scramble even havens are sold to raise dollars.[^nyfed]

## Trend-following crisis alpha: convex but episodic

Trend-following earns its reputation as "crisis alpha" because most equity bear markets grind down over months rather than crashing in a day. The average of the worst 60/40 drawdowns since 1903 ran roughly eighteen months,[^aqrcentury] which gives a time-series-momentum system room to flip short after the initial decline and ride the continuation. The result is a convex, long-volatility-like payoff: trend tends to do best in the most extreme down *and* up years, the so-called smile.

The economic counterparty is partly structural. Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen, using CFTC positioning data, show that speculators (the trend-followers) sit with the trend while hedgers take the other side; hedgers and other non-profit-seeking participants trade for reasons unrelated to expected return and predictably pay away the trend premium.[^mop] On top of that sits a behavioural layer - initial under-reaction to news followed by delayed over-reaction - which is the part most exposed to crowding and decay.[^mop] AQR's century study reports the headline crisis figure most often cited: a simulated trend strategy across 59 markets, 1903-2012, at a 10% volatility target and net of 2-and-20 fees, was positive in nine of the ten worst 60/40 drawdowns, including the dot-com unwind and the global financial crisis.[^aqrcentury] The live corroboration is 2022, when the SG Trend Index returned +27.3%, its best year since the index began in 2000, driven by short bonds and rates and long dollar and energy.[^hedgeweek]

Two failure modes matter. First, *crisis alpha is episodic because it needs a sustained trend*. It does nothing in fast, V-shaped shocks: Q4 2018 saw the S&P fall about 14% into its worst December since 1931 while trend gave no protection, defeated by the sharp reversal,[^graham] and the March-2020 COVID crash was too fast for trend to establish shorts before the rebound. The same property produced the 2011-2019 "lost decade" of flat-to-poor returns, often attributed to crowding.[^quantpedia] Second, the *statistical evidence for the underlying signal is contested*. Huang, Li, Wang and Zhou re-run the foundational tests asset-by-asset and find time-series momentum largely insignificant - 47 of 55 assets have predictive t-statistics below 1.65, and the headline pooled t-statistic of 4.34 falls below properly bootstrapped critical values - concluding the profitable strategy behaves much like a non-predictive "historical-mean" rule.[^huang] That this rebuttal is peer-reviewed and free of any fund-marketing conflict makes it the strongest single caution against treating crisis alpha as a law rather than a tendency.

## Defensive equity: a cushion, not a signal

The low-risk anomaly is the most robust of the three edges and the least useful for our purpose, and the tension between those two facts is the point. Low-beta, low-volatility, and defensive-sector equities have delivered equal-or-higher returns at lower risk across decades and markets, and they lose materially less in drawdowns - in 2022, SPLV fell about 4.9% and USMV about 9% against the S&P 500's roughly 19% decline.[^morningstar] But "loses less" is a *convergent* payoff: the sleeve still loses money in the crash. Its outperformance is relative, visible only when benchmarked against a falling market, which is a weaker and noisier signal than an absolute gain.

The mechanism is strong. Frazzini and Pedersen show that leverage- and benchmark-constrained investors who want above-market returns buy high-beta stocks instead of levering low-beta ones, bidding up high beta until it earns low alpha and leaving low beta cheap; their betting-against-beta factor earns a US Sharpe of about 0.78 since 1926 and the premium compresses when funding constraints tighten.[^bab] Baker, Bradley and Wurgler add the limits-to-arbitrage piece - managers judged on tracking error against a fixed benchmark will not arbitrage the anomaly because buying low-beta stocks adds tracking error - and document the raw effect: $1 in the lowest-volatility quintile in 1968 grew to $59.55 by 2008 while $1 in the highest-vol quintile fell to $0.58.[^bbw] Lottery-preference demand for volatile stocks, and the idiosyncratic-volatility puzzle, round out the behavioural side.[^bbw][^ang]

For a crash-signal sleeve the disqualifying problems are three. The premium *decays with crowding*: a decade of inflows erased the valuation discount that powered it, low-vol underperformed through the late 2010s, and by 2025 SPLV's P/E had converged on the market's.[^evidence] The cushion is *conditional, not guaranteed*: defensives and utilities trade as bond proxies and were hit by the 2022 rate leg and the 2013 taper tantrum, and in the indiscriminate March-2020 selloff "beta compression" meant even low-beta names fell hard.[^morningstar] And the flagship evidence is itself contested - Novy-Marx and Velikov argue the betting-against-beta Sharpe is an artifact of a non-standard construction that pours about $1.05 per dollar into the smallest 1% of stocks, so net of costs the anomaly is largely explained by profitability and investment tilts.[^nmv]

## What this means for a signal-bearing sleeve

The two divergent edges fail in opposite regimes, which is the most useful finding here. Flight-to-safety catches *fast growth shocks* - the COVID crash, 2018's risk-off - where capital stampedes into Treasuries and the dollar before any trend can form, but it breaks in inflation-driven selloffs like 2022. Trend-following catches *slow grinds and inflation-shock crashes* - it had its best year in 2022 by being short bonds - but it misses the fast V-shaped shocks where flight-to-safety shines. Their failure modes are close to complementary: each tends to work where the other goes quiet. A divergent sleeve built to span both would therefore be a more reliable bearish tell than either alone, and far more reliable than a defensive-equity sleeve whose signal is a merely-smaller loss. The defensive edge belongs in a *survival* mandate, not a *signal* mandate.

Spanning both regimes raises a practical problem: which regime you are in is exactly what a price-only system cannot observe. The growth-versus-inflation shock that decides whether bonds hedge is a macro state, not a price, so a sleeve fed only daily OHLCV has no clean regime variable to switch on. Time-series momentum is the regime-agnostic way out - rather than be told the shock type, it mechanically rotates into whatever is trending, going long the havens catching the flight-to-safety bid and short bonds while long energy when an inflation shock drives the selloff, so it spans both failure regimes without ever naming them. That is the case for building the combined divergent sleeve as momentum across a cross-asset book rather than as hand-coded regime rules.

## Limitations

- Confidence is uneven across the three edges. That bonds, gold, and the dollar rally in growth-shock crises, and that the hedge is regime-conditional on inflation, is well-established across independent academic and practitioner sources - the highest-confidence layer here.
- That trend-following is convex and crisis-friendly *on average* is well-supported, but the reliability of the underlying signal is genuinely contested,[^huang] and the single most-cited crisis figure - positive in nine of the ten worst 60/40 drawdowns - rests on one conflicted source built from hypothetical, simulated returns.[^aqrcentury] No independent, no-COI replication of that century-scale figure is cited here.
- That the two divergent edges are complementary in their failure timing is our own synthesis from the episode evidence, untested - not a claim any single source makes. It is the part most in need of a direct test on our data, and no opposing source for the combined-sleeve prescription has been sought yet.

## Strategy hypotheses this could seed

- [ ] A flight-to-safety sleeve (long Treasuries / gold / dollar, conditioned on rising volatility) produces positive returns in growth-shock crashes (2018-Q4, 2020-Q1) but not in 2022 - test the regime-conditionality directly.
- [ ] A time-series-momentum sleeve across the universe is convex: positive in slow/2022-style declines, flat-to-negative in fast V-shaped shocks - confirm the smile and the fast-crash failure.
- [ ] The two divergent edges are complementary: a combined sleeve has positive returns across a wider set of crash episodes than either alone (our synthesis - the key claim to falsify).
- [ ] A defensive-equity tilt (e.g. utilities) loses less but rarely gains in absolute terms - confirm it is a cushion, not a signal, and therefore a weaker allocator input.
