# The Orthogonal Non-Equity Trend Universe

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> Breadth and convexity are answers to different questions, and a trend book that conflates them mis-sizes its universe. A broad, non-equity-tilted, long/short cross-asset substrate is the right structure - the diversification literature supports it, and its crisis alpha lives in the rates, currency and commodity legs, not the equity leg - but adding markets to it is a **Sharpe** lever, not a convexity one: the information ratio climbs on $\sqrt{\text{independent bets}}$ and flattens somewhere between 20 and 50 futures markets, while the *convex shape* a crisis-alpha seat is hired for is beta-timing on a handful of large macro betas, so a convexity-ranked book naturally collapses toward a small orthogonal core rather than a wide one. The marginal breadth that does pay lives almost entirely in **commodities** (the financial axes saturate at one bet per family), one liquid instrument captures a whole family because asset-class trend is a latent factor, and on a small fixed-fee UCITS book the fee floor and ETC borrow cap the *economic* count well below the futures ceiling. This note is the per-axis map: one bare orthogonal instrument per macro family, tagged by whether it adds **convexity** (a candidate for a crisis-alpha seat) or only **Sharpe/breadth** (a challenger universe with a different objective).

## The case for a broad, non-equity-tilted substrate

Before asking how many markets or which speeds, the more basic question is what asset-class substrate a trend book should be built on in the first place. The substrate that answers it well is old and well-replicated: a broad cross-asset futures book - equity indices, government bonds and short rates, developed and emerging currencies, and physical commodities - held long or short and risk-weighted so no single class dominates. Lintner's 1983 study established that adding managed futures to a stock-bond portfolio improved the efficient frontier materially,[^lintner] and the century-long reconstruction of time-series momentum on this universe was positive in 8 of the 10 worst drawdowns of a 60/40 portfolio over 1880-2016.[^century] A peer-reviewed stochastic-spanning test makes the static version of the same point: stock-bond portfolios cannot replicate portfolios augmented with individual commodity futures, FX and real estate, and the spanning benefit is strongest in down markets.[^spanning] The substrate is also shared rather than trend-exclusive: carry, trend's mirror concave premium, is present in all nine asset classes the same futures book spans, so a trend sleeve and a carry sleeve can be read off the same instrument set with different signals rather than built as two disjoint universes.[^carry]

The convexity this substrate is prized for does not come from the equity leg. Man Group's decomposition of trend returns through equity crises finds the crisis alpha has historically come from fixed income, commodities and currencies, not from short-equity positions;[^truthortrend] Fidelity's attribution reaches the same place by a different method, with beta-timing across the non-equity-dominated classes carrying most of the crisis-alpha character and the cross-sectional equity component a minority of the risk (the source of the beta-timing split used below).[^fidelity] ThinkNewfound supplies the mechanism: a diversified book's left-tail payoff comes from cross-asset correlations crashing to extremes in stress, which lets the notional in short-equity, long-bond and short-commodity trades far exceed what an equity-only book could carry at the same risk target, provided enough other markets are trending at once.[^newfound] The peer-reviewed CTA-positions study closes the loop: crisis alpha originates from diversification across many futures markets plus fast exposure reduction in the crisis sector, not from a directional equity short.[^crisismyth] The construction consequence is direct: tilt the substrate away from equity, because an equity-heavy book collapses onto a single market factor and a lagged trend signal on it realizes the short-gamma opposite of the shape being sought.

That case has a credible opposing literature, and it matters because it pins down the *form* the substrate must be held in, not whether it belongs at all. A peer-reviewed study finds that adding five alternative asset classes (real estate, commodities, hedge funds, emerging markets, private equity) to a stock-bond book was harmful for US investors over 1997-2015, driven by estimation risk that grows with the number of noisy-input classes.[^harmful] Morningstar's repeated finding is that an eleven-asset diversified book lost to plain 60/40 on the Sharpe ratio over most rolling ten-year windows since ~2005, because the diversifiers cut risk but not enough to clear their return give-up, and correlations converge in exactly the crises diversification is bought for.[^morningstar] These results do not contradict the substrate case; they describe holding the same classes as **passive longs**, where each class contributes a noisy mean estimate and a long-only beta that re-correlates in a crash. The crisis-alpha evidence above is about holding the identical classes under a **long/short trend signal**, where the position flips to short in a downtrend and the payoff is convex rather than collinear - the squaring identity in the next section makes the gap quantitative. Passive multi-asset and long/short multi-asset are different objects: the first is the one the counter-literature finds harmful, the second is the one the crisis-alpha literature finds convex.

## Two questions wearing one word

"How many markets should the trend book trade?" is really two questions that the breadth literature keeps welding together. One is a **Sharpe** question: how many *independent bets* can the universe carry before the risk-adjusted return stops climbing? The other is a **convexity** question: where does the long-gamma, positive-skew payoff a crisis-alpha seat is hired for actually come from? They have different answers.

Grinold's law sets risk-adjusted skill at $IR \approx IC \times \sqrt{BR}$, where breadth is the number of *independent* bets, not the nominal asset count.[^grinold] For a single-signal trend program the information coefficient is roughly fixed by the signal, so breadth is the lever, and the universe is where you pull it. That is the Sharpe question, and the empirical answer is well-mapped (below). But a convexity objective is not a Sharpe objective: it ranks on an intrinsic own-horizon skew ([[what-makes-a-trend-sleeve-convex]]), and against that objective breadth runs backwards. Equity-heavy breadth realizes *short* gamma under a trend label, because an equity-dominated book collapses onto a single market factor; a minimal, orthogonal, non-equity-macro set realizes the long-gamma shape instead, and a book ranked on convexity tends to shed correlated equity breadth and concentrate into a handful of orthogonal crisis engines rather than grow wide (the measured result on our own book is in the hypotheses below). The reconciliation is Fidelity's decomposition: most of a trend book's risk and crisis alpha is *beta-timing* a few large macro betas, only a minority is cross-sectional market selection ([[what-makes-a-trend-sleeve-convex]]).[^fidelity] Breadth improves the Sharpe and the robustness of the sleeve; it is not the source of the convex shape. So the two questions pull apart, and the universe design has to serve them separately.

## The Sharpe lever and where it saturates

The breadth-adds-Sharpe claim is real and quantified, and the number is a plateau, not a line. Graham's long-running exercise (Tricker and Mitchell, refreshed May 2026) adds markets to a generic (20,120) trend model in order of liquidity and plots IR against count: the IR climbs steeply at first, **flattens between roughly 20 and 50 markets**, and past ~50 the additions "do not capture risk factors that are not already contained in the portfolio."[^graham] Their earlier note put the diminishing-returns knee at 40-60 markets and built a Market Diversification Index - the effective number of *independent* macro markets - that routinely collapses below 4 in crises and shifted structurally lower after 2008.[^graham] The mechanism is Grinold's, read through the effective-bet count: under equicorrelation a book of $N$ assets at pairwise correlation $\rho$ carries $N_{\text{eff}} = N / (1 + (N-1)\rho)$ independent bets, so at an equity-book $\rho \approx 0.85$ fifty tickers are ~1.2 bets, while at a non-equity $\rho \approx 0.20$ they are ~4.6.[^resolve] Measured on a real futures book, ReSolve finds 10.3 independent bets under inverse-vol weighting, rising to 16-22 under optimization - a Sharpe multiplier of ~1.5-1.9 over the naive book.[^resolve] And the best trend *years* run on even fewer axes: Quantica needed only 8 statistical factors to replicate the industry's 2022 return (its best year), against 33 in 2023 (its worst), an inverse-linear relationship between factor count and performance.[^quantica24q1] So the honest target is a low-teens effective-bet count, occupied once each - not a fifty-name sprawl whose marginal names are correlation, cost, and turnover.

## The marginal breadth is all in commodities

The decisive refinement - and the one that reshapes the per-axis budget - is *where* in a non-equity book the marginal bet still pays. It is not the financial axes. When Graham splits the additive markets, "the commodities have diversifying potential, the financial markets do not": beyond the essentials, adding rate and FX markets barely moves the IR while adding commodities keeps lifting it.[^graham] Quantica quantifies the same asymmetry inside commodities: the ten most-liquid commodity futures carry an average trend-return correlation of 0.30, but across the full 69 that average falls to **0.06**, taking the diversification multiplier from 1.65 to 3.6 - and crucially the *less-liquid* single commodities are what carry it, with no evidence that they trend better, only that they trend *less correlated*.[^quantica25q1] Auspice makes the intuition concrete: sugar, crude and milk are far less correlated to each other than the S&P, ACWI and Euro Stoxx are, so fifty commodity markets buy more independent bets than a hundred financial ones.[^auspice]

The engine underneath is a quadratic. In *trend* space, correlation is roughly the square of the underlying correlation: two assets that co-move at $\rho$ produce trend streams that co-move at ~$\rho^2$, because when one rises and the other falls you are long one and short the other and the P&Ls align.[^gresham] A 0.4 underlying correlation is a 0.16 trend correlation - which is why a trend book diversifies better than an equity book of the same count, and why *selecting low-underlying-correlation legs (commodities) has a quadratic payoff* in breadth. The same algebra warns that no genuinely negative-correlation market exists in trend space (the sign is squared away), so breadth caps out: past the orthogonal core, added names are near-duplicates that concentrate the book on the "macro trend" factor rather than diversifying it - many markets, one bet.[^gresham] The construction rule that falls out is asymmetric: spend exactly **one** bare name on each financial family (they saturate at ~one effective bet), and spend the remaining budget on **single commodities**, where the marginal orthogonality actually lives.

## One bare instrument per axis is enough

Why one name per financial family, not several? Because asset-class trend is a *latent factor*. Carver's point from replication work is that trend-following a broad index captures most of the P&L you would get from trend-following its constituents, so a single liquid representative of a macro family delivers most of that family's trend return.[^carver] It is also how the ETF industry already builds these products: the standard managed-futures replication set is ~20 contracts, five per sector (equity, fixed income, FX, commodity).[^kaminski] The families themselves are low-dimensional - a rate curve is level-plus-slope (the level factor alone is ~80% of curve variance), the FX complex is dominated by a single dollar factor (~60-80%), and even the commodity complex reduces to an energy factor and a metals factor at the top - so a second long-Treasury line or a second G10 cross is a near-duplicate in trend space, not a new axis.[^pca] This is the licence for the minimal design: the book is a set of *axes occupied once*, and depth within an axis is redundancy, not breadth.

## The wrapper transfers the math but taxes it

The breadth arithmetic is wrapper-agnostic - $\sqrt{BR}$ does not care whether a bet is a future or an ETF - but three taxes make a small UCITS book saturate *lower* than the futures ceiling. First, replication tax: managed-futures ETFs capture only about a quarter of the trend-divergence signal that the futures indices capture (roughly 50%+), delivering "less trend beta and less crisis alpha" from slower systems and *selective, smaller* market exposure.[^rpm] Second, the fee floor: at small AUM the frictional cost of each added name dominates its diversification benefit, and the optimal count collapses to a handful - Scherer's framework puts a low-single-digit optimum on a small, cost-sensitive account, exactly the regime of an account paying a flat per-order fee minimum ([[budgeting-the-divergent-seat]]).[^scherer] Third, the borrow constraint: single-commodity UCITS lines are **ETCs** (debt securities), buyable long but carrying little-to-no short inventory, which walls off the short side of a self-built commodity book, so the commodity breadth is available only as *long* legs. The US-listed ETF proxies that map these axes cleanly are worse than un-shortable: a EUR-retail account cannot even buy them (PRIIPs/KID rejection), so they are evidence about the axes, never a tradeable book. The consequence is that the UCITS book's economic saturation sits below the futures 20-50 - the binding constraint is the fee floor and the ETC borrow, not the axis count.

## The per-axis map

Each macro family gets one bare, orthogonal UCITS line, tagged by what it adds. **[convex]** = a scarce/haven asset or a beta-timing crisis axis whose *own* shape is long-gamma, a candidate for a crisis-alpha seat. **[breadth]** = genuinely orthogonal but a Sharpe/robustness lever whose standalone shape is not convex, and which *dilutes* a convexity-ranked book - the material of a Sharpe-ranked challenger instead. The named lines are LSE-listed representatives of each axis; verify live tradability on the target broker before any test.

| Macro axis | Bare UCITS line | What makes it orthogonal | Adds |
|---|---|---|---|
| US long duration | `IDTL` | Real-yield / flight-to-safety; the deflation-crisis leg | **[convex]** beta-timing crisis alpha, scarce-asset haven |
| EUR long duration (Bund bloc) | iShares € Govt 15-30y | Distinct ECB policy cycle; Bund is the *institutional*-crisis haven where UST is the *economic* one | **[convex]** second orthogonal duration bloc, haven |
| Gold | `IGLN` | Monetary scarce asset, no cash flow; geopolitical-crisis haven | **[convex]** the strongest single engine on this map |
| The dollar | WisdomTree long-USD ETP | The FX *level* factor (DOL, ~60-80% of FX variance); strengthens in economic/institutional stress | **[convex]** scarce-asset haven, beta-timing |
| Funding-haven FX (JPY) | `SJPY` long-JPY ETP | Carry-unwind risk-off leg, distinct from the dollar | **[convex]** risk-off convexity uncorrelated to USD |
| Real rates (US TIPS) | `ITPS` | Inflation-vs-nominal axis; decouples from nominal only on an inflation shock | **[weak-convex]** inflation-shock hedge, but co-crashes with nominal on a growth shock |
| Broad commodity | `ICOM` | Reflation / growth-inflation basket, optimized roll | **[convex-ish]** an inflation-crisis engine; straddles the tag |
| Energy (crude/Brent) | `BRNT` ETC | Supply-shock axis; asymmetric spike in energy-driven inflation (2022) | **[breadth]** orthogonal supply risk, weak crisis convexity |
| Natural gas | `NGAS` ETC | Weather / local storage; ~0 trend-correlation to crude - the marginal-breadth carrier | **[breadth]** highest orthogonality per name (Quantica: gas/power ~40% of alt-market edge) |
| Industrial metals (copper) | `COPA` ETC | Global-growth cycle metal | **[breadth]** pro-cyclical, co-crashes - Sharpe not convexity |
| Agriculture (grains/softs) | `AIGA` / `WEAT` ETC | Weather-driven, macro-orthogonal supply shocks | **[breadth]** genuinely uncorrelated to G10 macro, low crisis convexity |
| Carbon (EUA) | `CARB` ETC | Cap-and-trade policy; correlation ~0 to equities, bonds *and* commodities | **[breadth]** cleanest orthogonality, but a weak crisis hedge and thin (~EUR 50M) |

The financial families each take one line; the commodity block is where the count can grow toward the ~20 cap by splitting into its non-redundant single names (a second precious metal, a second grain, gasoline alongside crude), because that is the only region where the marginal name still lowers the average correlation. Silver is the cautionary case: as a second "precious" it looks like a second gold, but its industrial-demand load means it does *not* inherit gold's clean haven convexity,[^pca] so it is breadth, not a convex engine.

## What the marginal breadth buys a small book

Applying the tags above, a convexity-ranked seat should expand only into the axes tagged **[convex]**, never into the **[breadth]** block, and a book already anchored on US duration, gold and broad commodity has three untested convex candidates left on the map:

- **EUR long duration (Bund bloc)** - a second orthogonal duration engine on a different central-bank cycle, and the institutional-crisis haven that a single-country long-duration line is not.
- **The dollar** - the FX level factor and a scarce-asset haven that strengthens in exactly the economic and institutional stresses where gold and duration can lag.
- **A JPY funding haven** - a risk-off leg whose convexity is uncorrelated to the dollar's.

The right test for each is a drop-one-*in* on the book's held-out convexity payoff: a candidate earns its slot only if it *lifts* the book's convexity, or holds it while cutting drawdown - the same bar any existing convex holding had to clear to stay in (the measured result on our own book is in the hypotheses below). The breadth block (energy, gas, metals beyond gold, ags, carbon) is explicitly **not** for a convexity-ranked seat: on that objective it dilutes the shape rather than adding to it, because a wide, correlated commodity block pulls the book's realized correlation to equities back up even as it lifts nominal name count. Those axes are the universe for a *Sharpe*-ranked challenger instead - a separate sleeve, budgeted separately ([[budgeting-the-divergent-seat]]) - where the √BR lever is the right objective and the commodity breadth pays.

## Limitations

- **The two objectives can conflict in sizing.** Grafting a convex axis that also raises correlation to a held leg can lift standalone convexity yet cut the *book's* effective breadth; the drop-one-in test measures combined convexity, not each axis in isolation, so a candidate that helps alone can still be redundant in the book.
- **The effective-bet count is regime-dependent and rises toward one in crises.** Static full-sample $\rho$ overstates the breadth available in stress - exactly when the payoff is wanted - so the realized plateau sits lower than the calm-market estimate.[^graham]
- **The commodity breadth is long-only on this substrate.** Single-commodity UCITS are ETCs with no borrow, so the Sharpe-challenger universe cannot run the short side that carries part of trend's skew; the futures wrapper that reclaims it is a separate build, not this book.
- **The dollar and JPY legs are FX-ETP proxies.** The bare dollar line is a WisdomTree long-USD ETP (a basket or single-cross approximation), not a true DXY future; its roll, tracking and USD denomination separate it from the futures axis, and the EUR-investor currency overlay is a distinct question from the instrument test.
- **The passive/trend distinction is a comparison across literatures, not a single controlled study.** The harmful-diversification evidence is on passive longs;[^harmful] the crisis-alpha evidence is on long/short trend.[^truthortrend][^crisismyth] No one paper runs both arms on an identical universe and cost model, so the cleanest confirmation is our own pipeline, not a citation.
- **Crisis alpha from the non-equity legs is regime-conditional.** ThinkNewfound's own caveat is that the payoff requires enough markets to trend simultaneously, which past crises delivered but future ones need not.[^newfound] The most influential supporting decompositions are from CTA managers (conflict of interest);[^truthortrend] the peer-reviewed CTA-positions study is the neutral backbone.[^crisismyth]
- **The substrate's Sharpe verdict depends on the objective.** On *unconditional* Sharpe, plain 60/40 has beaten broad diversification since ~2005;[^morningstar] the substrate earns its slot on crisis-conditional and drawdown-path terms, not raw Sharpe.
- **Carbon is orthogonal but marginal.** EUA correlation to equities, bonds and commodities sits near zero and its intra-family correlation to California allowances is only ~0.23,[^carbon] but its crisis-hedge effectiveness is below precious metals and agriculture, its own return is low, and the UCITS line is thin - a clean Sharpe diversifier, not a convex engine.
- **The per-axis PCA structure is a literature estimate, not a decomposition on our data.** The level/slope, dollar-factor and energy/metals figures come from published factor studies; the realized correlation structure of a specific UCITS book at a specific horizon is what the drop-one tests measure.[^pca]
