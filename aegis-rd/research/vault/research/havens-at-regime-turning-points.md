# Havens at Regime Turning Points

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> Inside a stable growth/inflation regime each canonical haven has a known job, but at the transition between regimes the only loosely reliable regularity is an ordering - commodities and the dollar turn first, duration turns last - and that ordering is exactly backwards for an allocation rule, because the haven whose role is most regime-conditional (long duration) is also the one that confirms the flip latest, while a 6/12-month momentum rank does not register the new regime until months after it has begun.

The literature on havens is comfortable *inside* a regime. Conditional on a low-and-stable-inflation world, long Treasuries hedge equity selloffs; conditional on an inflation shock, commodities and the dollar do the hedging and bonds fail. That conditional map is well-established and is the subject of the universe articles here.[^divergent][^crashuniv] The hard question is the seam between the two pictures: what happens to long Treasuries, gold, the dollar, commodities, defensive equities and cash in the *months around* a flip, when the regime that defines which haven works is itself changing. This is where an allocation rule actually lives - it has to decide what to hold while the regime is mid-transition and the historical correlations it leans on are in the act of inverting. The motivating failure is concrete: a momentum-gated haven basket that mechanically rotates into yesterday's winners will hold the inflation trio into the moment an inflation regime tips over into a deflationary bust, because the trio was still the trailing winner on the day the regime changed. That single observation generalises into a question about transition speed, and the answer turns out to be unfriendly.

## Is there a stylised sequence, or is each flip idiosyncratic?

The practitioner tradition says there is a sequence. The intermarket framework associated with John Murphy and the six-stage business-cycle model of Martin Pring both assert a chronological ordering: in an inflationary upswing commodities rise first and signal inflation, bonds sell off as rate hikes get priced, the dollar moves, and equities crack last; when the cycle rolls over the sequence reverses - bonds bottom and rally first, commodities soften, the dollar turns, and equities bottom last.[^murphy][^pring] The ordering has an economic spine. Commodities are the inflation thermometer, so they lead the inflation regime by construction; the dollar is driven by the rate differential the inflation forces into being; duration is the asset that reprices once the rate path itself turns. Stated as a turning-point cascade, the practitioner claim is "real assets and the currency turn at the inflection, duration confirms once the policy path bends."

There is one peer-reviewed result that the ordering is more than chart folklore. Liang and Yen apply spectral analysis to US bond, equity and commodity markets and find statistically significant lead-lag relationships keyed to a 3.5-to-7.5-year business cycle: the cycle leads commodities, lags then leads stocks and bonds, and the bond market leads the commodity market.[^liangyen] That is genuine evidence of an ordering, but at a cycle wavelength of years, not at the scale of a transition that an allocator must trade in months - and the paper's own framing requires the investor to "perfectly gauge their positioning in the business cycle," which is the thing in dispute.

The counter-evidence is that beyond a very short horizon the sequence does not hold out of sample. The cleanest test is sector rotation, which is the same lead-lag claim applied within equities: a study that explicitly searched for the conventional-wisdom ordering across the business cycle, trying lags out to 24 months, found "no results that conventional wisdom sequence holds or in any way provides a better predictor than any other sequence we can think of," with significant lead-lag relations only at the one-month horizon and otherwise "seemingly random" - and even granting the investor *perfect* business-cycle foresight, rotating by the conventional wisdom beat the market by at most 2.3% a year gross over 1948-2007, shrinking to 1.1-1.9% after costs, statistically indistinguishable from zero.[^sectorrot] Cross-asset regime work reaches a milder version of the same verdict: lead-lag clusters are *regime-specific* and have to be re-estimated per regime, which is another way of saying the ordering is not a fixed law you can carry across a transition.[^arxivleadlag] The honest synthesis is that an ordering exists as a central tendency at cycle scale but is too noisy and too regime-specific to trade as a rule at transition scale. That is the first reason a sequence-following allocator is on thin ice.

## The case taxonomy: the sequence is loose, but duration lags consistently

Walk the historical flips and a pattern survives the noise - not the full cascade, but its tail. Across regime transitions the assets that turn *first* (commodities, the dollar) vary in order and even in direction, while long duration turns *last* with surprising consistency. That is the load-bearing regularity, and it is the opposite of what an allocator would want, because the most regime-conditional haven is also the slowest to confirm.

**1973-74, inflation onset.** The post-Bretton-Woods inflation regime announced itself through commodities and the currency, not through bonds. Gold, freed to float, ran from roughly $40 in 1971 to about $190 by end-1974, a multi-hundred-percent move, and oil quadrupled in the embargo.[^pomegra][^commboom] The dollar fell against most major currencies as US inflation outran trading partners'.[^pomegra] Long Treasuries gave no shelter and were the *worst* major asset: the 30-year fell from about 79 cents to 73 cents on the dollar as the long yield climbed from ~6.3% to ~8.5%, and over the full Great Inflation long duration lost on the order of 50-70% in nominal terms - the canonical year-by-year return record shows long governments delivering negative real returns through the period while bills roughly kept pace with inflation.[^pomegra][^pomegra70s][^ibbotson] Equities ground down 48% peak to trough.[^pomegra] The "haven" that worked was the inflation basket; the duration haven was the loss engine. Commodities and the dollar carried the signal; bonds simply bled - and the comovement is measured, not anecdotal: bond-stock betas turned positive in 1974 as a 400-basis-point tightening met the supply shock, the same inflation-driven sign flip the stock-bond-correlation work below returns to.[^thorbecke]

**1981-82, Volcker disinflation.** This is the sharpest counter to "duration leads the turn." Inflation peaked and rolled over hard - CPI fell from above 10% in early 1981 to under 6% by mid-1982 - yet the 10-year Treasury yield *rose* from about 13% to over 14% across that window, because markets did not believe the disinflation would hold.[^goodfriend][^fedhist] Long rates did not begin to decline until October 1982, well after inflation had turned, when the Fed let funds fall and credibility finally arrived.[^fedhist] So at the single most important disinflation turn of the modern era, the duration haven lagged the regime by the better part of a year, and a bond holder who waited for price confirmation of the new regime missed the front of the greatest bond bull market in history. The 1982 long-Treasury return of roughly +40% was the *payoff* of the new regime, banked only once duration finally confirmed - months after commodities (which fell ~25% across the 1981-82 recession) and inflation had already turned.[^portviz][^imf8182]

**Mid-2008, inflation spike to deflationary bust inside one calendar year.** This is the textbook intramarket sequence and the textbook duration lag in one episode. Headline CPI peaked at 5.5% in July 2008 on an energy run-up; oil hit its all-time $145 high on 3 July; the CRB commodity complex and the dollar both turned on essentially the same dates - commodities topping and the dollar bottoming in July.[^treasfx][^afraidtotrade] Then real WTI fell about 53% from July to October as the bust took hold.[^hamilton] The commodity-top-and-dollar-bottom pair fired right on the practitioner script. But the Treasury haven lagged badly: long-bond prices spent most of 2008 in a roughly 0-to-+5% range and did not stage their big flight-to-safety surge until November - a four-month gap between the regime turning in July and duration confirming it.[^afraidtotrade] The official chronology says the same thing in yields: through the panic itself the long yield stayed sluggish, hovering near 4% while bills collapsed toward zero in November, and it broke to about 2% only in December - with even that final leg partly the Fed's asset-purchase announcements rather than a spontaneous haven bid.[^stlfed][^bisqr] An allocator reading price to decide what to hold would have seen the inflation basket roll over in July with nothing yet rising to rotate into; the deflation haven did not light up until the equity collapse was most of the way done.

**2000-02 and the post-2000 structural break.** The dot-com unwind is the transition that flipped the *baseline*: the stock-bond correlation switched sign from positive to negative around the turn of the century, which is why duration became a reliable equity hedge at all for the next two decades.[^cpv][^flr] Inside 2000-02 the bond hedge worked - it was a growth/disinflation shock, duration's home regime - but the regime-defining event was the sign switch itself, and that switch is the thing the later inflation transitions had to un-do.

**March-2020 and 2022.** These two are worked in detail in the existing articles and only the transition timing matters here. March 2020 was a deflation shock that, within weeks, the policy response turned into a reflation; even the cleanest growth-shock havens were *sold* at the equity low in the dash for cash before they worked.[^nyfed] 2022 was an inflation regime persisting through an equity bear, the year the duration hedge failed outright and the inflation basket (energy, the dollar) did the work - the transition the post-2000 baseline was not built for.[^crashuniv][^cpv]

The taxonomy verdict: the front of the sequence (commodities, dollar) is real but its *order* and sign shuffle case to case - the dollar fell in 1973-74's onset but rose in 2008's bust and 2022's persistence - so it is not a stable rule. The back of the sequence is the durable part: long duration confirms the new regime late in every case, by months (1981-82, 2008) up to years (the full 1970s). For an allocation rule this is the worst possible regularity, because it means the one haven whose usefulness is most regime-dependent is also the one that price will tell you about last.

## Regime-conditional versus regime-robust haven properties

Sorting the havens by how much their hedge depends on the regime is the useful cut, because it tells you which ones a transition can turn against you.

**Long duration is the most regime-conditional.** Its equity-hedging property is not structural - it is a function of whether growth or inflation volatility dominates, which is the macro-regime variable that decides the sign of the stock-bond correlation. Campbell, Pflueger and Viceira show the bond-stock beta is a macro-regime quantity that switched sign around 2001 and can switch back, and AQR's decomposition attributes roughly 70% of the long-run correlation to the relative volatility of growth versus inflation and their comovement.[^cpv][^aqrcorr] When the regime is an inflation/rate shock, duration is not a weak haven - it is on the *wrong side*, falling with equities, as 1973-74, 1981 and 2022 all show. This is the haven a transition can flip from asset to liability.

**Commodities are regime-conditional in the opposite direction.** They hedge inflation shocks and get destroyed in the deflationary bust that often ends an inflation regime - the 2008 round-trip (oil +100% into July, then -53% by October) is the whole conditionality in one year.[^hamilton][^afraidtotrade] Commodities are the asset most likely to be the *trailing winner on the day the regime flips against them*, which is the precise mechanism by which a momentum-gated haven basket gets caught holding the inflation trio into a bust.

**Gold sits in between, and the in-between is regime-conditional too.** Gold strongly hedges *energy* inflation but its core-inflation beta is statistically indistinguishable from zero;[^flr] its CPI sensitivity is time-varying and tied to the dollar, rising during dollar depreciations;[^goldcpi] and a Markov-switching analysis finds gold's response to inflation is "sharp and sustained" only in high-inflation regimes and "subdued" in low-inflation ones, which is why naive full-sample studies understate it.[^valadkhani] Gold also fails the liquidity test at the worst moment - it was sold with everything else in the March-2020 scramble before recovering.[^nyfed][^baurmcdermott] So gold is a *high-inflation-regime* haven that goes quiet between inflation episodes, not a both-regimes anchor.

**Defensive equity and cash are cushions, not signals, and the cushion is conditional.** Low-beta and defensive-sector equity loses less but still loses, and the sectors most often called defensive (utilities, REIT-like rate-sensitives) trade as bond proxies and took the 2022 rate leg on the chin - they are regime-conditional in the same direction as duration.[^divergent][^crashuniv] Cash is the only literally regime-robust holding, but it is regime-robust by *not hedging*: it neither gains in a crash nor loses, so it is a survival allocation, not a divergent haven that an allocator can read as a tell.

**The dollar is the closest thing to a both-regimes haven, and the reason is structural, not statistical.** It is the one asset with two independent channels that both point the same way in stress. The rate-differential channel pulls capital into the dollar when US inflation forces the Fed to tighten (the right side of the dollar smile, 2022), and the flight-to-liquidity channel pulls capital into dollar cash and Treasuries in any global risk-off regardless of US fundamentals, because the dollar is the world's funding and reserve currency (the left side, March 2020).[^crashuniv] Avdjiev and co-authors establish the dollar as a global risk factor; Lustig-Roussanov-Verdelhan show the dollar earns a premium for appreciating in bad global states.[^crashuniv] The dollar's regime-robustness is real but bounded - it rests on the reserve-currency convenience yield and fails when the US is itself the source of the stress (or when the Fed eases aggressively and the rate differential that drives the right-side channel collapses). The relevant point for transitions is that the dollar is the only haven whose hedge does not have to be switched off and on as the growth/inflation regime turns - which is exactly why it is the natural anchor for an allocation that has to survive the seam.

## How fast do momentum measures turn at a flip - the measurable lag

The transition-speed question has a sharp answer once you ask it the right way: how long after the regime turns does a 6- or 12-month momentum rank reflect the new regime, and the answer is "most of the lookback window," which is months.

Start with the regime variable itself. The stock-bond correlation does not switch instantly; it transitions, and the speed is observable. Smooth-transition-regression work finds the correlation regime is governed by the VIX, the short rate and the yield spread and moves *smoothly* between the negative and positive states rather than jumping.[^aslanidis][^ohmi] More tellingly, at the 2022 flip the bond-stock beta's rise from negative to positive came *relatively late* compared with the rise of inflation in 2021, and the brief beta flip after the Ukraine invasion was *short-lived* - so even the regime indicator lags the regime, and can give a false confirmation that reverses.[^cpvshort][^w34323] If the regime variable lags the regime, any price-momentum signal built to be slower than the regime variable lags it further.

Now the momentum signal. The whole point of a 12-month lookback is to suppress noise by averaging over a year of returns, which mechanically means it cannot reflect a turn until the new regime's returns have accumulated enough weight to outvote the old regime's. Goulding and Harvey formalise this as the central tension of trend speed: a long lookback reduces noise but is "slow to react to a turning point," a Type II error of missed detection, while a short lookback reacts fast but false-alarms on noise.[^goulding] A practitioner's-method primer puts numbers on the trade-off - a 6-12-month horizon gives "a reasonably fast response to market regime shifts" with low turnover, which is the *best* compromise, and it still means the signal is reacting on a multi-month delay; shorter 1-3-month horizons react faster but carry larger drawdowns and whipsaw.[^jpmmom] The arithmetic is unforgiving: a 12-month trailing rank gives roughly equal weight to each of the prior twelve months, so a regime that flipped two months ago is still outvoted ten-to-two by the regime that preceded it. The rank does not cross over until the new regime has run something like half the lookback - on the order of a *quarter to two quarters* for a 6/12-month signal.

This is precisely the lag that catches a momentum-gated haven basket. The inflation trio is the trailing 6/12-month winner on the day the inflation regime tips into a deflationary bust; the rank keeps the basket long the trio for months while commodities collapse, because the rank is still dominated by the months *before* the flip. The 2008 case makes the magnitude concrete - commodities and the dollar turned in July, but a 12-month momentum rank would not have demoted the commodity leg until late in the year, by which point DBC-style exposure had given back most of the inflation regime's gains. The measurable lag between the regime turning and the momentum rank reflecting it is, in these cases, the same order as the lookback itself: months, not weeks. A faster signal closes the lag but reintroduces exactly the whipsaw that a transition - which is noisy by definition, full of false turns like the Ukraine beta flip - punishes hardest.

The synthesis across the three questions is one uncomfortable alignment. The havens are ordered at a flip (commodities and dollar first, duration last); the haven whose role is most regime-conditional (duration) is the one that confirms last; and the momentum measure an allocator would use to follow the rotation lags the regime by most of its own lookback. All three point the same way: a transition is the one environment where a follow-the-trend haven rule is structurally late, and the only haven that does not require the rule to be timed correctly is the dollar, whose two stress channels mean it does not have to be switched as the regime turns.

## Limitations

- The stylised sequence's strongest statements are practitioner-grade with conflicts of interest (intermarket and six-stage frameworks sold by their authors), and the one peer-reviewed lead-lag result operates at a multi-year cycle wavelength, not at the transition timescale an allocator trades. The counter-evidence (no sequence beyond one-month lags out of sample) is itself drawn mostly from within-equity sector rotation, an analogy to cross-asset behaviour rather than a direct test. Confidence that *an* ordering exists: medium. Confidence that it is tradeable as a rule at transition scale: low.
- "Duration lags consistently" rests on a handful of historical flips (1973-74, 1981-82, 2008), which is a small sample by construction - there are only so many regime transitions. The 1981-82 lag is well-documented and load-bearing; the 2008 four-month gap, initially taken from a single practitioner cross-asset review, is now corroborated by the St. Louis Fed and BIS chronologies of the Treasury market through the panic (long yields sluggish near 4% until November-December, and the final leg partly policy-driven rather than a spontaneous haven bid). Treat the regularity as a strong tendency, not a law.
- The momentum-lag arithmetic ("roughly half the lookback") is a mechanical property of equal-weighted trailing windows, not an empirical estimate; the exact crossover depends on the size and persistence of the post-flip move and on the specific signal construction. It is a lower-bound intuition, not a measured number, and our own runs are where it must be measured.
- The inflation-beta evidence (gold/commodities hedge energy not core inflation; betas regime-dependent) is well-supported across independent peer-reviewed sources, but it is measured on monthly index data over decades; whether those betas are stable enough *within* a single transition to act on is not something the slow-moving kernel estimates can answer.

- [ ] **Demote the reigning winner faster.** The D1 refutation replaced the fixed-laggard reading with a rotating one, and in each observed transition the leg that lagged was the *pre-flip winner* (commodities into 2008, the dollar into 2021-22). Test an asymmetric demotion clock on the same basket: the leg currently ranked top by trailing return uses the fastest existing lookback (63d) for its own exit while the others stay at 252d. Practitioner cross-asset review; chronology corroborated by the Treasury FX report and Hamilton. https://blog.afraidtotrade.com/comparing-cross-market-performance-for-2008/
[^hamilton]: Hamilton, J., "Causes and Consequences of the Oil Shock of 2007-08", Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, 2009; and Caballero et al., "Financial Crash, Commodity Prices, and Global Imbalances", BPEA 2008. Oil hit an all-time $145 on 3 July 2008; real WTI fell ~53% July-to-October as the bust took hold - the commodity round-trip inside one year. Peer-reviewed / institutional. https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/2009a_bpea_hamilton.pdf
[^cpv]: Campbell, J., Pflueger, C. & Viceira, L., "Macroeconomic Drivers of Bond and Equity Risks", Journal of Political Economy 128(8):3148-3185, 2020. The bond-stock beta is a macro-regime variable that switched sign around 2001 (positive to negative) and can switch back; the sign decides whether duration hedges equities. Peer-reviewed, no COI. https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/707766
[^flr]: Fang, X., Liu, Y. & Roussanov, N., "Getting to the Core: Inflation Risks Within and Across Asset Classes", NBER Working Paper 30169, 2022. Core inflation betas of stocks are negative; gold, commodities, currencies and REITs hedge *energy* inflation but not *core* (gold and platinum core betas indistinguishable from zero); TIPS headline beta 0.64, core beta 4.54; betas are time-varying and split at the ~2000 structural break, with energy betas rising significantly post-1999. The regime-conditional inflation-hedge anchor. Peer-reviewed working paper, no COI. https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30169/w30169.pdf
[^goldcpi]: Beckmann & Czudaj / Tkacz et al., "On the economic determinants of the gold-inflation relation", Resources Policy, 2014. Gold's long-term CPI beta is time-varying (Kalman filter), declined in the 1990s and rose again in the 2000s, and is forecastable by short- and long-term interest rates; gold's inflation sensitivity rises during dollar depreciations. Peer-reviewed. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0301420714000312
[^valadkhani]: Valadkhani, A. & O'Mahony, B., "Dynamic hedging responses of gold and silver to inflation: A Markov regime-switching VAR analysis", The North American Journal of Economics and Finance, 2024 (monthly data 1975-2023). Gold's response to inflation is "sharp and sustained" in high-inflation regimes and "subdued" in low-inflation periods; studies that ignore prolonged high-inflation regimes mistakenly conclude gold's hedge has decayed; silver complements gold in low-inflation periods. Peer-reviewed, no COI. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1059056024007330
[^aqrcorr]: Brixton, A. et al. (AQR), "A Changing Stock-Bond Correlation", Journal of Portfolio Management, Q1 2023. The relative volatility of growth versus inflation and their comovement explains ~70% of the rolling 10-year stock-bond correlation; the sign decides whether bonds hedge. AQR markets diversifiers (COI on the prescription); the regime model is sound. https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/A-Changing-Stock-Bond-Correlation
[^baurmcdermott]: Baur, D. & McDermott, T., "Is Gold a Safe Haven? International Evidence", Journal of Banking & Finance 34:1886-1898, 2010; with Baur & Lucey 2010. Gold is a safe haven for US/European equities but the effect is short-lived (~15 trading days) and not universal across markets. Peer-reviewed. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378426609003343
[^nyfed]: Federal Reserve Bank of New York, "The Global Dash for Cash in March 2020", Liberty Street Economics, 2022. Even safe havens, including gold and Treasuries, were sold for dollars at the equity low; sovereign dollar-reserve sales exceeded 80% of total reserve sales. Fed research (institutional COI). https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2022/07/the-global-dash-for-cash-in-march-2020/
[^aslanidis]: Aslanidis, N. & Christiansen, C., "Smooth transition patterns in the realized stock-bond correlation", Journal of Empirical Finance 19(4):454-464, 2012. The realized stock-bond correlation moves *smoothly* between large-positive and large-negative regimes, governed by the short rate, yield spread and VIX (high VIX -> negative correlation / flight to safety); the transition is gradual, not a jump. Peer-reviewed, no COI. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927539812000254
[^ohmi]: Ohmi, H. & Okimoto, T., "Trends in Stock-Bond Correlations", Applied Economics 48(6):536-552, 2016 (RIETI DP 15-E-115). G7 stock-bond correlations fell from over +0.4 (1999) to about -0.30 (2003) and ~-0.53 by 2012; the regime change is driven by VIX and a time trend and is gradual. Peer-reviewed / institutional, no COI. https://www.rieti.go.jp/jp/publications/dp/15e115.pdf
[^cpvshort]: Brixton, A. et al. (AQR), "A Changing Stock-Bond Correlation: Explaining Short-term Fluctuations", SSRN 4672744, 2023. The macro framework explains ~70% of long-run correlation variation; tactical (3-month) forecasting adds forecaster-disagreement measures - implying the correlation is hard to forecast at short horizons even with the right model. AQR COI on the prescription. https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4672744
[^w34323]: NBER Working Paper 34323 (2025, building on Campbell-Pflueger-Viceira and Pflueger 2025). After 2000 bond-stock betas turned negative in the US, UK and Eurozone; the 2022 rise back toward positive came "relatively late compared to the rise of inflation in 2021"; the Ukraine-invasion beta flip was "short-lived." Evidence the regime indicator itself lags the regime and can give a reversing false signal. Peer-reviewed working paper. https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34323/revisions/w34323.rev0.pdf
[^ibbotson]: Ibbotson, R. G. & Sinquefield, R. A., "Stocks, Bonds, Bills, and Inflation: Year-by-Year Historical Returns (1926-1974)", Journal of Business 49(1):11-47, 1976. The canonical peer-reviewed series of total and real returns for stocks, long governments, corporates, bills and inflation; long governments' real returns are negative through the early-1970s inflation while bills roughly track inflation. No COI. https://doi.org/10.1086/295803
[^thorbecke]: Thorbecke, W., "Bond-Stock Price Comovements: Evidence from the 1960s to the 1990s", International Journal of Financial Studies 14(3):59, 2026. Bond-stock betas turned positive in 1974 - inflation and monetary-policy news moved both markets in the same direction - after the funds rate rose 400bp between February and June 1974; comovements stayed positive through 1991. Peer-reviewed, no COI. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijfs14030059
[^stlfed]: Noeth, B. & Sengupta, R., "Flight to Safety and U.S. Treasury Securities", Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, The Regional Economist, July 2010. Short Treasury yields fell to near zero in November 2008 while long-term yields stayed sluggish near 4%, falling to about 2% only in December - a decline partly prompted by the Fed's large-scale asset purchases. Fed (institutional COI), primary yield chronology. https://www.stlouisfed.org/publications/regional-economist/july-2010/flight-to-safety-and-us-treasury-securities
[^bisqr]: Fender, I. & Gyntelberg, J., "Overview: global financial crisis spurs unprecedented policy actions", BIS Quarterly Review, December 2008. The four-stage post-Lehman chronology: government bond yields declined in very volatile conditions as recession fears and safe-haven flows outweighed anticipated deficit expansion, with the decisive moves from mid-October. Institutional, no COI. https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt0812a.pdf
[^goulding]: Goulding, C., Harvey, C. & Mazzoleni, M., "Momentum Turning Points", Journal of Financial Economics 149(3), 2023. Trend speed trades off Type II missed-detection (slow 12-month signal, slow to react to turning points) against Type I false alarms (fast 1-month signal, noise-driven); turning points are exactly where time-series-momentum strategies place bad bets. Peer-reviewed, no COI. https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P158_Momentum_turning_points.pdf
[^jpmmom]: J.P. Morgan, "Momentum Strategies Across Asset Classes" (2015). A 6-12-month trend horizon gives "a reasonably fast response to market regime shifts" with low turnover and better risk-adjusted performance than 1-3-month horizons, which react faster but carry larger drawdowns and whipsaw - the lookback/responsiveness trade-off quantified. Sell-side practitioner with shown method. https://www.cmegroup.com/education/files/jpm-momentum-strategies-2015-04-15-1681565.pdf
