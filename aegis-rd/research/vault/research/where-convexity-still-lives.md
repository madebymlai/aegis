# Where Convexity Still Lives

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> "Trend convexity is decaying" conflates three different facts: the flagship CTA product's *benchmark* convexity to equities did collapse after 2014, but that is a capacity-driven design *choice* (managers drifted to slow signals), not the death of the property; the one genuinely structural, non-reversible death is *fast* trend on electronic *small-tick* markets (the impact loop broke under HFT market-making); and the *intrinsic* skew of *slow* trend on *large-tick, non-equity* macro markets - the axis our ranker prices - is intact. Convexity has not left the market; it has been designed out of the wrong product and measured on the wrong axis. No non-trend source wins the seat outright: bought convexity is the tail tier's job (division of labor), the concave income is carry's, and the only convex non-trend systematics (cross-sectional / macro-divergence) are things to *combine* with the trend pole, not replacements for it.

The trend seat exists to be the floor's drawdown payer: slow long gamma, positive skew, on a non-equity-dominated substrate ([[the-tiered-strategy-roster]], [[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]]). The industry has spent three years arguing that exactly this property is disappearing. If it is, our ranker is pricing a ghost. This article separates what has actually decayed from what has not, and asks whether any non-trend source now buys the convex shape more cheaply than a re-specified trend pole. The answer is that the decay is real but confined to a corner the seat deliberately avoids, that our own book is a clean instance of the confinement, and that the honest response is a decay *monitor* plus a convex *complement*, not a re-specification of the metric or a defection from trend.

## Three claims wearing one coat

The load-bearing evidence for decay is Versor's *Has Trend Gone Flat?*. Regressing SG Trend returns on the S&P 500 and its square over rolling five-year windows, they find the index's convexity fell from nearly 2 in the post-2008 years to zero or below after about 2014, and turned negative for the long-term signals that now dominate large CTAs.[^versor] A convexity near 2 means a strategy that tracks equities up *and* rises when they fall; a convexity of zero means it tracks equities both ways - no protection. The cause they name is not signal erosion but capacity: trend AUM grew ~50% after 2008, and to absorb it managers migrated to slower signals that trade less, and slower signals carry less convexity. "It's not that the signals have changed their stripes. The managers have."[^versormannix]

That is a real finding, but it is a claim about *one estimator on one benchmark on one product*, and three distinct things hide inside the headline. The first is the benchmark-convexity of the SG Trend index against the S&P 500 - which did fall, and is a *design choice* driven by capacity, reversible in principle by tilting faster, but capacity-bound for a giant. The second is whether the convex *shape* is still *reachable* at faster speeds - Versor says yes (a short-term signal still showed convexity 0.5-1), and prescribes tilting faster.[^versor] The third is whether the *intrinsic* long-gamma skew of a slow trend rule - measured at its own horizon rather than against equities - has decayed, which Versor's benchmark regression does not measure at all. These three have different answers, and the campaign's own book turns out to be the cleanest place to see them come apart.

## The wall in front of "just go faster"

Versor's fix - tilt to shorter signals - collides with the best-identified structural break in the recent trend literature. Kurth, Eisler, Rej and Bouchaud document that since roughly 2008/9, *short-horizon* trend has ceased to deliver on a cross-section of ~100 futures, while *slow* signals are approximately intact; the break is abrupt (a regime shift, not gradual decay), and it is asset-class heterogeneous - sparing yields and most commodities while strongly depressing equity indices and currencies.[^micro] Their mechanism is the sharpest part. Trend is, in large part, a self-fulfilling impact loop: signals trigger directional trades whose market impact reinforces the move that produced the signal. The post-crisis shift to HFT-dominated market-making withdrew the resting depth that let trend followers execute aggressively on *small-tick* contracts; once they disengaged, the loop broke on its input side and the small-tick signal itself - not just its harvest - decayed. On *large-tick* books, residual depth remained and both signal and PnL survived.[^micro] The decisive variable is the volatility-normalised tick size, and no partition by asset class or liquidity reproduces the dichotomy.

This is the wall. The fast signals that carry the best convex *shape* (Versor) are the same ones whose *return* has structurally collapsed on the electronic, small-tick, equity-and-FX markets (Kurth et al.), and the mortality is highest the further a program sits from slow trend.[^molyboga] So "restore convexity by going faster" is not a free lunch: faster buys shape and forfeits return on exactly the markets where the loop is broken. The source matters here because it cuts against its own authors' interest - it is a CFM-affiliated academic paper arguing that CFM's own product has structurally decayed - which is the opposite of the conflict that discounts most trend research. And it dovetails with the campaign's own kill of a fast leg, which re-imported the whipsaw the sleeve exists to ride through ([[trend-following-in-whipsaw-regimes]], [[what-makes-a-trend-sleeve-convex]]).

## The two convexities come apart on our own book

The reconciliation is that "convexity" names two different measurements that the literature freely conflates. *Benchmark* convexity is the Treynor-Mazuy $\beta_2$ of the stream against equities - Versor's estimator, and the one that collapsed. *Intrinsic* convexity is the positive skew of the stream measured over its own trend horizon, which CFM show is a built-in property of dynamic position-taking - the P&L is the excess of long-horizon over short-horizon realized variance, a chi-squared quantity with positive skew that holds even at zero expected return, provided you measure over the trend's timescale and not below it.[^cfm] These two need not move together, and on the campaign's book they visibly do not.

The atalanta champion's $\beta_2$ against the S&P 500 is flat-to-mildly-negative at every horizon - daily, monthly, quarterly - which is precisely the industry-wide benchmark-convexity decay Versor reports, reproduced on our own stream ([[what-makes-a-trend-sleeve-convex]] records the H1/H2 kills). Yet the same champion scores a strongly positive `trend_convexity_payoff` (~0.45), the intrinsic net-tail skew over overlapping two-to-six-month windows. The stream is not convex *to equities* and is convex *in itself*. Our ranker was built on the intrinsic axis on purpose ([[verifying-strategy-family-membership]]), and the theoretical literature is what justifies that choice: a diversified CTA loses some benchmark convexity precisely because it trades many contracts against a single equity reference, so the equity-$\beta_2$ understates the protection while the own-horizon skew preserves it.[^cfm] The property Versor says is disappearing (equity-$\beta_2$) is not the property our metric prices (intrinsic skew), and confusing the two would have us re-specifying a metric that is already on the surviving axis.

There is even a live disagreement about *which speed* is more convex, and it resolves the same way. Versor's realized regression finds short signals more convex than long over the 2010s;[^versor] Roncalli and Jusselin's option-profile algebra finds the opposite - convexity of the trend payoff *rises* with the moving-average duration, so long-term rules are theoretically more convex.[^roncalli] Both are right about different objects: the theoretical *payoff* is more convex the slower you go, but the *realized* benchmark-convexity of slow signals in the 2010s was low because, as Aspect's Remsing notes, the slow timeframe was simply never called upon - there was no sustained divergence lasting more than a handful of months, and in a genuine grinding bear the slow signals would be expected to out-convex the fast.[^versormannix] Decay of realized benchmark-convexity, environmental starvation of slow signals, and structural death of fast signals are three separate stories, and only the last is irreversible.

## Where convexity still lives, then

Line the three findings up and they point to the same corner. Convexity survives at *slow-to-medium* speed (Quantica's robust 60-120 day band, the campaign's ~190-day peak),[^quantica] on *large-tick, traditional-macro* markets (the rates, gold, commodity-major and FX legs the micro-paper's break spares),[^micro] measured at a *multi-month* horizon on the *intrinsic* skew axis. That description is the trend seat's spec almost verbatim: slow, non-equity-dominated, own-horizon. The decay Versor measures lives in the opposite corner - fast, small-tick, equity-index, benchmark-$\beta_2$ - which the seat was already written to avoid. The property is not gone; it has retreated to exactly the ground the seat stands on.

One caveat sharpens rather than softens this. Man's market-mix work finds that broad-selloff convexity concentrates in *traditional* markets, because a cross-asset crisis amplifies the largest macro factors, while *alternative* (niche, esoteric) markets add idiosyncratic Sharpe but dilute crisis convexity.[^manmix] The seat's "non-equity" mandate must therefore be read as *non-equity traditional macro* - rates, metals, energy, majors - not a drift into exotic alt-markets chasing breadth. Breadth that buys orthogonality is a Sharpe lever ([[the-orthogonal-non-equity-trend-universe]]); it is not where the convex shape comes from. The convex shape comes from timing a few large, large-tick macro betas well.

## What else buys it - and why none wins the seat

If convexity is retreating, the natural question is whether something non-trend now buys the shape more cheaply. Priced on one axis - net-of-cost convexity per unit of calm bleed - the field sorts cleanly, and nothing displaces the trend pole.

*Bought convexity* - long puts, long-vol, VIX calls, variance swaps - is the most reliable per event and the only thing that pays a *fast* gap that slow trend sleeps through. But it bleeds structurally, fighting both the equity and the volatility risk premia, and the recent consensus is that it only works net of cost if it is *actively* traded - dynamic strikes, disciplined monetisation, bought cheap in calm - not held as a passive sleeve.[^ambrus][^oneriver] That is a description of the tail tier ([[the-ucits-constrained-tail-sleeve]], [[monetizing-the-tail-sleeve]]), not a replacement for trend: the two are a division of labor across crisis *speed*, and the roster already runs both. Man's own head-to-head makes the point from the trend side - over a three-month horizon a trend sleeve beats an options straddle in *both* tails while keeping a positive average return, because trend pays its convexity as realized variance rather than as option premium.[^manconvex]

*Non-trend systematic convexity* is real but is a complement, not a substitute. Cross-sectional and network momentum are relative-value bets on trend *dispersion* that like *correlated* universes, the mirror of time-series trend's preference for zero correlation,[^roncalli] and their *standalone* shape is the wrong sign for the seat: cross-sectional momentum is generically negatively skewed under realistic (near-zero cross-autocorrelation) parameters and measures as negatively *convex*,[^kwonsatchell][^seppcs] cross-sectional *equity* momentum embeds a written call on its short leg that turns it short-convexity in exactly the turbulent states that matter, which is why it crashes,[^danielcrash] and the one recent study that targets network momentum's convexity finds the standard construction *loses* convexity and positive skew unless it is grafted onto a continuous trend base.[^liferreira] They are convex only as complements married to the trend pole, never as standalone replacements - the non-equity cross-sections do not escape the crash either, and a thin UCITS cross-section is exactly the low-count, high-correlation setting where the rank carries least information (the campaign's own `cross_sectional_momentum` v1 confirmed this live, killed 2026-07-06 as non-convex and loss-making on a 5-name basket, below). Value and global-macro divergence carry positive convexity and are most useful precisely when trend is starved by uncertainty, making them genuine diversifiers of the trend pole rather than seat challengers.[^loim] Gold, the dollar and duration are convex "scarce" assets by coskewness, but conditionally so - regime-fragile, and duration can co-crash on an inflation shock.[^golts][^janus] And two candidates are ruled out as convex hedges outright: low-volatility equity co-crashes (positive equity correlation in the COVID gap),[^janus] and dispersion / vol-arb has no defined crisis payoff at all - it pays on convergence, not on the severity of the drawdown.[^ambrus] Versor's own constructive result is the tell: the way they raise a trend book's convexity is to *add* non-trend convex signals to it, not to replace trend.[^versor]

The reason none of this dethrones trend is the edge-existence argument the axis rests on ([[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]]). Convexity is something the market *charges* for - the crash-insurance premium is structural and paid to sellers, so a bought hedge is negative-carry by construction. Trend is the rare exception that is *paid* to be convex, because it manufactures the shape through dynamic trading rather than buying it, an anomaly rather than a risk premium.[^lemp] That anomaly's engine is exactly what has partly decayed (the impact loop, on fast/small-tick markets) and partly survived (information diffusion, on slow/large-tick markets).[^micro] So the honest read is not "trend is dead, buy convexity instead" - it is "trend's free convexity is thinner than a decade ago but intact on the substrate the seat trades, and everything else charges a premium trend does not."

That no single source wins does not make the seat a single mechanism, though. The newest hedge-quality work finds the optimum is an interior trend-plus-overlay hybrid rather than either pure sleeve, so the honest reading of "none wins" is that the seat is a *budget* across near-independent convexity axes, not a slot for one winner - the sizing question this article's verdict opens, and the subject of [[budgeting-the-divergent-seat]].

## Limitations

- The strongest decay evidence (Versor) and the strongest survival evidence (Kurth et al.) both have CFM/quant-fund proximity; the cleanest insulation is that they cut *against* their authors' product interest (Versor sells enhanced trend yet reports its decay; the micro-paper is CFM-affiliated yet argues CFM's product decayed), and the Aspect/Veritas rebuttals are from managers with no aligned enhanced product.[^versormannix]
- Skewness and $\beta_2$ are both statistically unstable - few-observation tail estimates - so every "convexity rose/fell with X" here is directional, not precise ([[skewness-in-asset-returns]]). The claim that our champion is intrinsically convex while equity-flat rests on a point estimate, and the decay monitor below exists precisely because the number is noisy.
- The micro-paper's small-tick/large-tick partition is a *futures* result; the campaign trades UCITS ETFs, where the tick structure and execution channel differ. The *direction* (fast/electronic trend decayed, slow/macro survived) transfers cleanly and matches our own fast-leg kill, but the exact mechanism does not map one-to-one onto an ETF book.
- "Convexity has retreated to the seat's corner" is a reassuring conclusion for a book already sitting in that corner; the risk of motivated reasoning is real, which is why the first hypothesis below is a *decay monitor* that could falsify it on our own substrate, not a restatement of the thesis.
