# What Makes a Trend Sleeve Convex

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> The long-gamma, positive-skew shape a trend sleeve is hired for is manufactured by speed, response-function design, and measurement horizon - not by breadth or instrument substrate; a slow, sign-based, Sharpe-or-UPI-ranked book realizes near-zero or negative skew, which is the configuration most large programs (and most naive backtests) silently drift into.

A trend sleeve earns its slot in a roster by being long-gamma: it should lose a little in calm markets and pay out in dislocation, the opposite skew to the carry pole it is paired with (the role taxonomy is in [[the-tiered-strategy-roster]] and [[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]]). The mistake this article is written against is assuming that *any* profitable trend rule delivers that shape. It does not. Convexity and positive skew are a specific, fragile property of *how* the rule is built and *at what horizon you measure it*, and the design choices that maximize a strategy's Sharpe ratio are very often the same ones that destroy its skew. A book can be a perfectly good Sharpe machine and a failed long-gamma sleeve at the same time.

## The shape is built-in, but only at the right horizon

Start with the mechanism, because it explains why the shape is both real and easy to lose. A single-asset trend strategy has built-in convexity: Bouchaud and co-authors show its P&L, aggregated over the trend filter's own timescale, equals the difference between a long-term realized variance and a short-term realized variance, so the payoff is proportional to the square of the past long-horizon return - a chi-squared variable with known positive skew.[^cfmtail] Strikingly, this holds *even if the trend has zero expected return*: positive skew is a property of the dynamic position-taking, not of whether the signal predicts anything.[^cfmtail] This is the same fact Fung and Hsieh captured by modelling the primitive trend strategy as a lookback straddle.[^funghsieh] Richard Martin makes the design dependence explicit: the skewness of a momentum strategy's returns is "a product of the design of the strategy", in contrast to long-only equities where negative skew is an intrinsic property of the asset.[^martin] A recent practitioner decomposition makes the option analogy operational rather than metaphorical: trend delta-replicates a straddle *struck at inception*, paying for its convexity as quadratic variation - slippage banked at every failed reversal - rather than as the time decay an outright option pays, and because the strike is fixed at entry it sidesteps the maturity-seasoning that erodes a rolled option's gamma.[^bhansaliopt]

The catch is in the words "aggregated over the trend filter's own timescale". The convexity only appears when the return-measurement period exceeds the half-life of the trend smoothing. Sepp shows that on the SG Trend index the positive skew and convexity show up at monthly, quarterly, and annual horizons but not below a month, and recommends evaluating a trend program on quarterly returns.[^sepp] Bouchaud makes the same point as a warning: a six-to-nine-month trend rebalanced weekly protects against moves unfolding over months, and is "just blind" to a crash that completes in fewer days than its rebalance interval.[^cfmtail] So the first design rule is a measurement rule: judge the shape at a horizon matched to the signal, and never expect a slow sleeve to show convexity in daily or weekly data.

## Speed is the master lever for skew

The most consistent, independently-replicated finding in the practitioner literature is that **skew falls as holding period rises** - the skew term structure slopes down. Carver demonstrates that trend rules have their advertised positive skew "only at the right time horizon": fast rules show it in weekly and monthly returns, but the slowest rules do not reveal positive skew until *annual* returns, and are persistently negative-skewed at shorter horizons.[^carver] Nilsson builds a clean test - 86 liquid futures, breakout lookbacks from 2 to 256 days - and finds the same downward-sloping term structure on both daily and monthly data, with the best monthly skew at a 4-day breakout and skew turning negative by the 64-day horizon; longer-term streams have realized increasingly negative skew since roughly 2015.[^nilsson] An older managed-futures decomposition reaches the matching conclusion from the other direction: the two sources of positive skew in the industry are *shorter holding periods* and *trading the short side*, while long-horizon and long-only trades carry near-zero skew.[^liftingveil]

This is not a free recommendation to go fast. Versor documents that the long-term signals favored by the largest CTAs "display very little evidence of positive convexity" and that the industry's convexity has *declined over time*, precisely because scale and cost pressure pushed managers toward slow signals.[^versor] But speeding up has a cost wall of its own: short-term trend's edge is "extremely sensitive to transaction costs, market impact, and execution quality", evaporating without specialized execution,[^molyboga] and the very fastest trends (three-day) have decayed since 1990 while slow trends have not.[^cfm2c] The reconciliation that the survivors actually run is *medium* speed: a reverse-engineering of Mulvaney's 25-year, ~20%-per-year program fits a ~120-to-140-day lookback - not slow, not ultra-fast - with a realized trade-level skew near +6 and a ~35% win rate.[^mulvaney] The lesson is that speed is the lever, the objective sets the direction (faster for skew), and cost sets the floor.

## The response function manufactures the right tail

Given a speed, the mapping from signal to position size decides how much of the convexity survives. Three common Sharpe-improving steps each shrink it, and the same CFM study quantifies all three: capping the position once a trend is established (it "reduces the size of the fat tails"), replacing a proportional response with the *sign* of the signal, and adding an extra layer of smoothing to cut turnover - each lifts risk-adjusted return "at the expense of convexity".[^cfmconvex] Martin's algebra agrees that the binary plus-one/minus-one transform is non-optimal and can drive skewness to zero or negative.[^martin] Research Affiliates frames the whole construction as a Sharpe-versus-skew trade-off and shows a sigmoid (magnitude) response carries materially higher positive skew than a sign rule, especially at shorter lookbacks; they also surface a non-obvious interaction - a long lookback paired with a *short* (3-month) volatility-estimation window "all but negates any positive skew", while a longer (2-year) volatility window restores skew near +0.8.[^ratradeoffs] Going the other way, deliberately *adding* to a winning position (pyramiding) pushes the right tail out further.[^nilsson][^mulvaney]

So the response-function recipe for the shape is: proportional or saturating (not sign), uncapped or capped only loosely, a volatility window long relative to the trend lookback, and ideally pyramiding into established trends. Every one of these trades away some Sharpe ratio for skew - which is only the wrong trade if you forgot the sleeve was hired for skew.

## Rank for the shape, not for the Sharpe ratio

The two rules above imply a third that is easy to violate in an optimizer. If convexity lives at a quarterly-or-slower measurement horizon, and if the response choices that maximize skew cost Sharpe, then **ranking candidate configurations by a Sharpe-like objective systematically selects the least convex book**. Research Affiliates state this as a design fact - maximizing one of {Sharpe, skew} comes at the cost of the other - and disclose that they themselves "lean towards trading off Sharpe ratio for more positive skew" when the mandate is protection.[^ratradeoffs] A selection metric built on the drawdown path (Sharpe, Calmar, Ulcer Performance Index) is dominated by calm-market smoothness and will reliably prefer the slow, sign-based, capped configuration that the previous two sections identified as skew-dead. If the sleeve's job is convexity, the held-out objective has to be realized skew, a convexity beta, or crisis-conditional return measured at the signal's horizon (the measurement machinery is in [[measuring-crisis-alpha]]).

## The convexity is beta-timing, not breadth

A natural assumption - and one easy to over-weight - is that a trend sleeve's protective shape comes from holding many independent markets so that gains in trending markets offset the crisis market. Fidelity's decomposition argues the opposite: roughly **76% of a trend strategy's risk is beta-timing** (the blunt "are equities rising or falling" directional call), and that beta-timing component has a higher Sharpe ratio, larger negative downside correlation with equities, and higher conditional Sharpe when stocks fall than the cross-sectional market-selection component.[^fidelity] Their headline result is that you can replace the trend-based relative-value engine entirely with a different, higher-Sharpe relative-value strategy and keep most of the crisis alpha, as long as the overall beta profile is preserved.[^fidelity] Independent practitioner coverage of the same work concludes the "blunt instrument" does the heavy lifting and market selection is "historically less powerful" for crisis protection.[^croce]

This reframes breadth. More independent markets raise the *Sharpe ratio* of the sleeve and reduce its dependence on any single bet - a real benefit, and the right answer to a robustness question - but they are not the source of the convex *shape*. The shape comes from timing a few large macro betas well, which is why a thin book can still be convex and a wide book can fail to be. Breadth and convexity are answers to different questions, and conflating them sends you optimizing the wrong axis.

An independent decomposition from a different manager reaches the same place by a different route and discharges much of the single-study risk. Quantica splits CTA returns into alpha plus a time-varying equity beta quarter by quarter and finds that *negative crisis beta was positive in fifteen of the sixteen worst equity quarters since 2000, accounting for more than 40% of trend performance in those windows*, with positive-or-negative equity beta explaining more than 80% of total trend performance.[^quanticacb] The constructive implication is the one the response-function sections already point at: if the convex shape is mostly well-timed beta, it is *manufactured by signal speed* and reproducible without breadth - and equally, it is *destroyed* by any response choice that mutes the beta-timing, which is exactly what slow-plus-sign-plus-cap does.

## Trend is the cheapest convex shape, and the least reliable per event

Finally, the honest limit of what a trend sleeve can promise. Trend is duration-dependent crisis protection: across a long crisis sample it delivered positive returns in every crisis that unfolded over more than three months, but failed in two of three crises that completed in under three months, including March 2020.[^quantdecoded] Man frames the same fact through skew - over a three-month horizon trend is positively skewed because positions have time to react, but for a day-scale shock it is "a lot like the toss of a coin"; faster signals improve crisis responsiveness at the cost of long-term return.[^manskew] Kaminski's metaphor is that trend is a "second responder", adapting slowly then powerfully once a new regime establishes.[^kaminski] And crisis alpha is "an empirical observation, not a guarantee": a stress-test of a CTA proxy through a re-run of the 2007-08 credit crisis loses money in every scrambled path, and the industry itself is split, with AHL comfortable using the term and Winton's founder pointedly not.[^neuron]

What trend uniquely offers is that this convexity comes at *near-zero or even positive* long-run carry, unlike options, VIX futures, or tail-risk funds, which bleed 2-to-5% per year fighting the equity and volatility risk premia.[^quantdecoded][^aqrtail][^manconvex] A one-month straddle is more convex than trend, but at a persistent premium cost; over a three-month horizon trend delivers better returns in both extreme tails *and* a positive average return.[^manconvex] The practical implication for a roster is division of labor: trend is the standing, low-cost, slow-crisis convex engine, and fast-crash protection belongs to an episodic tail sleeve ([[the-ucits-constrained-tail-sleeve]]), not to over-speeding the trend pole until its costs eat it.

## Limitations

- Skewness is statistically unstable by construction - it measures the tail of a distribution from few observations - so all the "skew rises/falls with X" findings carry wide error bars, a caveat the response-function source states explicitly.[^ratradeoffs] Treat skew rankings as directional, not precise.
- Several load-bearing sources have a conflict of interest (CFM, Man, AQR, Versor, Fidelity all run or sell trend or convexity products). The speed-skew term structure is the best-insulated claim, confirmed by an independent blogger and a buy-side researcher with no aligned product.[^carver][^nilsson]
- The "slow trend is negative-skewed" result is partly horizon-dependent (it inverts at annual measurement) and partly recent (post-2015 deterioration); it is a statement about the *measured* shape at usable horizons, not a claim that slow trend has no economic value.[^carver][^nilsson]
- The beta-timing-dominates-breadth result now rests on two independent managers reaching it by different decompositions - Fidelity's ~76% beta-timing share and Quantica's >80% equity-beta share - which materially reduces the single-study risk the first draft flagged; both still carry product COI, and neither is a peer-reviewed replication of the other.[^fidelity][^croce][^quanticacb]

## Strategy hypotheses this could seed

- [ ] Re-specifying a trend sleeve *for the shape* - medium/fast lookback, magnitude or saturating response (not sign), uncapped, a volatility window long relative to the lookback, ranked on held-out realized skew or convexity beta at a quarterly horizon - restores positive skew on a book that the slow / sign / buffered / Sharpe-ranked configuration leaves at ~0 or negative. Pre-register: held-out quarterly skew > 0 and convexity beta above the current config.
- [x] Pyramiding into established winners raises right-tail skew versus a fixed-size inverse-volatility position at matched gross exposure. **Supported (directional), bounded to short ramps - 2026-06-17** ([[2026-06-17]]): built `atalanta.trendStraddleBufferedPyramid` (gross floats up as trends mature over `ramp_steps` bars; ramp 1 == the constant-gross champion). On the 20-name London ETF book, full L/S, skew-ranked, held-out is an **inverted-U peaking at ramp ~21** (~1 month): skew +0.54->+0.65 AND Sharpe +0.18->+0.31 vs the no-pyramid baseline, at flat drawdown - a Pareto win. Past ~21 it **over-de-risks** (under-invested -> return/Sharpe fall *below* baseline, only drawdown improves). So "add to winners" deepens the tail *only at short ramp*; long ramps starve exposure. Open: does ramp~21 help the **long-only** champion (live sleeve, held-out 0.89)?
- [ ] The short side contributes most of a trend sleeve's positive skew; a long-only trend leg realizes near-zero skew (ties into the borrowable-universe constraint in [[the-orthogonal-non-equity-trend-universe]]).
- [ ] Crisis alpha survives replacing the cross-sectional market-selection component with a higher-Sharpe relative-value signal while preserving the beta-timing core - Fidelity's claim, tested on our universe.
- [ ] A trend sleeve sized for slow-crisis convexity plus a small episodic tail overlay dominates an over-sped trend sleeve on a convexity-per-cost basis (pairs with [[the-ucits-constrained-tail-sleeve]]).
