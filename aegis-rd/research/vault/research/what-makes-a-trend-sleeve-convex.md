# What Makes a Trend Sleeve Convex

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> The long-gamma, positive-skew shape a trend sleeve is hired for is manufactured by speed, response-function design, and measurement horizon - not by breadth or instrument substrate - and it is paid for by structurally price-insensitive counterparties (central banks, hedgers, mechanical rebalancers) whose flows will not arbitrage away. A slow, sign-based, Sharpe-or-UPI-ranked book realizes near-zero or negative skew, which is the configuration most large programs (and most naive backtests) silently drift into; and since any genuine timing edge on Gaussian returns must be positively skewed, a positive-Sharpe candidate without own-horizon skew is either shape-destroyed by design or regime luck.

A trend sleeve earns its slot in a roster by being long-gamma: it should lose a little in calm markets and pay out in dislocation, the opposite skew to the carry pole it is paired with (the role taxonomy is in [[the-tiered-strategy-roster]] and [[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]]). The mistake this article is written against is assuming that *any* profitable trend rule delivers that shape. It does not. Convexity and positive skew are a specific, fragile property of *how* the rule is built and *at what horizon you measure it*, and the design choices that maximize a strategy's Sharpe ratio are very often the same ones that destroy its skew. A book can be a perfectly good Sharpe machine and a failed long-gamma sleeve at the same time.

## The shape is built-in, but only at the right horizon

Start with the mechanism, because it explains why the shape is both real and easy to lose. A single-asset trend strategy has built-in convexity: Bouchaud and co-authors show its P&L, aggregated over the trend filter's own timescale, equals the difference between a long-term realized variance and a short-term realized variance, so the payoff is proportional to the square of the past long-horizon return - a chi-squared variable with known positive skew.[^cfmtail] Strikingly, this holds *even if the trend has zero expected return*: positive skew is a property of the dynamic position-taking, not of whether the signal predicts anything.[^cfmtail] This is the same fact Fung and Hsieh captured by modelling the primitive trend strategy as a lookback straddle.[^funghsieh] Richard Martin makes the design dependence explicit: the skewness of a momentum strategy's returns is "a product of the design of the strategy", in contrast to long-only equities where negative skew is an intrinsic property of the asset.[^martin] Firoozye and Koshiyama close the loop at theorem grade: assuming Gaussian returns and any linear-filter signal (moving averages, EWMAs, ARIMA forecasts), the strategy's first four moments have closed forms, and positive skewness and excess kurtosis fall out as *essential components of every positive-Sharpe dynamic strategy* - a rule that genuinely times anything must be right-tailed, so a timing strategy without positive skew at the matched horizon is inconsistent with having an edge.[^fk] A recent practitioner decomposition makes the option analogy operational rather than metaphorical: trend delta-replicates a straddle *struck at inception*, paying for its convexity as quadratic variation - slippage banked at every failed reversal - rather than as the time decay an outright option pays, and because the strike is fixed at entry it sidesteps the maturity-seasoning that erodes a rolled option's gamma.[^bhansaliopt]

The catch is in the words "aggregated over the trend filter's own timescale". The convexity only appears when the return-measurement period exceeds the half-life of the trend smoothing. Sepp shows that on the SG Trend index the positive skew and convexity show up at monthly, quarterly, and annual horizons but not below a month, and recommends evaluating a trend program on quarterly returns.[^sepp] Bouchaud makes the same point as a warning: a six-to-nine-month trend rebalanced weekly protects against moves unfolding over months, and is "just blind" to a crash that completes in fewer days than its rebalance interval.[^cfmtail] So the first design rule is a measurement rule: judge the shape at a horizon matched to the signal, and never expect a slow sleeve to show convexity in daily or weekly data.

## Who pays for the shape, and why they keep paying

A convex payoff with positive expectancy should not survive in equilibrium unless someone is durably on the other side, so the design argument needs its economics. The trend itself is seeded by underreaction: anchoring, the disposition effect (selling winners early slows the adjustment to good news; clinging to losers slows the adjustment to bad), and the slow diffusion of information across investors all make prices incorporate news too gradually, and Hong and Stein's model shows that once univariate momentum traders arbitrage that drift they generate the delayed overreaction that extends it.[^hongstein][^hop] The fingerprint of this two-phase anatomy is in the data: time-series momentum profits partially reverse after roughly a year - the underreaction leg of the trend is permanent price discovery and does not reverse, the herding leg does.[^hop]

The durability comes from who the standing counterparties are. Central banks smoothing exchange rates and yields, corporate hedging programs that buy strength and sell weakness in their exposures, and mechanical rebalancers - a 60/40 investor sells equities into every rally by construction - are all *structurally* trading against the trend, paying the premium as a cost of a mandate rather than a bet they expect to win.[^hop] A price-insensitive counterparty is the strongest answer to "why is it not arbitraged away". A newer microstructural account adds a self-reinforcing channel on top: trend followers' own aggressive flow, mediated by price impact, sustains the nascent trends the next round of signals reads, a loop that coexists with the information-diffusion channels and amplifies them - and whose breakdown on small-tick contracts after the HFT market-making transition is precisely the structural fast-trend death documented in [[is-the-trend-premium-decaying]].[^kurthloop]

There is a third leg, and it is the one this article's design argument turns into a feature: the shape protects itself. A positively-skewed stream loses more often than it wins and spends long stretches in drawdown before an unpredictable acceleration pays for everything - CFM calls it a performance chaser's nightmare, sold after the inevitable disappointment and re-bought after the spike has passed.[^fatright] Institutions selected on smooth Sharpe ratios systematically under-hold such streams, which limits the capital that can crowd the anomaly. The inference that the psychological cost of the shape is part of why the shape persists is our synthesis, not a tested claim - but it is consistent with the skew-premium line of evidence that markets *pay* for accepting negative skew and charge for holding positive skew ([[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]]).

## Speed is the master lever for skew

The most consistent, independently-replicated finding in the practitioner literature is that **skew falls as holding period rises** - the skew term structure slopes down. Carver demonstrates that trend rules have their advertised positive skew "only at the right time horizon": fast rules show it in weekly and monthly returns, but the slowest rules do not reveal positive skew until *annual* returns, and are persistently negative-skewed at shorter horizons.[^carver] Nilsson builds a clean test - 86 liquid futures, breakout lookbacks from 2 to 256 days - and finds the same downward-sloping term structure on both daily and monthly data, with the best monthly skew at a 4-day breakout and skew turning negative by the 64-day horizon; longer-term streams have realized increasingly negative skew since roughly 2015.[^nilsson] An older managed-futures decomposition reaches the matching conclusion from the other direction: the two sources of positive skew in the industry are *shorter holding periods* and *trading the short side*, while long-horizon and long-only trades carry near-zero skew.[^liftingveil] And the term structure has a 56-year precedent that predates every "post-2015 decay" narrative: on 1960-2015 monthly data across bonds, commodities, currencies and equities, positive skew concentrates in strategies built on the most recent four months of returns and is much lower or negative at intermediate lags, while the intermediate 5-to-8-month lag band underperformed *consistently, over time and across asset classes* - the medium-horizon deadzone is a structural feature of the premium, not a recent development.[^hrvh]

This is not a free recommendation to go fast. Versor documents that the long-term signals favored by the largest CTAs "display very little evidence of positive convexity" and that the industry's convexity has *declined over time*, precisely because scale and cost pressure pushed managers toward slow signals.[^versor] But speeding up has a cost wall of its own: short-term trend's edge is "extremely sensitive to transaction costs, market impact, and execution quality", evaporating without specialized execution,[^molyboga] and the very fastest trends (three-day) have decayed since 1990 while slow trends have not.[^cfm2c] The reconciliation that the survivors actually run is *medium* speed: a reverse-engineering of Mulvaney's 25-year, ~20%-per-year program fits a ~120-to-140-day lookback - not slow, not ultra-fast - with a realized trade-level skew near +6 and a ~35% win rate.[^mulvaney] The lesson is that speed is the lever, the objective sets the direction (faster for skew), and cost sets the floor.

## The response function manufactures the right tail

Given a speed, the mapping from signal to position size decides how much of the convexity survives. Three common Sharpe-improving steps each shrink it, and the same CFM study quantifies all three: capping the position once a trend is established (it "reduces the size of the fat tails"), replacing a proportional response with the *sign* of the signal, and adding an extra layer of smoothing to cut turnover - each lifts risk-adjusted return "at the expense of convexity".[^cfmconvex] Martin's algebra agrees that the binary plus-one/minus-one transform is non-optimal and can drive skewness to zero or negative.[^martin] Research Affiliates frames the whole construction as a Sharpe-versus-skew trade-off and shows a sigmoid (magnitude) response carries materially higher positive skew than a sign rule, especially at shorter lookbacks; they also surface a non-obvious interaction - a long lookback paired with a *short* (3-month) volatility-estimation window "all but negates any positive skew", while a longer (2-year) volatility window restores skew near +0.8.[^ratradeoffs] Going the other way, deliberately *adding* to a winning position (pyramiding) pushes the right tail out further.[^nilsson][^mulvaney]

A blunter lever operates on the position's admissible sign rather than its size. Forbidding long positions in the crisis-reference asset (a no-long-equity or no-long-bond restriction) mechanically deepens crisis-conditional return, and the 1960-2015 evidence quantifies the price: the restriction improves the worst-quintile payoff while reducing the average return *and* weakening the protection in the other asset class - a no-long-equity book does worse in bond crises, because the long-equity position it forgoes was part of what paid there.[^hrvh] Sign restriction is therefore a real shape lever, but one that trades diversified crisis coverage for concentrated crisis depth.

So the response-function recipe for the shape is: proportional or saturating (not sign), uncapped or capped only loosely, a volatility window long relative to the trend lookback, and ideally pyramiding into established trends. Every one of these trades away some Sharpe ratio for skew - which is only the wrong trade if you forgot the sleeve was hired for skew.

## Rank for the shape, not for the Sharpe ratio

The two rules above imply a third that is easy to violate in an optimizer. If convexity lives at a quarterly-or-slower measurement horizon, and if the response choices that maximize skew cost Sharpe, then **ranking candidate configurations by a Sharpe-like objective systematically selects the least convex book**. Research Affiliates state this as a design fact - maximizing one of {Sharpe, skew} comes at the cost of the other - and disclose that they themselves "lean towards trading off Sharpe ratio for more positive skew" when the mandate is protection.[^ratradeoffs] A selection metric built on the drawdown path (Sharpe, Calmar, Ulcer Performance Index) is dominated by calm-market smoothness and will reliably prefer the slow, sign-based, capped configuration that the previous two sections identified as skew-dead. If the sleeve's job is convexity, the held-out objective has to be realized skew, a convexity beta, or crisis-conditional return measured at the signal's horizon (the measurement machinery is in [[measuring-crisis-alpha]]).

The Gaussian-returns theorem sharpens this into a diagnostic. Since every positive-Sharpe linear dynamic strategy on Gaussian returns *must* carry positive skew and excess kurtosis, a sweep whose winners show zero or negative skew at the matched horizon admits only two readings: the design (sign response, caps, symmetric vol targeting) destroyed the shape the signal generated, or there was never a timing edge and the Sharpe is regime luck.[^fk] Either reading disqualifies the candidate for a convexity seat, which makes measured own-horizon skew not just a preference but a consistency check on the whole sweep.

## Benchmark convexity is not intrinsic convexity

"Trend convexity is decaying" is a claim about a specific measurement, and conflating it with the intrinsic shape this article defines causes real confusion. Versor's widely-cited finding regresses SG Trend index returns on the S&P 500 and its square over rolling five-year windows and finds the index's convexity to equities fell from nearly 2 in the years after 2008 to zero or below after about 2014, turning negative for the long-term signals that now dominate large CTAs.[^versor] That is a real result, but it is *benchmark* convexity - a Treynor-Mazuy-style $\beta_2$ against equities, the signature used in [[verifying-strategy-family-membership]] - not the *intrinsic* convexity this article has been building up from the CFM decomposition,[^cfmtail] which is a strategy's own-horizon skew, measured against its own trend timescale rather than against an external index. The two need not move together. Versor's own explanation for the benchmark-convexity decline is capacity, not signal death: trend AUM grew roughly 50% after 2008, and to absorb it managers migrated to slower signals that trade less and carry less benchmark convexity - "it's not that the signals have changed their stripes, the managers have."[^versormannix] That is a reversible design choice by an industry chasing scale, not evidence the underlying long-gamma mechanism stopped working. And a diversified CTA loses some benchmark convexity almost mechanically: it trades many contracts against a single equity reference, so the equity-$\beta_2$ understates the protection while the own-horizon skew this article ranks on is preserved.[^cfmtail]

There is even a live disagreement about which speed is more convex, and it resolves the same way. Versor's realized regression finds short signals more convex than long over the 2010s;[^versor] Roncalli and Jusselin's option-profile algebra finds the opposite - the theoretical convexity of the trend payoff *rises* with the moving-average duration, so long-term rules should be more convex.[^roncalli] Both are right about different objects: the theoretical payoff is more convex the slower you go, but the realized benchmark-convexity of slow signals in the 2010s was low simply because the slow timeframe was rarely *called upon* - there was no sustained divergence lasting more than a handful of months in that decade, and in a genuine grinding bear the slow signals would be expected to out-convex the fast.[^versormannix] Environmental starvation of a slow signal and structural decay of a fast one are different failure modes, and only the fast, small-tick one is irreversible.

The practical conclusion is the one the previous section already reaches by a different route: rank a trend sleeve on its own-horizon skew or convexity payoff, not on its $\beta_2$ to equities. A sleeve can be flat against the benchmark and still be a genuine long-gamma stream.

## The convexity is beta-timing, not breadth

A natural assumption - and one easy to over-weight - is that a trend sleeve's protective shape comes from holding many independent markets so that gains in trending markets offset the crisis market. Fidelity's decomposition argues the opposite: roughly **76% of a trend strategy's risk is beta-timing** (the blunt "are equities rising or falling" directional call), and that beta-timing component has a higher Sharpe ratio, larger negative downside correlation with equities, and higher conditional Sharpe when stocks fall than the cross-sectional market-selection component.[^fidelity] Their headline result is that you can replace the trend-based relative-value engine entirely with a different, higher-Sharpe relative-value strategy and keep most of the crisis alpha, as long as the overall beta profile is preserved.[^fidelity] Independent practitioner coverage of the same work concludes the "blunt instrument" does the heavy lifting and market selection is "historically less powerful" for crisis protection.[^croce]

This reframes breadth. More independent markets raise the *Sharpe ratio* of the sleeve and reduce its dependence on any single bet - a real benefit, and the right answer to a robustness question - but they are not the source of the convex *shape*. The shape comes from timing a few large macro betas well, which is why a thin book can still be convex and a wide book can fail to be. Breadth and convexity are answers to different questions, and conflating them sends you optimizing the wrong axis.

An independent decomposition from a different manager reaches the same place by a different route and discharges much of the single-study risk. Quantica splits CTA returns into alpha plus a time-varying equity beta quarter by quarter and finds that *negative crisis beta was positive in fifteen of the sixteen worst equity quarters since 2000, accounting for more than 40% of trend performance in those windows*, with positive-or-negative equity beta explaining more than 80% of total trend performance.[^quanticacb] The constructive implication is the one the response-function sections already point at: if the convex shape is mostly well-timed beta, it is *manufactured by signal speed* and reproducible without breadth - and equally, it is *destroyed* by any response choice that mutes the beta-timing, which is exactly what slow-plus-sign-plus-cap does.

There is a sharp corollary for signal construction. Levine-Pedersen prove the OLS trend regression is mathematically equivalent to time-series momentum, moving-average crossover, and the Kalman filter - it *is* the state of the art for the signal form, not a naive placeholder academia has since improved on.[^whichtrend] Graham's cross-speed anatomy reaches the same equivalence from the construction side: moving-average crossovers and channel breakouts alike reduce to weighted sums of past returns, so systems that look structurally different are highly correlated once their speeds match, and the parameterization that sets the speed is the only design choice doing real work.[^grahamspeed] The "better" formulas the literature offers on top of it - significance gates, fractional-difference reweighting, faster speeds - are not different signals but the same signal plus a bolt-on, and each one's benefit is a breadth-averaging effect: it works by covering a false read on one name with a true read on another across a wide cross-section. On a thin, low-count book there is no cross-section left to average across, so the bolt-on's cost (lag, added turnover, or both) survives without its compensating benefit, and the raw signal dominates. The lesson is not that the literature's refinements are wrong; it is that the signal-form levers that manufacture convexity mostly require breadth a thin substrate does not have (see the hypotheses below).

## Trend is the cheapest convex shape, and the least reliable per event

Finally, the honest limit of what a trend sleeve can promise. Trend is duration-dependent crisis protection: across a long crisis sample it delivered positive returns in every crisis that unfolded over more than three months, but failed in two of three crises that completed in under three months, including March 2020.[^quantdecoded] Man frames the same fact through skew - over a three-month horizon trend is positively skewed because positions have time to react, but for a day-scale shock it is "a lot like the toss of a coin"; faster signals improve crisis responsiveness at the cost of long-term return.[^manskew] February 2018 is the coin landing wrong: the SG CTA index lost 6.3% in a month where the S&P 500 lost 3.9%, because an instantaneous jump gives even the fastest signal no time to reposition - no design choice inside the trend family fixes a shock that completes inside the rebalance interval.[^fatright]

What the duration-dependence does *not* mean is that the protection is equity-referenced only. On 1960-2015 data the trend payoff shows a *bond smile* even more pronounced than the familiar equity smile: the strategy paid in the worst bond quarters as well as the best, and performed consistently through the pre-1985 rate bear market - a 25-year regime with no bond tailwind at all.[^hrvh] For an allocator whose duration hedge fails exactly in inflation-and-rates shocks, this is the material claim: a slow trend sleeve is the one convex payoff with documented crisis behavior on *both* the growth-shock and rate-shock axes, which no static haven can offer ([[havens-at-regime-turning-points]]). Kaminski's metaphor is that trend is a "second responder", adapting slowly then powerfully once a new regime establishes.[^kaminski] And crisis alpha is "an empirical observation, not a guarantee": a stress-test of a CTA proxy through a re-run of the 2007-08 credit crisis loses money in every scrambled path, and the industry itself is split, with AHL comfortable using the term and Winton's founder pointedly not.[^neuron]

What trend uniquely offers is that this convexity comes at *near-zero or even positive* long-run carry, unlike options, VIX futures, or tail-risk funds, which bleed 2-to-5% per year fighting the equity and volatility risk premia.[^quantdecoded][^aqrtail][^manconvex] A one-month straddle is more convex than trend, but at a persistent premium cost; over a three-month horizon trend delivers better returns in both extreme tails *and* a positive average return.[^manconvex] The practical implication for a roster is division of labor: trend is the standing, low-cost, slow-crisis convex engine, and fast-crash protection belongs to an episodic tail sleeve ([[the-ucits-constrained-tail-sleeve]]), not to over-speeding the trend pole until its costs eat it.

The final cost is not financial but institutional, and it belongs in the design because it decides whether the shape is ever collected. A stream that loses more often than it wins, spends years between paydays, and delivers its value in bursts no one times is exactly the stream an allocator reviewing quarterly performance abandons at the bottom - and the payoff distribution guarantees the abandonment happens before the acceleration that justified the sleeve.[^fatright] Manufacturing the convexity and then governing the allocation like a Sharpe product is the institutional version of buying the straddle and selling it at the low: the last design lever is a pre-commitment to hold the sleeve at stable weight through its inevitable droughts.

## Limitations

- Skewness is statistically unstable by construction - it measures the tail of a distribution from few observations ([[skewness-in-asset-returns]] surveys the moment and why it is the least stable number on the page) - so all the "skew rises/falls with X" findings carry wide error bars, a caveat the response-function source states explicitly.[^ratradeoffs] Treat skew rankings as directional, not precise.
- Several load-bearing sources have a conflict of interest (CFM, Man, AQR, Versor, Fidelity all run or sell trend or convexity products). The speed-skew term structure is the best-insulated claim, confirmed by an independent blogger and a buy-side researcher with no aligned product.[^carver][^nilsson]
- The "slow trend is negative-skewed" result is partly horizon-dependent (it inverts at annual measurement) and partly recent (post-2015 deterioration); it is a statement about the *measured* shape at usable horizons, not a claim that slow trend has no economic value.[^carver][^nilsson]
- Versor's "trend convexity has declined" and this article's "the shape is built-in but fragile to design" are about *different measurements*, not a contradiction - see "Benchmark convexity is not intrinsic convexity" above. A sleeve can read equity-$\beta_2$-flat under [[verifying-strategy-family-membership]]'s convexity-horizon test and still carry a strongly positive own-horizon skew; the industry-wide "decay" commentators cite is largely the benchmark axis, not the one this article's ranker prices.
- The beta-timing-dominates-breadth result now rests on two independent managers reaching it by different decompositions - Fidelity's ~76% beta-timing share and Quantica's >80% equity-beta share - which materially reduces the single-study risk the first draft flagged; both still carry product COI, and neither is a peer-reviewed replication of the other.[^fidelity][^croce][^quanticacb]
- The Firoozye-Koshiyama theorem assumes Gaussian returns and *linear* signals; real returns are fat-tailed and a sign or saturating response is nonlinear, so the "positive Sharpe implies positive skew" diagnostic is exact only for the idealized case and directional outside it. The authors' own extension list (normalized signals, nonlinear transforms) is precisely the gap.[^fk]
- The who-pays synthesis stacks a peer-reviewed underreaction model, a practitioner lifecycle account, and one microstructural study; each leg is sourced, but the composite - and especially the claim that the shape's holding cost protects it from crowding - is our own inference, untested and probably untestable on our data.
- The bond-smile evidence is monthly, gross of costs, partly proxy-built before futures existed, and comes from a single manager with a trend product (Man AHL); no independent replication of the *bond* smile specifically surfaced in the sweep, so treat its magnitude (not its existence) as soft.[^hrvh]

## Strategy hypotheses this could seed

- [ ] Re-specifying a trend sleeve *for the shape* - medium/fast lookback, magnitude or saturating response (not sign), uncapped, a volatility window long relative to the lookback, ranked on held-out realized skew or convexity beta at a quarterly horizon - restores positive skew on a book that the slow / sign / buffered / Sharpe-ranked configuration leaves at ~0 or negative. Pre-register: held-out quarterly skew > 0 and convexity beta above the current config.
- [x] **Pyramiding into established winners raises right-tail skew versus a fixed-size position at matched gross exposure.** **Settled, book-dependent (2026-06-17 to 2026-07-07)** ([[runs/atalanta/2026-06-17|2026-06-17]], [[runs/atalanta/2026-07-03|2026-07-03]], [[runs/atalanta/2026-07-06|2026-07-06]], [[runs/atalanta/2026-07-07|2026-07-07]]): the lever is real and transfers - Pareto-positive on a broad 20-name L/S book, and financed add-on tranches roughly double held-out convexity on a thin 3-name champion book - but on the thin book convexity and drawdown ride the same ray: every dose that clears the roster's survivability bar reverts to champion-level convexity. Verdict: book-specific; the live lever on a thin book is breadth, not a different ramp.
- [ ] The short side contributes most of a trend sleeve's positive skew; a long-only trend leg realizes near-zero skew (ties into the borrowable-universe constraint in [[the-orthogonal-non-equity-trend-universe]]).
- [ ] Crisis alpha survives replacing the cross-sectional market-selection component with a higher-Sharpe relative-value signal while preserving the beta-timing core - Fidelity's claim, tested on our universe.
- [ ] A trend sleeve sized for slow-crisis convexity plus a small episodic tail overlay dominates an over-sped trend sleeve on a convexity-per-cost basis (pairs with [[the-ucits-constrained-tail-sleeve]]).
- [ ] A non-positive position restriction on the crisis-reference legs (no-long-equity / no-long-duration inside the trend sleeve) deepens held-out crisis-conditional return at a measurable cost in average return and cross-asset protection - replicating the 1960-2015 sign-restriction trade-off on a small book, and quantifying whether the concentrated crisis depth is worth the forgone diversification.[^hrvh]
- [ ] Held-out realized skew at the matched horizon, used as a *consistency gate* rather than a ranker (drop candidates whose positive Sharpe comes with non-positive own-horizon skew), removes more regime-luck candidates than a deflated-Sharpe gate at equal pass rates - the testable form of the Gaussian-returns diagnostic.[^fk]
