# Skewness in Asset Returns

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> Skewness is the third moment - the asymmetry that mean-variance discards - and finance cares about it because it is priced twice over: as a *risk* you are paid to bear (systematic coskewness) and as a *preference* you overpay to indulge (idiosyncratic, lottery-like skew). But its sign is not a property of "returns": it flips with the level of aggregation (single names positive, the index negative), with a strategy's horizon and rebalancing (trend positive, carry negative), and it is estimated from the handful of tail observations that make it the least stable number on the page. The systematic-skew premium survives out of sample, but mainly at short horizons and with a magnitude that moves materially under reasonable research choices. The useful question is never "are returns skewed" but "whose returns, aggregated how, measured over what horizon" - which is exactly why skewness is both the most economically loaded higher moment and the one most likely to lie to a backtest.

## What skewness measures

Skewness is the third standardized moment of a return distribution, the average cubed deviation from the mean divided by the cube of the standard deviation:

$$\gamma = \frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(r-\mu)^3\right]}{\sigma^3}$$

The cube preserves sign, so a positive $\gamma$ means the distribution has a long right tail (frequent small losses, occasional large gains - a lottery ticket), and a negative $\gamma$ a long left tail (frequent small gains, occasional large losses - a written insurance contract). A symmetric distribution, the Gaussian included, has $\gamma = 0$; mean-variance analysis assumes exactly this and therefore throws the number away. Everything below is the case for picking it back up.

The first-principles reason skewness belongs in asset pricing is that a rational, risk-averse investor is not indifferent to it. Scott and Horvath prove the direction of the preference from utility axioms alone: any investor with positive marginal utility of wealth, consistent (decreasing-absolute) risk aversion at all wealth levels, and a stable ordering of moment preferences has a *positive* preference for positive skewness (and for every odd moment) and an aversion to variance (and to every even moment).[^scotthorvath] The preference for a long right tail is not a behavioral quirk; it falls out of the same concave, prudent utility that produces risk aversion in the first place. Arditti had already made the empirical case a decade earlier - investors are averse to variance and pay up for positive skew - and Kraus and Litzenberger turned it into an equilibrium model.[^arditti][^krauslitz] So the third moment is not a curiosity bolted onto mean-variance; it is the first thing mean-variance leaves out that a coherent investor actually trades against.

## Priced as a risk: systematic skewness

The first way skewness earns a price is as non-diversifiable risk. Kraus and Litzenberger extended the Sharpe-Lintner CAPM to three moments and showed that what markets pay for is not an asset's *total* skewness but its *systematic* skewness - its coskewness with the market portfolio, the part that survives diversification.[^krauslitz] An asset whose returns fall hardest exactly when the market's own distribution is most left-skewed makes a diversified portfolio more crash-prone, so investors demand a higher expected return to hold it; an asset that adds positive skew to the market portfolio is worth overpaying for. In their tests the price of systematic skewness carries the predicted sign and the security-market-line intercept collapses to the riskless rate, repairing anomalies earlier work had blamed on borrowing constraints.[^krauslitz]

Harvey and Siddique made the estimate conditional and put a number on it: systematic (conditional) coskewness commands a risk premium averaging about 3.60% per year, and it remains significant after controlling for size and book-to-market.[^harveysiddique] Their result also quietly reframes a famous anomaly - momentum's winners-minus-losers portfolio has *negative* coskewness (its crashes coincide with the market's), so part of the momentum premium is compensation for bearing systematic skewness rather than free alpha.[^harveysiddique] That thread runs straight into the roster's convexity axis: a strategy paid to be short crash-skew is being paid for a risk, and one paid to be long it is buying protection ([[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]]). The negative skew of momentum outside equities is examined directly in [[momentum-crashes-outside-equities]].

Twenty-five years of follow-up evidence preserve the sign but narrow the confidence interval around the story, not around one number. Independent replications put the premium near 2.6-3.2% per year in revised samples, while Harvey and Siddique's own original-construction out-of-sample estimate is 2.0%; another international replication finds 1.4% in the United States and 3.4% in developed markets outside it, but no effect in emerging markets.[^hs25] Reasonable choices of weighting, estimation window, missing-data tolerance and portfolio breakpoints move reproduced estimates from roughly 2.1% to 3.9%.[^hs25] Systematic skewness is therefore a durable priced direction, not a universal 3.60% constant.

## Priced as a preference: idiosyncratic skewness

Classical theory says only systematic skew should be priced, because idiosyncratic skew diversifies away. The second, more surprising body of work shows that it is priced anyway - because real investors do not diversify it away. Barberis and Huang, building on the probability-weighting component of cumulative prospect theory, prove that a security's *own* skewness can be priced even when it is small and independent of everything else: an agent who overweights the tiny probabilities in the tail values a positively skewed security above its expected-utility price, so the security becomes overpriced and earns a *negative* average excess return.[^barberishuang] The prediction is the exact opposite of the risk story - here more positive skew means *lower* returns - and it holds at zero coskewness, which is what makes it a distinct channel rather than a restatement of Kraus-Litzenberger.

The empirical support is broad and, unusually, cross-asset. Boyer, Mitton and Vorkink model each stock's *expected* idiosyncratic skewness (lagged skew alone forecasts poorly) and find the low-expected-skew quintile beats the high-expected-skew quintile by 1.00% per month in Fama-French alpha; expected skew also absorbs much of the idiosyncratic-volatility puzzle.[^bmv] The lottery-proxy literature reaches the same place through a cruder signal: Bali, Cakici and Whitelaw's MAX (the prior month's single largest daily return) predicts a value-weighted high-minus-low return of about -1.03% per month, -1.18% in four-factor alpha.[^max] Amaya and co-authors push the horizon down to a week using intraday-estimated realized skewness and still find it: buying the lowest and shorting the highest realized-skewness decile earns roughly 19 basis points the next week (t = 3.70).[^amaya] The effect survives out of sample in markets with no shared microstructure - across roughly 47,000 stocks in 40-odd countries the MAX premium is significant worldwide and larger where investors are more individualistic,[^cheonlee] and in commodity futures a factor long the most negatively-skewed and short the most positively-skewed contracts earns about 8.01% per year (t = 3.83, Sharpe ~0.78), with skewness priced at roughly 5% per year.[^commodityskew]

Who is on the other side, and why they stay there, is the part a return claim has to answer. Kumar identifies the buyers directly: individual investors prefer lottery-like stocks, their demand rises in economic downturns exactly as state-lottery sales do, and it is concentrated among low-income, gambling-prone demographics - who, predictably, underperform for it.[^kumar] The premium is not arbitraged away because eliminating it means *shorting* the lottery, and short-sellers face impediments the long side does not; Stambaugh, Yu and Yuan formalize this arbitrage asymmetry and note the specific reason it bites hardest here - shorting a positively-skewed stock means carrying that stock's own right-tail risk (the short squeeze), so arbitrageurs demand a premium to take the trade, and the mispricing persists.[^stambaugh] The edge is behavioral in origin (a demand for skew) but structural in its persistence (an asymmetric limit to arbitrage). This is the cross-sectional face of the same premium the concave sleeve harvests in the time series ([[carry-as-the-short-gamma-income-pole]], [[what-makes-a-carry-sleeve-an-income-engine]]).

## The sign is not a property of "returns"

The single most useful fact about skewness is that "the skewness of stock returns" is an ill-posed quantity: the sign depends on what you aggregate. Individual stocks are *positively* skewed - most drift sideways while a few multiply - yet the market index built from them is reliably *negatively* skewed. Albuquerque resolves the apparent contradiction: aggregation does not average skew, it is dominated by *co-skewness* terms, and firm returns exhibit asymmetric correlations - a low return for one firm coincides with high volatility in the others - which shifts probability mass in the index toward the left tail and flips the sign.[^albuquerque] Negative index skew is therefore a cross-sectional phenomenon, not a scaled-up version of any single stock's shape.

The same level-dependence governs *strategy* returns, which is where it matters for a book. A strategy manufactures its own skew through position-taking, and the sign is a design choice, not an inheritance from the underlying. A trend follower holds a payoff that is structurally long-gamma and positively skewed, realized only when returns are measured over a horizon longer than the trend filter ([[what-makes-a-trend-sleeve-convex]]); a carry or short-volatility sleeve is structurally short-gamma and negatively skewed, but only in the asset classes where funding-unwind dynamics bite ([[carry-as-the-short-gamma-income-pole]]). Lempériere and co-authors show the whole cross-section of "risk premium" strategies lines up along the sign of skewness rather than volatility, which is why [[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]] treats skew, not mechanism, as the axis of diversification. The practical corollary: a sleeve's skew is a property of the *sleeve*, measured on its own return stream, and it need not resemble the skew of anything it holds.

## The horizon is part of the estimand

Aggregation over time is not a cosmetic sampling choice. Aretz and Arisoy construct return skewness at arbitrary horizons with a block-bootstrap estimator and find that the asset-pricing evidence is driven mainly by short-horizon physical skewness; common proxies differ because some forecast short-horizon skew and others long-horizon skew.[^aretzarisoy] Jin, Conlon and Cotter reach the sharper result for systematic skewness: the same asset can have positive coskewness at one horizon and negative coskewness at another, especially among small stocks, and coskewness is priced only over a narrow band of short horizons.[^jinconloncotter] A daily estimate cannot be relabeled as a monthly or quarterly property by multiplying it by a scaling constant.

This does not contradict a trend sleeve becoming positively skewed over quarters while looking flat or wrong-way over days. The two literatures ask different questions: the asset-pricing studies locate the horizon over which stock-level skew or coskewness forecasts cross-sectional returns, while a dynamic strategy manufactures an own-horizon payoff through repeated position changes. Together they impose a stricter measurement rule: state the return aggregation horizon, match it to the economic mechanism, and do not transfer either the sign or the price across horizons without testing it.

## Physical versus risk-neutral skewness

There are two skewness numbers for the same asset, and they disagree by a premium. The *physical* (realized) skew is the third moment of the returns that actually happened. The *risk-neutral* skew is the asymmetry embedded in option prices - what you would infer from the relative cost of out-of-the-money puts versus calls. Bakshi, Kapadia and Madan derive the laws connecting them and show two things that recur everywhere: risk aversion mechanically bakes negative skew into the risk-neutral density even when the physical distribution is milder, and the index's risk-neutral distribution is far more negatively skewed than any individual stock's, because index puts are bid up as crash insurance while single-name skew is diluted by idiosyncratic, often positive, components.[^bkm] The gap between the two - the market pays as if crashes are worse than they have been - is itself a harvestable premium and is the option-market sibling of the variance risk premium the concave pole already sells.

This is also where the evidence is genuinely mixed, and it is worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over. Signals built on *physical* skew - realized, historical, or modeled expected skew - deliver the negative skew-return relation with fair consistency (Kumar, Bali et al., Amaya et al., the commodity factor above). Signals built on *option-implied* skew do not agree on the sign: some studies find more negative implied skew predicts higher returns (consistent with the preference story), others find the opposite, and the survey in the commodity paper lists both camps.[^commodityskew] The disagreement is unresolved and probably reflects that implied skew mixes the physical asymmetry with a time-varying risk premium; a strategy that trades on it is exposed to whichever component dominates in its sample.

Even physical skew does not support every forecasting claim made in its name. Annaert, De Ceuster and Van Cappellen cannot replicate the claim that average individual-stock skewness predicts the next one or two months of euro-area stock-index or index-futures returns, including after changing estimators, restricting the sample to large liquid firms and substituting MAX as an idiosyncratic-skew proxy.[^annaert] That null concerns time-series prediction of the aggregate market, not the cross-sectional pricing of coskewness or lottery demand, so it does not overturn those results. It does rule out treating "skewness is priced" as permission to use any skew aggregate as a market-timing signal.

## Why it is hard to measure

Every claim above inherits one weakness: skewness is estimated almost entirely from the tails, which by definition contain few observations, so it is the least stable of the low-order moments. A sample third moment is dominated by its largest handful of deviations, cubed, and its standard error is large; a strategy's measured skew can and does flip sign across sub-samples that look otherwise similar. This is not a nuisance to footnote - it governs how skew can be used. A family taxonomy that classifies a sleeve by measuring the realized skew of its stream ([[verifying-strategy-family-membership]]) must use tail-aware, regime-conditional estimators rather than a single full-sample moment, and must judge the shape at a horizon matched to the mechanism, because the same stream reads as convex over quarters and flat over days ([[measuring-crisis-alpha]]). Block resampling belongs here because it preserves enough serial structure to estimate multi-period skew directly; in simulations, the horizon-specific block-bootstrap estimator beats competing realized-skew estimators.[^aretzarisoy] The honest position is that skew is the right economic quantity and a treacherous statistical one, and the response is measurement discipline (pre-declared horizons, robust estimators, block-bootstrap intervals), not a more precise point estimate.

## Limitations

- The two pricing stories - systematic skew as priced risk (Kraus-Litzenberger, Harvey-Siddique) and idiosyncratic skew as behavioral mispricing (Barberis-Huang, Boyer-Mitton-Vorkink) - are both well-supported and make *opposite* sign predictions for total skew, so which one dominates a given sample is an empirical question, not a settled one. A signal that does not separate the two channels is trading their net, which can switch sign across universes.[^harveysiddique][^barberishuang]
- The lottery/idiosyncratic-skew premium is strongest exactly where it is least tradable - small, illiquid, high-retail-ownership names - and thins out in the liquid large-caps and index instruments a systematic book actually trades; re-examinations under value-weighting find mispricing factors can subsume the MAX effect, and the underlying mechanism (prospect-theory demand vs simple overreaction) is itself disputed.[^max][^stambaugh] The commodity-futures result is the load-bearing evidence that the effect survives in a liquid, shortable, institutionally-traded market.[^commodityskew]
- Option-implied skewness does not agree with physical skewness on the sign of its return relation; the risk-neutral literature is genuinely split.[^commodityskew][^bkm]
- Almost all figures here are gross academic estimates subject to post-publication decay, and skew's own estimation instability means every "skew predicts X" magnitude should be read as directional, not precise.
- The systematic-skew premium survives post-publication, but its magnitude is research-design sensitive and its international reach is incomplete; emerging-market and long-horizon nulls prevent treating the three-moment CAPM as universal.[^hs25][^jinconloncotter]
- Average skewness fails as an aggregate market-return predictor in the euro-area replication. This limits a timing use of skew, not the separate cross-sectional claims about coskewness and lottery demand.[^annaert]
- The sources here are unusually free of conflict of interest (peer-reviewed asset-pricing journals), which is a strength; the exception is the practitioner-adjacent convexity framing that this article connects to, whose COI is documented in [[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]] and [[what-makes-a-trend-sleeve-convex]].

## Strategy hypotheses this could seed

- [ ] **A tail-aware skew estimator is the canonical family classifier.** A regime-conditional, block-bootstrapped skewness estimate measured at the mechanism's horizon separates our Candidates into long- and short-gamma poles more stably (fewer sign flips across held-out splits) than a naive full-sample third moment - making skew a trustworthy gate input for [[verifying-strategy-family-membership]] rather than a noisy one. Pre-register the horizon before looking at the sign and require the classification to survive adjacent aggregation horizons; a sign that exists at only one arbitrary frequency fails the gate.[^aretzarisoy][^jinconloncotter]
- [ ] **Sleeve skew is not instrument skew.** Measure both the per-instrument skew and the book-level realized skew of each sleeve and confirm the aggregation effect on our own universe: the sleeve's net skew (what the roster budgets) diverges from the average skew of its holdings because of asymmetric cross-instrument correlations, per [[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]]. If they coincide, the asymmetric-correlation channel is weak on this book and net-skew budgeting can be approximated cheaply.
- [ ] **Cross-sectional skew sort is a concave signal.** On the futures/ETF cross-section, a sleeve long the most negatively-skewed and short the most positively-skewed instruments (the commodity-futures result out of sample) earns positive held-out return *and* its own stream is negatively skewed - confirming it is concave-pole risk, not orthogonal alpha. Pre-register: held-out Sharpe > 0, sleeve quarterly skew < 0, and test whether it adds anything beyond carry where high-yield already implies low skew.[^commodityskew]
- [ ] **The physical-minus-risk-neutral skew gap is a distinct concave source.** Where option data is investable, the skewness risk premium (physical realized skew richer than option-implied) is a short-gamma income source separable from the variance risk premium already run as the concave pole - test whether it carries incremental crisis-conditional information at matched volatility, or is spanned by the VRP.[^bkm]
