# When Conditioning Pays

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> Making an allocation adaptive pays only when three conditions line up - the conditioning state is persistent relative to your rebalancing horizon, the conditioning variable maps cleanly onto the right weight without polluting the return forecast, and the cost of being wrong is symmetric - and when any one fails (transient states, noisy signals, or returns concentrated in the few periods you would sit out) adaptation reliably loses to the static rule out of sample; the three conditions are diagnosable in advance, which is more than the catalogue of timing studies usually admits.

The instinct to condition an allocation - turn it off in bad regimes, scale it by a signal, rotate into what is working - is so natural that the burden of proof has quietly inverted. The honest prior, given the out-of-sample record, runs the other way: most conditioning rules lose to their static benchmark after costs, and the ones that survive do so for identifiable structural reasons. This article assembles the failure cases and the survival cases on the same axes and extracts the principle that separates them. The campaign that motivates it learned one instance the hard way - a momentum gate that selects yesterday's regime and unwinds at the flip ([[relative-vs-absolute-momentum-in-small-universes]]) - but the question is general and the literature is genuinely split, so the synthesis matters more than the anecdote.

## The static benchmark is harder to beat than it looks

Start with the cleanest possible test, because it sets the bar. DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal evaluate fourteen optimizing portfolio rules - sample mean-variance, Bayes-Stein shrinkage, minimum-variance, and their constrained variants - against the naive 1/N rule that simply equal-weights everything and conditions on nothing.[^dgu] Across seven empirical datasets, *none of the fourteen is consistently better than 1/N* on out-of-sample Sharpe ratio, certainty-equivalent return, or turnover. The mechanism is estimation error, and they quantify it brutally: for a 25-asset universe calibrated to US equities, the sample mean-variance rule needs an estimation window of about 3,000 months to reliably beat 1/N out of sample; for 50 assets, about 6,000 months.[^dgu] Investors in practice use 120 months. The in-sample gap is real - on the S&P sectors dataset the in-sample mean-variance Sharpe is 0.3848 against 1/N's 0.1876, more than double - and it evaporates entirely once the moments have to be estimated from finite data going forward.[^dgu]

This is the foundational result for everything that follows, and it generalizes past portfolio optimization to any adaptive rule. Conditioning means estimating something - a regime probability, a volatility forecast, a momentum rank, an optimal weight - and acting on the estimate. The estimate carries error. The static rule carries none, because it estimates nothing. So adaptation only wins when the information it adds exceeds the error it introduces, and the DGU result says that crossover point sits much further out than intuition expects: the sophisticated rule has to be *right by a wide and stable margin* before the noise in its own inputs stops dominating. The practical corollary is that a regularized grid, a constrained optimizer, or a single robust default often lands in the same basin as the fancy version precisely because the search space was tamed - the fragility only bites when the rule is free to chase noise ([[measuring-crisis-alpha]]).

## Market timing: the asymmetry that raises the bar

The oldest quantitative treatment of conditioning is Sharpe's, and it isolated the part of the problem that the estimation-error literature misses. Sharpe asked how often a manager switching annually between stocks and cash must correctly call the year for timing to beat staying invested, and concluded that *unless you can tell a good year from a bad one at least seven times out of ten, you should not attempt it at all*; adjusting for the lower risk of the partly-in-cash timing portfolio, the break-even accuracy is around 74%.[^sharpe] Chua, Woodward and To reproduced and sharpened this with Monte Carlo on Canadian data and found the required accuracy is 70-80%, but their decomposition is the load-bearing insight: *forecasting bull markets correctly matters far more than forecasting bear markets*, because the returns that give equities their premium are concentrated in a small number of periods, and missing those while sitting in cash is the dominant cost.[^cwt] A buy-and-hold investor is, in their framing, a timer with 100% bull-market accuracy and 0% bear-market accuracy - and that turns out to be a hard configuration to beat. Being right 50% of the time underperforms holding even with *perfect* bear-market avoidance, because the bull misses swamp it.[^cwt]

The structural fact underneath is that equity returns are right-skewed and clustered, so the cost of being wrong is asymmetric. A more recent confirmation: the market's best and worst days cluster together - the median gap between a top-ten day and a bottom-ten day has been about seven days - so any rule trying to dodge the worst days courts a high probability of also missing the best, and the net edge from perfectly avoiding the ten worst days over two decades was only about 26 basis points a year over the penalty for missing the ten best.[^morningstar_days] The multiplicative structure makes it worse still: because returns compound, the most probable outcome of all-or-nothing timing is a *below-median* return even before costs - the timing return distribution is asymmetric in the wrong direction.[^aspara] This is the first leg of the principle. **Conditioning that involves stepping out of a positively-skewed, premium-bearing exposure faces an asymmetric penalty: the rule must be right not at 50% but at the 65-80% level just to break even**, and that threshold is a property of the return distribution, not of the cleverness of the signal.

## Regime switching: the right model can still forecast worse than a coin

If the asymmetry of returns sets the bar, regime-switching models show how a technically *correct* model can still fail to clear it. Dacco and Satchell asked why regime-switching models, which fit exchange-rate data beautifully in sample, are routinely beaten out of sample by a random walk - and proved analytically that for a simple two-state switching model, *only a small probability of misclassifying which regime you are in is enough to lose the entire advantage of knowing the true model*.[^daccosatchell] The damage is not symmetric around a good forecast: a wrong regime call does not merely produce a suboptimal allocation, it produces an allocation pointed in the *contrary* direction relative to the neutral single-state choice, so several correct calls are needed to repair the damage of one wrong one.[^vroomen][^daccosatchell] Bessec and Bouabdallah decomposed the forecast error of Markov-switching models across many specifications by Monte Carlo and found the same dominant term: the main source of error is misclassification of the future regime, not parameter estimation or anything else.[^bessec] This is exactly Dacco-Satchell's analytic result borne out across the model class.

The contrast with a more favorable case is instructive. Ang and Bekaert built regime-switching models on international equity returns and found that in an *all-equity* global allocation the regimes are hard to exploit - the optimal portfolio barely changes across regimes, so knowing the regime buys you little, and the benefits of plain international diversification dominate the cost of ignoring the regimes entirely.[^angbekaert] But when the investor can move between cash, bonds and equity, regime switching *does* add out-of-sample value, for a precise reason: persistent bear-market regimes (high volatility, high correlations) coincide with periods of relatively high interest rates, so the model tells the investor to switch primarily into cash - and cash is then actually attractive.[^angbekaert] Conditioning paid in the second case and not the first because of two things that recur throughout this article: the bear regime was *persistent* (so a correct call stayed correct long enough to matter), and the conditioning variable mapped onto a *large and genuinely better* allocation difference (all-the-way to cash, well rewarded). When the conditioned-on state is transient or the implied weight change is small, even a correct model is not worth the misclassification risk.

## Volatility management: persistence and clean mapping in, structural breaks out

The volatility-managed portfolio is the sharpest natural experiment for the principle because the same rule looks like a triumph in one study and a failure in the next, and the difference is exactly the diagnostic. Moreira and Muir scale each factor's exposure by the inverse of its recent realized variance and report large alphas and Sharpe gains in sample across the market, value, momentum, profitability and carry factors.[^moreiramuir] Their own stated mechanism is the cleanest possible illustration of when conditioning *should* work: volatility is highly persistent and forecastable, and - critically - volatility *does not forecast returns*, so a spike in volatility signals a deteriorated risk-return tradeoff that you can lean against without contaminating any return forecast.[^moreiramuir] The conditioning variable (vol) maps onto the optimal weight (1/vol) with no cross-talk. They even exploit a *differential* persistence: volatility mean-reverts fast while expected returns drift slowly, so the optimal rule de-risks hard on the variance spike and then rebuilds exposure over roughly eighteen months as vol fades but the elevated expected return lingers.[^moreiramuir] On paper this is the textbook case: persistent state, clean mapping, symmetric enough payoff.

Then Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang and Yang ran it out of sample across 103 equity strategies for a real-time investor and the edge largely disappeared.[^cowy] The volatility-managed portfolios show the positive in-sample alphas Moreira-Muir reported, but the trading strategies implied by those spanning regressions *are not implementable in real time*, and reasonable out-of-sample versions generally earn lower certainty-equivalent returns and Sharpe ratios than simply holding the unmanaged portfolio.[^cowy] Their managed market portfolio earns an out-of-sample Sharpe of 0.42 against 0.46 for the unmanaged one.[^cowy] The diagnosis is specific and it is the one that matters: the poor out-of-sample performance stems primarily from *structural instability in the spanning regressions* - the parameters a real-time investor estimates from past data fail to predict the future managed-versus-unmanaged relationship.[^cowy] In other words, the conditioning relationship that looked stationary in the full-sample fit was not stationary going forward. The earlier vault treatment from the trend side reaches the compatible conclusion that vol-scaling is a drawdown-control tool, not an alpha source, and that the Harvey et al. evidence already restricts even the drawdown benefit to equity-like assets ([[trend-following-in-whipsaw-regimes]]).[^harvey] The instructive part is that vol management satisfied two of the three conditions - persistent state, clean variable-to-weight map - and still failed on the third, stationarity of the conditioning relationship. **Persistence and clean mapping are necessary but not sufficient; the conditioning relationship itself must hold up out of sample.**

## The survival case: trend-following and why it is the exception

Set against this graveyard, time-series momentum is the conditioning rule with the strongest out-of-sample record, and understanding *why* it survives completes the principle. Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen documented that an asset's own past 12-month return positively predicts its next-month return across 58 futures and forwards spanning equities, bonds, currencies and commodities, with the effect persisting about a year before partially reversing.[^mop] The genuinely out-of-sample test came later: Hurst, Ooi and Pedersen extended a simple trend rule back to 1880 across 67 markets and found positive average returns in *every decade since 1880* and in 8 of the 10 worst drawdowns for a 60/40 portfolio - performance that holds across recessions, wars, and high- and low-inflation regimes.[^century] This is the kind of cross-regime, cross-market, multi-century stability that the regime-switching and vol-managed rules conspicuously lack.

Trend survives because it satisfies all three conditions, and one of them in a way the others cannot. First, the conditioning state is genuinely persistent: the positive return autocorrelation at the one-to-twelve-month horizon is the documented source of the profit, and it is what the regime-switchers wish they had.[^mop] Second, the variable maps cleanly to the position - the sign of the trend *is* the position, with no intervening estimated optimal weight to corrupt. Third and decisively, **the cost of being wrong is bounded by construction**. A long-only or long-flat trend rule that misclassifies the regime simply exits or fails to enter; it does not, like the regime-switching allocator, take a position pointed in the contrary direction. The asymmetric penalty that Sharpe and Chua-Woodward-To identified - missing the bull - is real for trend too and shows up as the whipsaw bill in trendless years, but the convexity that the same rule buys in sustained moves is the compensating payoff ([[trend-following-in-whipsaw-regimes]] treats this as the structural premium, not a fixable defect, and [[measuring-crisis-alpha]] treats the crisis-window gain as the thing worth measuring). Even here the conditioning is fragile in the specific way the principle predicts: trend's weak years are caused by the *absence of large moves*, a state no filter can convert into signal, which is why adding a regime filter on top of trend reliably fails ([[trend-following-in-whipsaw-regimes]]).

One sharpening for long-only books. The cash gate of time-series momentum is the conditioning, and cross-sectional rank selection on top of it is a *second* layer of conditioning with a much weaker case - it adds asset-picking estimation error without adding crash protection, and at small N inside a correlated block the rank carries little information ([[relative-vs-absolute-momentum-in-small-universes]]). Layering conditioning multiplies the estimation-error and misclassification costs while the persistent-state benefit does not stack the same way. The principle is not "conditioning good, static bad" but a budget: each adaptive layer must clear its own version of the three-condition test.

## The principle, and the diagnostics

The four cases line up on one frame. Conditioning beats static out of sample when, and only when, three conditions hold jointly:

1. **State persistence relative to the acting horizon.** The conditioned-on state must last long enough, relative to how often you rebalance and how laggy your signal is, that a correct classification stays correct long enough to pay for the switch. Volatility and trend persist (vol mean-reverts over weeks, trend over months); exchange-rate regimes and equity bull/bear calls at annual frequency do not persist relative to the noise in classifying them. Ang-Bekaert's regimes paid only where they were persistent enough to act on.[^angbekaert][^moreiramuir]
2. **Clean variable-to-weight mapping.** The conditioning variable must map onto the right position without polluting the return forecast. Volatility works as a denominator precisely because it does not forecast returns; an optimizer that conditions weights on estimated expected returns fails because those estimates are the noisiest input there is.[^moreiramuir][^dgu]
3. **Bounded, symmetric cost of being wrong.** A misclassification must cost roughly what a correct call earns. Trend (exit on a wrong call) and vol-scaling (de-risk on a wrong call) are bounded; a regime allocator that flips contrary to neutral, and an all-or-nothing equity timer that sits out a clustered bull, face the asymmetric penalty that pushes the break-even hit rate to 70-80%.[^sharpe][^cwt][^daccosatchell]

A fourth condition is implicit in COWY and worth stating separately: **the conditioning relationship must be stationary out of sample** - a rule can pass the first three and still fail because the in-sample mapping breaks via structural instability.[^cowy] This is the hardest to verify in advance and the one that turns paper alphas into live losses.

These are diagnosable before deployment, which is the article's actual claim. Persistence is measurable: estimate the autocorrelation or AR(1) half-life of the conditioning variable and compare it to the signal lag plus rebalance interval; if the state half-life is shorter than the time it takes you to detect and act, the rule is a noise amplifier - which is exactly why coincident detection triggers (fast-vol throttles, gap triggers) fail on daily bars, arriving after the state they meant to catch has passed ([[detecting-fast-crashes-from-price]] documents the post-shock-arrival failure directly). Mapping cleanliness is testable: regress forward returns on the conditioning variable; if the variable predicts returns (not just risk), you have cross-talk and the simple inverse mapping is wrong. Cost asymmetry is structural: write down what a wrong call does to the position and check whether it merely flattens exposure (bounded) or reverses it (unbounded). And stationarity is testable in the only honest way - rolling out-of-sample, with the deflation discipline that single-window crisis statistics demand ([[measuring-crisis-alpha]]); the Garleanu-Pedersen result that slower-decaying signals deserve more weight and faster ones less is the formal version of grading a signal by its persistence before trusting it.[^garleanu]

## Limitations

- The unifying three-(or four-)condition principle is our synthesis across four literatures, not a single published theorem. Each condition is individually well-sourced (persistence: Moreira-Muir, Ang-Bekaert; mapping: Moreira-Muir, DGU; asymmetry: Sharpe, Chua-Woodward-To, Dacco-Satchell; stationarity: COWY), but the claim that *these and only these* govern when conditioning pays is a conjecture that fits the cases assembled here and could be falsified by a counterexample - a rule that passes all four and still loses, or fails one and still wins robustly.
- The market-timing threshold literature (Sharpe; Chua-Woodward-To) is all-or-nothing annual switching on a single risky asset; the 70-80% break-even does not transfer mechanically to partial scaling, multi-asset, or higher-frequency rules, where the asymmetry is muted by never fully exiting. Confidence on the *direction* (asymmetry raises the bar) is high; on the exact numbers for other designs, low.
- Trend-following's strong record carries a survivorship and COI caveat: the century studies are largely produced by firms that run trend funds, and the with-leverage, with-shorts futures construction differs from a long-only ETF book where the convexity is documented to be weaker at daily/weekly frequency ([[what-makes-a-trend-sleeve-convex]]).[^century]
- Moreira-Muir versus COWY is a live, unresolved disagreement in the literature about whether vol management adds out-of-sample value; this article sides with the OOS critique on the strength of the structural-break diagnosis, but the original authors and others dispute the implementation choices in the OOS tests. Both are cited.
