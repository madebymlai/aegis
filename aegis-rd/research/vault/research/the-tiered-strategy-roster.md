# The Tiered Strategy Roster: Floor, Target, Expansion

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> The strategies an allocator needs are not a count, they are an order of operations: cover both signs of skew (floor - trend ⊕ carry/mean-reversion), split the convex pole by speed (target - add tail/defensive), then add an off-axis stream (expansion - add market-neutral). Each tier fixes a failure the previous one cannot.

## The question is order, not count

"How many strategies does an allocator need" is the wrong question. It invites a list of mechanisms and a book that looks varied while behaving as one position. The right question is which *failure modes* the roster must cover and in what order, because each strategy earns its slot by surviving a regime the others do not.

That reframing comes straight from the convexity axis: a strategy's diversifying value is set by the sign and speed of its convexity to the shocks that move all risk assets at once, not by its signal logic. The full argument is in [[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]]; this note turns that axis into a build order. A family is a target a candidate is built toward and then *verified* against, on its realized stream - the gates for that are in [[verifying-strategy-family-membership]].

## Floor: both signs of skew

The irreducible minimum is two roles of opposite convexity: a trend/momentum sleeve (long gamma, positive skew) and a carry or mean-reversion sleeve (short gamma, negative skew). The division of labor is exact. The short-gamma sleeve is the income engine - it collects the premium that structurally accrues to sellers of crash risk and compounds it through calm markets. The long-gamma sleeve is the drawdown payer - it bleeds a little in calm and pays out in dislocation, buying the income engine time to recover. The clearest statement of the pairing is empirical: across twenty markets and four asset classes from 1960 to 2014, carry and trend are mutually diversifying, and most sharply *in the extreme states*, because carry harvests a premium that pays when prices sit still while trend is a long-tail option that pays when they move.[^carrytrend] The two are not competing guesses about the same thing; they are opposite sides of one insurance contract, and holding both is what turns a set of tickers into a portfolio.

The gain is not a portfolio-theory abstraction. Combining even the two simplest versions - a currency momentum sleeve and a currency carry sleeve, equal-weighted - lifted the Sharpe ratio from 0.79 and 0.63 for the sleeves alone to 0.98, and improved the Calmar ratio by 71% and 289% over momentum and carry respectively, with the sleeves' low correlation doing most of the work.[^olszewski] Holding either alone forgoes that entire cross-term. And a book of several short-gamma strategies dressed as different mechanisms is not diversified at all - it is one position wearing several tickers, and it learns as much only in the single regime where every insurance seller is hit at once.

The trend pole carries a substrate requirement that is easy to miss: its long gamma is to *its own* universe's moves, so the universe must be one whose moves are *sustained trends*, not reversals. The century of evidence is specific here - time-series momentum has earned positive returns in every decade since 1880 and performed best precisely in *low-correlation* environments, where a cross-asset trend axis is separable from any single market.[^century] An equity-dominated book fails that test: its move-magnitude is nearly collinear with equities, so there is no separable trend axis, and a lagged signal is wrong-footed on every fast equity reversal, realizing *short* gamma under a trend label. A non-equity book (rates, FX, commodities) decorrelates the trend axis from equities and restores the long-gamma profile, which is why managed-futures programs are built on broad cross-asset futures rather than equity baskets, and why trend's "crisis alpha" - its positive returns in the large, drawn-out drawdowns - comes from fast de-risking across a diversified universe, not from any single hedge.[^century][^crisisalpha] The substrate is therefore not merely "wide" but "non-equity-dominated": breadth that only adds correlated equity sectors dilutes the convex tail rather than building it.

Institutional fund-of-funds allocators reach the same floor under different names: divergent (convex - trend, macro) strategies supply diversification in stress, convergent (concave - relative-value, carry) strategies supply the calm-market carry.[^bf][^jai] The recurring construction error is to fill both slots from the *same* pole - a roster whose two "diversifiers" are a trend follower and a crash hedge owns two long-gamma sleeves and no income, and the two bleed together through every quiet, range-bound year. The missing piece is almost always the concave sleeve, precisely because it is the one that quietly loses in the dislocations the convex sleeve is bought for. It is also the harder pole to source: where a genuinely negative-skew carry cannot be found net of costs, the evidence favors tilting toward a trend that minimizes its own negative carry over manufacturing a weak income sleeve.[^carrytrend] That constraint - and the related finding that the concave pole buys calm-market income efficiency more than extra crisis protection - is where the floor's validation effort concentrates, and it is tested directly below.

## Target: split the convex pole by speed

The third role is tail/defensive, and it is not a new direction on the axis - it is the convex pole split by *speed*. Trend is lagged long gamma: a move must establish before the strategy is positioned, so it harvests protracted bear markets (the dot-com grind) and misses sudden gaps (1987, March 2020). A tail sleeve is immediate long gamma for exactly the crash trend arrives too late for. AQR's comparison of index puts against trend draws the same line - puts returned over 40% in the fast COVID crash, trend did its best work in drawn-out bears - and because the slow drawdowns do more lasting damage to compounding, trend is the workhorse and tail the supplement.[^aqrtail]

That immediacy is not free, and its cost decides how the tail is sized. A standing crash-insurance position pays the full premium every calm day - the negative carry is the price of the immediacy.[^aqrtail] That bleed is large and well-measured: index put protection is expensive even when option prices look low, and a naive full-time protective-put program has historically delivered *worse* drawdown-per-unit-return than simply holding less of the risk asset - except in the one case that defines this tier, the sudden one-day gap, where option convexity is uniquely effective.[^israelov] So the tail is not held as a constant allocation, and not timed on a view; it is held as a *cost budget*. Fix the annual premium the book will pay for a target amount of coverage at a defined stress attachment (say a -20% move), rank candidate convex legs by crisis-payoff-per-unit-carry, fill to the coverage target under that premium ceiling, and revisit the budget on a slow cadence. Trend remains the *standing* engine beneath it, because it has a round-trip recovery advantage the option leg lacks - it participates in the rebound and keeps its drawdown gains, where rolled puts decay and expire worthless if the crash does not come.[^aqrtail] And the coverage is best sourced from a convex anomaly - a haven that pays in stress without the structural bleed - over rented volatility whose decay *is* the premium: the instrument-level trade-off and the UCITS-constrained operational build are in [[the-ucits-constrained-tail-sleeve]], and havens' regime-dependence in [[havens-at-regime-turning-points]].

The two convex mechanisms are complementary, not redundant: a peer-reviewed study combining a slow trend overlay with an option-based tail hedge via portable alpha improved performance in all nine crisis episodes tested, adding roughly 0.25% per month after factor controls.[^schwalbach] What turns a bleeding hedge into a contributor is not clever timing but *monetization* - selling the convex leg into the spike and buying risk at the trough - and a fixed-weight, continuously rebalanced sleeve performs that rule automatically, which is why the cost budget is paired with disciplined rebalancing rather than a discretionary trigger. The tempting alternative is to size the tail *episodically* - up when a stress regime looks likely, down otherwise - and regime-parity gives that idea a principled form: equal-risk-contribution weights computed conditional on each regime and weighted by the regime's steady-state probability, a construction with genuine peer-reviewed support.[^lombardodier][^costakwon] The roster does not adopt it for the tail. Episodic sizing needs a live regime signal that *leads* the shock, and stress triggers computable from daily data tend to fire after the gap they are meant to pre-position for (see Limitations), so the executable version collapses back to the small standing sleeve the rebalance already monetizes. Regime-parity stays the contested alternative - defensible for a sleeve slow enough that a stable regime signal can drive it, but not for the tail, whose triggers arrive too late to act on.

## Expansion: add an off-axis stream

Market-neutral and relative-value are the fourth tier because they sit *off* the skew axis entirely - near-zero net gamma, low correlation to either pole, returns driven by cross-sectional dispersion rather than by which regime the market is in. They are the diversifier of diversifiers, and they belong last for two reasons. They add the least to a book that has not yet covered both signs and both speeds. And they are the most sensitive to breadth: a ranking strategy needs a wide cross-section to separate winners from losers, and a thin universe starves it. Ilmanen's case for harvesting many low-correlated premia in parallel is the steady state this tier aims at, but only once the floor and target are in place.[^ilm]

## Why this order and not another

Each tier answers a failure the one below cannot. The floor fixes the worst one - a single-sign book that is undiversified by construction. The target fixes the floor's blind spot - the fast crash the lagged convex sleeve sleeps through. The expansion fixes the residual - dependence on regime itself - by adding a stream that does not care about regime. Adding the tiers out of order buys little: a tail overlay on a single-sign book still has no income, and a market-neutral sleeve on a book with no convexity still has nothing to pay it in a crash.

There is a fourth failure the order exists to avoid, and it is the one most allocators actually commit: the *over-diversified book*. More strategies is not more diversification - an allocator with three long-gamma sleeves and no short-gamma one is less diversified than one holding a single matched pair - and the fund-of-funds evidence shows the failure is active, not passive. Past roughly twenty underlying funds the variance-reduction from adding names stalls, and beyond that over-diversification *raises* left-tail risk while lowering returns, because each added sleeve piles on correlated crash exposure and a due-diligence cost but no new independent bet.[^overdiv] Stacking same-axis strategies manufactures precisely the hidden, synchronized short gamma the roster is built to avoid. A roster grows safely only by adding an axis it does not already span - which is what each tier, and only a tier, does.

## The upgrade path

A taxonomy that cannot grow without breaking its contract is a dead end. The roster scales along three horizons, none of which touches the return-stream contract the allocator consumes, so each is additive.

First, decompose sub-roles inside the existing families before adding new ones. The trend sleeve is the natural first candidate, but the robust decomposition is not the obvious fast/slow barbell: a fast leg re-imports the very whipsaw the sleeve exists to ride through. What survives instead is an *ensemble of adjacent slow lookbacks* that diversifies lookback specification-luck into distinct Locked Candidates without adding a fast leg. And for the drawdown-payer role specifically, the higher-value lever is not a new lookback at all but a speed-neutral *turnover buffer* - a no-trade band that Pareto-improves the single-lookback champion on Sharpe, crisis capture, and drawdown at once. The horizon trade-offs behind both are in [[trend-following-in-whipsaw-regimes]] and [[relative-vs-absolute-momentum-in-small-universes]].

Second, widen the asset universe along orthogonal, non-equity axes. Going from the current book to a larger set of independent macro drivers - the rate curve, real rates, currencies, and broad and single commodities - raises the number of *independent* bets, not merely the nominal count, the lever that most directly lifts a small book's risk-adjusted ceiling. Adding correlated equity exposure would raise the count while diluting the convex tail (the substrate requirement above); the construction that actually adds breadth, and the point where it saturates, are in [[the-orthogonal-non-equity-trend-universe]].

Third, add a fifth family only when a genuinely new payoff shape is available. The natural candidate is volatility-arbitrage or liquidity-provision: a near-zero-beta sleeve that harvests the volatility risk premium by selling implied and buying realized protection - a convex/concave hybrid that sits apart from the four regime roles (the VRP mechanics are in [[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]]).

## Breadth and capacity

Two numbers bound the roster: how wide it can be across families, and how deep within one.

Breadth is richer than the asset count suggests. By Grinold's Fundamental Law, risk-adjusted skill scales with the square root of the number of independent bets, $IR \approx IC \times \sqrt{BR}$.[^grinold] A naive reading caps a ten-instrument universe at ten bets, but breadth is set by *independent* bets, not nominal assets, and that distinction has a formal measure. Meucci's effective number of bets is the exponential entropy of a portfolio's uncorrelated-factor risk distribution: one when a single principal portfolio carries all the risk, rising toward the factor count only as risk spreads across genuinely uncorrelated sources.[^meucci] Choueifaty's diversification ratio recovers the same quantity by another route - its square is the number of independent risk factors a portfolio effectively spans.[^choueifaty] Under either measure, highly correlated assets collapse to a few effective bets while near-orthogonal cross-asset ETFs preserve them; one practitioner estimate puts the effective bets in a ten-ETF cross-asset book near 6.8, far above what a same-size single-sector book would deliver.[^resolve] This matters most where the roster needs it: the effective number of bets predicts realized performance specifically in bear markets, so orthogonal breadth is not a fair-weather nicety but the quantity that pays in the stress the roster is built for.[^enbstress] It softens, without removing, the breadth limit on the market-neutral expansion tier - the universe is thin in names but not in orthogonal risk.

Depth within a family is capped by redundancy. Adding strategies helps only while they stay uncorrelated; the marginal variance reduction from the $M$-th strategy in a family of uniform pairwise correlation $\rho$ is

$$\frac{\partial \sigma_p^2}{\partial M} = -\frac{\sigma^2(1-\rho)}{M^2}$$

which decays as $1/M^2$. At a redundancy ceiling of $\rho \approx 0.65$ the benefit is meaningful from one strategy to two, modest from two to three, and negligible past three - the within-family echo of the over-diversified book above, now as a numeric cap. A workable default follows: cap a family at roughly three active, non-redundant Locked Candidates, and enforce a within-family correlation ceiling (realized-return $\rho \lesssim 0.65$ over a rolling year) above which the lower-Sharpe candidate is retired or merged. These specific cut points are engineering defaults, not laws.

## Limitations

Tail's tier rank is genuinely contested, on two axes. On *whether* to hold one: AQR's own reading leans against a standing tail sleeve at all, on the grounds that its bleed costs more than the slow-drawdown protection trend already supplies.[^aqrtail] On *how to size* it: the roster holds a standing cost-budgeted sleeve monetized by rebalancing, but the episodic / regime-parity alternative[^lombardodier][^costakwon] remains a live research question, demoted here because its size-up triggers fired too late on our daily data, not because the evidence for regime-conditioning is weak. Treat "tail = target tier, cost-budgeted" as the working stance, not a settled one, and let the convexity-per-bleed evidence decide per universe.

The expansion tier is breadth-limited, though less than the raw count implies. Cross-sectional market-neutral wants many names to rank, and a ten-instrument book is thin on that axis - but its near-orthogonal cross-asset structure preserves an estimated ~6.8 independent bets (see Breadth and capacity), so the fourth tier is constrained, not foreclosed. Whether it earns its slot at this universe size is an empirical question, not a foregone one.

The floor's concave sleeve is the hardest to source, and the whole roster rests on it. If carry and mean-reversion cannot be made to pay net of costs in a given universe, the floor degrades to a single sign and loses its foundation. So the floor's feasibility, not the higher tiers, is where validation effort belongs first.
