# The Tiered Strategy Roster: Floor, Target, Expansion

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> The strategies an allocator needs are not a count but an order of operations: pair a convergent ordinary-market engine ⊕ a persistent-crisis responder, add immediate defense for the responder's speed gap, then add off-axis alpha. Carry and mean reversion are candidates for the convergent role, not requirements; negative skew is an implementation property, not the role's definition. Each tier fixes a failure the previous one cannot.

## The question is order, not count

"How many strategies does an allocator need" is the wrong question. It invites a list of mechanisms and a book that looks varied while behaving as one position. The right question is which *failure modes* the roster must cover and in what order, because each strategy earns its slot by surviving a regime the others do not.

That reframing comes straight from the convexity axis: a strategy's diversifying value is set by the sign and speed of its convexity to the shocks that move all risk assets at once, not by its signal logic. The full argument is in [[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]]; this note turns that axis into a build order. A family is a target a candidate is built toward and then *verified* against, on its realized stream - the gates for that are in [[verifying-strategy-family-membership]].

The resulting roster is a job specification, not a shopping list. “Strongest benchmark” means the cleanest institutional expression supported by the research, not a universal implementation or a particular investment vehicle.

| Job | Strongest institutional benchmark | Purpose | Payoff and principal failure | Qualification test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Convergent income engine** | Diversified long/short convergence premia; cross-asset carry is the best-documented generic benchmark[^carry] | Earn a positive full-cycle return in ordinary markets and finance defense | Construction-specific, not inherently short gamma; fails when convergence breaks or common recession, liquidity and volatility stress dominates | Positive net premium, an understood convergence-break state and positive marginal whole-book utility |
| **Persistent-crisis responder** | Broad, volatility-scaled multi-asset time-series trend[^tsmom] | Adapt after a directional dislocation persists | Straddle-like exposure to large moves; vulnerable at turning points, sharp reversals and range-bound whipsaw | Genuine cross-market breadth and reliable response to persistent dislocations after turnover and whipsaw costs |
| **Immediate defense** | `DAR4020`-like defensive factor selection; puts only for residual gap-speed or contractual-convexity needs[^baltussen] | Respond before trend has repositioned | DAR supplies pre-existing negative beta, not contractual convexity; puts supply impact convexity with persistent premium drag | Improves early-drawdown outcomes net of ordinary-market drag and adds protection not already supplied by trend |
| **Off-axis alpha ensemble** | Diversified beta-neutral value, cross-sectional momentum, quality and defensive factors[^globalfactors] | Earn from cross-sectional dispersion without duplicating the other jobs | Neither convergent nor convex by definition; crowding, reversal, value traps and costs remain factor-specific failures | Low residual dependence on the first three jobs, enough independent breadth and positive return after implementation costs |

Two distinctions in the table are load-bearing. First, “convergent” describes the economic job, not a guaranteed skew sign: Koijen et al.'s diversified global carry factor has little aggregate skew even though common recession, liquidity and volatility shocks still matter.[^carry] A candidate earns the slot from its mechanism and marginal contribution to the paired book, not merely from looking negatively skewed. Second, immediate defense is not synonymous with convexity. `DAR4020` arrives with a defensive factor portfolio and negative beta, while trend becomes more useful as a drawdown persists; explicit options remain the clean residual tool when contractual gap-speed convexity is actually required.[^baltussen]

## Floor: ordinary markets plus persistent crises

The irreducible minimum is two economic jobs: a **convergent income engine** that compounds a compensated premium through ordinary markets, and a trend/momentum sleeve that adapts when a directional crisis persists. Opposite convexity is the strongest documented implementation of that floor, not its definition. Carry, mean reversion, liquidity provision, relative value and defined-risk volatility selling are candidate convergent mechanisms; many are short gamma and negatively skewed, but none qualifies from its label or skew alone. The clearest evidence for one implementation is the carry-trend pairing: across twenty markets and four asset classes from 1960 to 2014, carry and trend are mutually diversifying, and most sharply *in the extreme states*, because carry harvests a premium that pays when prices sit still while trend is a long-tail option that pays when they move.[^carrytrend] The two jobs are not competing guesses about the same thing; one funds the book in ordinary markets and the other responds to a persistent dislocation.

The gain is not a portfolio-theory abstraction. Combining even the two simplest versions - a currency momentum sleeve and a currency carry sleeve, equal-weighted - lifted the Sharpe ratio from 0.79 and 0.63 for the sleeves alone to 0.98, and improved the Calmar ratio by 71% and 289% over momentum and carry respectively, with the sleeves' low correlation doing most of the work.[^olszewski] Holding either alone forgoes that entire cross-term. And a book of several short-gamma strategies dressed as different mechanisms is not diversified at all - it is one position wearing several tickers, and it learns as much only in the single regime where every insurance seller is hit at once.

The trend pole carries a substrate requirement that is easy to miss: its long gamma is to *its own* universe's moves, so the universe must be one whose moves are *sustained trends*, not reversals. The century of evidence is specific here - time-series momentum has earned positive returns in every decade since 1880 and performed best precisely in *low-correlation* environments, where a cross-asset trend axis is separable from any single market.[^century] An equity-dominated book fails that test: its move-magnitude is nearly collinear with equities, so there is no separable trend axis, and a lagged signal is wrong-footed on every fast equity reversal, realizing *short* gamma under a trend label. A non-equity book (rates, FX, commodities) decorrelates the trend axis from equities and restores the long-gamma profile, which is why managed-futures programs are built on broad cross-asset futures rather than equity baskets, and why trend's "crisis alpha" - its positive returns in the large, drawn-out drawdowns - comes from fast de-risking across a diversified universe, not from any single hedge.[^century][^crisisalpha] The substrate is therefore not merely "wide" but "non-equity-dominated": breadth that only adds correlated equity sectors dilutes the convex tail rather than building it.

Institutional fund-of-funds allocators reach the same floor under different names: divergent (convex - trend, macro) strategies supply diversification in stress, while convergent (concave - relative-value, carry) strategies supply calm-market income.[^bf][^jai] The recurring construction error is to fill both slots from the *same* pole - a roster whose two "diversifiers" are a trend follower and a crash hedge owns two long-gamma sleeves and no income, and the two bleed together through every quiet, range-bound year. The missing piece is almost always the convergent income engine, precisely because it is the one that quietly loses in the dislocations the convex sleeve is bought for. It is also the harder role to source: where no compensated convergent engine can be found net of costs, the evidence favors tilting toward a trend that minimizes its own negative carry over manufacturing a weak income sleeve.[^carrytrend] Negative skew alone does not qualify a candidate; the loss mechanism must be understood and the complete portfolio must improve. That constraint - and the related finding that the concave pole buys calm-market income efficiency more than extra crisis protection - is where the floor's validation effort concentrates, and it is tested directly below.

## Target: close the responder's speed gap

The third job is immediate defense: respond before trend has observed enough of a move to reposition. It does not have to be another convex sleeve. The strongest institutional benchmark is `DAR4020`, whose dynamically selected defensive-factor portfolio tends to arrive with negative beta at drawdown onset while trend becomes more useful as the dislocation persists.[^baltussen] Contractual convexity remains the residual tool for a speed gap that negative-beta factors cannot guarantee. AQR's comparison of index puts against trend draws that narrower line - puts returned over 40% in the fast COVID crash, while trend did its best work in drawn-out bears - and because slow drawdowns do more lasting damage to compounding, trend is the workhorse and explicit tail protection the supplement.[^aqrtail]

When contractual convexity is necessary, its cost decides how much the book can own. A standing crash-insurance position pays the full premium every calm day.[^aqrtail] That bleed is large and well-measured: index put protection is expensive even when option prices look low, and a naive full-time protective-put program has historically delivered *worse* drawdown-per-unit-return than simply holding less of the risk asset - except against the sudden gap where option convexity is uniquely effective.[^israelov] So explicit tail protection is held as a *cost budget*, not treated as the default meaning of immediate defense. Fix the annual premium the book will pay for a target amount of coverage at a defined stress attachment, rank candidate convex legs by crisis-payoff-per-unit-carry, fill only the residual coverage need, and revisit the budget slowly. Where a diversified defensive-factor implementation is unavailable, explicit convexity is a fallback imposed by access constraints rather than the target architecture.

The two convex mechanisms are complementary, not redundant: a peer-reviewed study combining a slow trend overlay with an option-based tail hedge via portable alpha improved performance in all nine crisis episodes tested, adding roughly 0.25% per month after factor controls.[^schwalbach] What turns a bleeding hedge into a contributor is not clever timing but *monetization* - selling the convex leg into the spike and buying risk at the trough - and a fixed-weight, continuously rebalanced sleeve performs that rule automatically, which is why the cost budget is paired with disciplined rebalancing rather than a discretionary trigger. The tempting alternative is to size the tail *episodically* - up when a stress regime looks likely, down otherwise - and regime-parity gives that idea a principled form: equal-risk-contribution weights computed conditional on each regime and weighted by the regime's steady-state probability, a construction with genuine peer-reviewed support.[^lombardodier][^costakwon] The roster does not adopt it for the tail. Episodic sizing needs a live regime signal that *leads* the shock, and stress triggers computable from daily data tend to fire after the gap they are meant to pre-position for (see Limitations), so the executable version collapses back to the small standing sleeve the rebalance already monetizes. Regime-parity stays the contested alternative - defensible for a sleeve slow enough that a stable regime signal can drive it, but not for the tail, whose triggers arrive too late to act on.

## Expansion: add an off-axis stream

Market-neutral and relative-value are the fourth tier because they sit *off* the main regime axis - near-zero net beta/gamma, low dependence on the first three jobs, and returns driven by cross-sectional dispersion. They are the diversifier of diversifiers, and they belong last for two reasons. They add the least before the book has ordinary-market funding, persistent-crisis response and immediate defense. And they are the most sensitive to breadth: a ranking strategy needs a wide cross-section to separate winners from losers, and a thin universe starves it. Ilmanen's case for harvesting many low-correlated premia in parallel is the steady state this tier aims at, but only once the floor and target are in place.[^ilm]

## Why this order and not another

Each tier answers a failure the one below cannot. The floor fixes the worst one - a one-regime book with either no ordinary-market funding engine or no persistent-crisis response. The target fixes the floor's blind spot - the fast crash the lagged responder sleeps through. The expansion fixes the residual - dependence on regime itself - by adding a stream driven by cross-sectional dispersion. Adding the tiers out of order buys little: a tail overlay on a book with no income still bleeds unfunded, and a market-neutral sleeve cannot replace missing crisis response.

There is a fourth failure the order exists to avoid, and it is the one most allocators actually commit: the *over-diversified book*. More strategies is not more diversification - three mechanisms that all fail in the same state are less useful than one matched pair of independent jobs - and the fund-of-funds evidence shows the failure is active, not passive. Past roughly twenty underlying funds the variance-reduction from adding names stalls, and beyond that over-diversification *raises* left-tail risk while lowering returns, because each added sleeve piles on correlated crash exposure and a due-diligence cost but no new independent bet.[^overdiv] A roster grows safely only by adding a job or risk axis it does not already span - which is what each tier, and only a tier, does.

## The upgrade path

A taxonomy that cannot grow without breaking its contract is a dead end. The roster scales along three horizons, none of which touches the return-stream contract the allocator consumes, so each is additive.

First, decompose sub-roles inside the existing families before adding new ones. The trend sleeve is the natural first candidate, but the robust decomposition is not the obvious fast/slow barbell: a fast leg re-imports the very whipsaw the sleeve exists to ride through. What survives instead is an *ensemble of adjacent slow lookbacks* that diversifies lookback specification-luck into distinct Locked Candidates without adding a fast leg. And for the drawdown-payer role specifically, the higher-value lever is not a new lookback at all but a speed-neutral *turnover buffer* - a no-trade band that Pareto-improves the single-lookback champion on Sharpe, crisis capture, and drawdown at once. The horizon trade-offs behind both are in [[trend-following-in-whipsaw-regimes]] and [[relative-vs-absolute-momentum-in-small-universes]].

Second, widen the asset universe along orthogonal, non-equity axes. Adding independent macro drivers - the rate curve, real rates, currencies, and broad and single commodities - raises the number of *independent* bets, not merely the nominal count. Adding correlated equity exposure would raise the count while diluting the convex tail (the substrate requirement above); the construction that actually adds breadth, and the point where it saturates, are in [[the-orthogonal-non-equity-trend-universe]].

Third, add a fifth family only when a genuinely new payoff shape is available. The natural candidate is volatility-arbitrage or liquidity-provision: a near-zero-beta sleeve that harvests the volatility risk premium by selling implied and buying realized protection - a convex/concave hybrid that sits apart from the four regime roles (the VRP mechanics are in [[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]]).

## Breadth and capacity

Two numbers bound the roster: how wide it can be across families, and how deep within one.

Breadth is richer than the asset count suggests. By Grinold's Fundamental Law, risk-adjusted skill scales with the square root of the number of independent bets, $IR \approx IC \times \sqrt{BR}$.[^grinold] A naive reading caps a ten-instrument universe at ten bets, but breadth is set by *independent* bets, not nominal assets, and that distinction has a formal measure. Meucci's effective number of bets is the exponential entropy of a portfolio's uncorrelated-factor risk distribution: one when a single principal portfolio carries all the risk, rising toward the factor count only as risk spreads across genuinely uncorrelated sources.[^meucci] Choueifaty's diversification ratio recovers the same quantity by another route - its square is the number of independent risk factors a portfolio effectively spans.[^choueifaty] Under either measure, highly correlated assets collapse to a few effective bets while near-orthogonal cross-asset ETFs preserve them; one practitioner estimate puts the effective bets in a ten-ETF cross-asset book near 6.8, far above what a same-size single-sector book would deliver.[^resolve] This matters most where the roster needs it: the effective number of bets predicts realized performance specifically in bear markets, so orthogonal breadth is not a fair-weather nicety but the quantity that pays in the stress the roster is built for.[^enbstress] It softens, without removing, the breadth limit on the market-neutral expansion tier - the universe is thin in names but not in orthogonal risk.

Depth within a family is capped by redundancy. Adding strategies helps only while they stay uncorrelated; the marginal variance reduction from the $M$-th strategy in a family of uniform pairwise correlation $\rho$ is

$$\frac{\partial \sigma_p^2}{\partial M} = -\frac{\sigma^2(1-\rho)}{M^2}$$

which decays as $1/M^2$. At a redundancy ceiling of $\rho \approx 0.65$ the benefit is meaningful from one strategy to two, modest from two to three, and negligible past three - the within-family echo of the over-diversified book above, now as a numeric cap. A workable default follows: cap a family at roughly three active, non-redundant Locked Candidates, and enforce a within-family correlation ceiling (realized-return $\rho \lesssim 0.65$ over a rolling year) above which the lower-Sharpe candidate is retired or merged. These specific cut points are engineering defaults, not laws.

## Limitations

Tail's tier rank is genuinely contested, on two axes. On *whether* to hold one: AQR's own reading leans against a standing tail sleeve at all, on the grounds that its bleed costs more than the slow-drawdown protection trend already supplies.[^aqrtail] On *how to size* it: a standing cost-budgeted sleeve monetized by rebalancing competes with episodic or regime-parity sizing.[^lombardodier][^costakwon] The latter requires a stable signal that leads the shock rather than identifying stress after the gap, so “tail = target tier, cost-budgeted” remains a working stance rather than a settled result.

The expansion tier is breadth-limited, though less than raw asset count implies. Cross-sectional market-neutral strategies need many names to rank; a narrow universe may retain several independent macro bets yet still lack enough cross-sectional breadth. Whether the fourth tier earns its slot is therefore an empirical question, not a foregone conclusion.

The floor's convergent income engine is the hardest role to source, and the whole roster rests on it. If no compensated convergent strategy can be made to pay net of costs in the available universe, the floor loses its ordinary-market funding leg. Carry and mean reversion are candidates, not mandatory implementations. So the floor's feasibility, not the higher tiers, is where validation effort belongs first.
