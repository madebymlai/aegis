# Trend-Following in Whipsaw Regimes

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> A trend sleeve's losses in choppy, trendless years are not a fixable defect but the premium it pays for crash-time convexity; the evidence says you cannot filter your way out of whipsaw, only diversify trend speed and control drawdown. The one asymmetric exception - compressing only the short side's deepest readings into rebound-prone states - is drawdown control with a peer-reviewed spine, not a filter.

Every trend-follower bleeds in range-bound markets, and the instinct is to add a filter that switches the strategy off when no trend is present. The literature is unusually clear that this instinct is mostly wrong: the bleed is structural, the filters that promise to remove it are lagging and overfit, and the strongest neutral evidence says the bad years are caused by something no filter can manufacture - the absence of large market moves. What *does* survive out-of-sample is narrower and less satisfying: diversify across trend speeds so turning points hurt less, and use volatility scaling for drawdown control rather than as a source of chop-year alpha. This article separates the two.

## Whipsaw is the price of convexity, not a bug

A trend rule is mechanically a delta-replicated long straddle. Fung and Hsieh showed that a primitive trend-following strategy has the payoff of a lookback straddle - it earns the maximum-minus-minimum price travel over its window - which is why trend returns are long-volatility and largest in the most extreme up *and* down months.[^funghsieh] Bruder and Gaussel decompose the same payoff into an option component (the convexity) plus a trading-impact component (a premium proportional to the underlying's Sharpe), making explicit that the convex profile is bought, not free.[^bruder] The cost of that option is whipsaw: a strategy that scales into moves and out of reversals must repeatedly pay up at every reversal that fails to become a trend.

The deeper mechanism is autocorrelation. Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen show time-series momentum is driven by positive return autocorrelation at horizons of one to twelve months, partially reversing thereafter.[^mop] When a market goes trendless, that autocorrelation collapses toward zero or turns negative, and every entry sits on the wrong side of the next move. The collapse is not uniformly distributed in time, and the century evidence says where it clusters: in the extended aftermath of financial crises. Hutchinson and O'Brien, running a diversified trend portfolio over 63 futures and forward markets from 1925 to 2013 net of costs and 2-and-20 fees, find trend returns in the roughly four years following a crisis start are less than half those of no-crisis periods (two-year windows: 4.0% versus 13.6%, Sharpe 0.36 versus 1.24), and trace it to the substrate - the futures markets themselves lose their time-series predictability in those windows, consistent with the behavioural models' prediction of fewer overreactions after a deleveraging.[^hob] The practical prior this adds: a whipsaw regime is most likely to arrive right after the sleeve has been paid its crisis alpha (2009-2013, 2023-2025), which is exactly when the pressure to "fix" the sleeve is strongest. This matters for diagnosis: the chop-year bleed is a *reversal-sign* problem, not a *weak-trend-strength* problem. Raising a trend-strength threshold filters out weak-but-real trends while doing nothing about reversals at regime transitions, which is precisely where the losses concentrate. A strategy can be holding assets with perfectly adequate trend strength and still be whipsawed as those trends turn.

## The filter trap

The popular regime filters - Kaufman's Efficiency Ratio, ADX, the Hurst exponent - all share two disqualifying properties. They are *lagging*: the Efficiency Ratio and Hurst exponent describe what a window has already done, not what it will do next, so they flag a chop regime only after the whipsaw losses have been taken.[^kaufman][^hurst] And they carry *no out-of-sample validation*: the published performance figures for efficiency-ratio or Hurst-gated systems are blog-grade and uncontrolled, with no peer-reviewed evidence that a standalone range filter improves a diversified trend book. The structural trade-off is that gating out low-efficiency tape forfeits trend capture during weak-but-real trends about as often as it saves losses in genuine chop.

The strongest neutral evidence against the filter instinct is AQR's "You Can't Always Trend When You Want."[^aqrtrend] Analysing post-crisis trend underperformance, they find it is explained by the average absolute size of market moves being unusually muted, not by any decline in trend's ability to convert moves into returns - the relationship between move size and trend's risk-adjusted return is positive and stable. The implication is blunt: if the bleed is the absence of large moves, no efficiency-ratio or Hurst gate can create trend that is not there, and filtering only sacrifices the convex payoff for when moves return. That this argument comes from a firm running trend funds (which has an interest in "stay invested") is a conflict, but the argument cuts *against* the easy-fix narrative its own filters could sell, which strengthens rather than weakens it.

## What survives out of sample: speed diversification

The one fix with genuine peer-reviewed support is diversifying across trend speeds. Garg, Goulding, Harvey and Mazzoleni show that an intermediate blend of fast and slow signals earns a Sharpe of 1.12 against 0.87 for fast-only and 0.81 for slow-only, and - directly relevant to whipsaw - that *disagreement* between fast and slow signals is itself a turning-point warning, the moment when reversal risk is highest.[^garg] Blending speeds smooths the equity curve through exactly the regime transitions that whipsaw a single-speed system.

The nuance is that more speeds is not more diversification. Recent work shows adjacent lookbacks are highly redundant - 125-day and 250-day signals correlate around 0.84, 250-day and 500-day around 0.90 - so a "barbell" of one short and one well-separated long horizon captures nearly all the benefit, and a dense ladder of intermediate lookbacks adds almost nothing once the short and long legs are present.[^barbell] The actionable form of the fix is two well-separated speeds, not a stack of them.

Recent work also upgrades signal *disagreement* from a warning into an operational rule, and this is what survives even where adding a fast trading leg does not. Harvey, Goulding and Mazzoleni show that the frequency of trend turning points is the single best contemporaneous explainer of weak trend returns, and - decisively for the filter question - that it is *not predictable in advance*, so no forecast can avoid it.[^breakingbad] What does work is to *react* at the turn: when a fast (~2-month) and a slow (~12-month) signal disagree, the slow signal is placing its worst bets, and scaling the position down or pausing new entries through the disagreement improves risk-adjusted returns out of sample.[^breakingbad] This dissolves an apparent contradiction. A fast signal fails as an *additive trading leg* on a thin correlated book because it buys its own whipsaw; the same fast signal used only as a *turning-point gate* on the slow leg - a position-size modulator, never a position of its own - is a different instrument with published support, and it targets exactly the reversal-sign losses that a trend-strength threshold leaves untouched.

## Volatility scaling is drawdown control, not chop-alpha

Volatility targeting is often proposed as a chop remedy, and the evidence asks for care. Harvey and co-authors find that vol-scaling raises the Sharpe ratio only for equity-like and credit assets, through the leverage effect (volatility and returns are negatively related there), and that the Sharpe benefit is negligible for bonds, currencies, and commodities.[^harveyvol] For a trend sleeve that is mostly *not* equity, the robust benefit of vol-scaling is therefore not higher Sharpe but lower tail risk - reduced vol-of-vol, kurtosis, and drawdowns. And the broader vol-timing claim is OOS-fragile: Moreira and Muir's in-sample finding that scaling down in high-vol periods raises alpha[^moreiramuir] is contradicted out-of-sample by Cederburg and co-authors, who find real-time vol-managed portfolios generally earn *lower* certainty-equivalent returns and Sharpe ratios than simply holding the unmanaged portfolio.[^cederburg] Use vol-scaling to control drawdown, and do not expect it to pay for the chop.

Two weaker levers round out the menu. Cross-asset *breadth or dispersion* gates - throttling exposure by how many instruments are genuinely trending - are plausible and correlate with trend performance, but the supporting evidence is thin and largely CTA-sourced, so they are research-grade rather than established. And *signal smoothing* via a saturating response function reduces turnover and therefore the transaction-cost share of the whipsaw bill,[^baz] but it does not restore the missing autocorrelation; it makes whipsaw cheaper, not rarer.

A third lever has gathered credible recent evidence: a *one-sided* response adjustment. Invesco show that muting only the most-negative signal readings toward zero, while leaving the positive tail uncapped, cut a momentum book's maximum drawdown by roughly 40% (about 21% to 13%) while preserving most of its crisis-window gains, because the deepest whipsaw losses come from holding full inverse exposure into a sharp upside reversal.[^invescomute] That asymmetry is not a vendor invention - its peer-reviewed origin is Daniel and Moskowitz's momentum-crash anatomy: crashes concentrate in panic states (after market declines, when volatility is high), arrive with the rebound rather than the fall, and are owned by the short side, whose up-market beta in bear states (2.16) far exceeds its down-market beta (1.56), so the book behaves like a written call option on the market - in the 1932 and 2009 rebounds the loser decile "crashed up" 232% and 163% while the market rose far less.[^dmcrash] The same option-like short-side behaviour appears in index-futures, commodity, fixed-income and currency momentum, and exposure rules built on it roughly double the static strategy's Sharpe.[^dmcrash] The allocation literature has since reached the same place independently: Zakamulin, computing Sharpe-optimal exposures per market regime strictly out of sample, finds the optimal bear-regime exposure is typically near zero and occasionally mildly positive - almost never the full short a naive trend signal takes - and that repositioning within regimes, not a better signal, carries the gain (average out-of-sample Sharpe 0.21 to 0.51 across eighteen diversified datasets).[^zakopt] The asymmetry is the whole point: capping the positive tail would trade away the convexity, but compressing the negative extreme is mostly drawdown control. What does *not* work is the cruder version of the same instinct - de-risking the whole book after a drawdown threshold. Man show that a 20%-drawdown-triggered de-allocation, even spread across three uncorrelated trend programs, underperforms staying the course, because it misses the rebounds that follow managed-futures drawdowns; the recovery is where the return is.[^patience] This is the filter section's null reached from the drawdown side: the no-trade band already absorbs the cheap part of the whipsaw, and timing re-entry destroys the patience premium rather than capturing it. Whether the 2023-2025 run of losses is decay rather than weather - the natural next worry once a sleeve is whipsawed for three years - is treated separately in [[is-the-trend-premium-decaying]]; the short answer is weather plus a thin-book capacity tax, not a broken signal.

## Limitations

- The "filters overfit" conclusion rests partly on an *absence* of evidence (no peer-reviewed study shows efficiency-ratio or Hurst gates work OOS) plus AQR's muted-moves argument; it is well-motivated but not a single clean disproof. Confidence: medium-high. One nuance from the counter-lane: Zakamulin's regime-optimal result shows regime-*conditioned position sizing* can add large OOS value even where regime-*forecasting filters* fail - the gain comes from how much to hold within a detected state, not from predicting the state.[^zakopt]
- Speed-blending's benefit is well-supported (two independent sources), but the figures come from broad futures universes; the magnitude in a thin equity-ETF book is unverified. The barbell-redundancy figures (adjacent-lookback correlations 0.84-0.90) additionally rest on a 2025 preprint that remains unrefereed with no citations as of mid-2026 and is authored by a CTA-replication vendor (Ai For Alpha) - a conflict the original citation did not flag; treat the numbers as practitioner-grade until a peer-reviewed home appears.[^barbell]
- Vol-scaling's asset-class dependence and OOS fragility are strongly sourced (Harvey plus Cederburg); the breadth-gate lever is genuinely uncertain and flagged as such.
- The one-sided mute now has three legs (the Invesco backtest, Daniel-Moskowitz's short-side crash anatomy, Zakamulin's near-zero optimal bear exposure), but both academic legs are equity-substrate - US cross-sectional momentum deciles and equity-index/French-library portfolios respectively - and Zakamulin's is a single not-yet-refereed working paper. The transfer to a thin cross-asset time-series book is by analogy; our own runs are the bridge. The mute's cost is also real and priced: the short leg genuinely earns crisis-conditional return in sustained bears, so the cap trades crisis capture for drawdown relief - a trade, not a free lunch.[^dmcrash][^zakopt]

## Strategy hypotheses this could seed

- [x] A two-speed barbell (one short, one well-separated long lookback) blended in the trend core reduces turning-point/whipsaw bleed in trendless years without flattening crash-time convexity - the best-evidenced fix. **Killed 2026-06-10** (in our book): the fast leg added whipsaw rather than dampening it - 2023 worsened to -0.99 from the champion's -0.50 and overall held-out Sharpe fell to +0.731 from +1.008. The article's own thin-book caveat cut the other way; the broad-futures magnitude did not transfer to ten correlated ETFs. See [[runs/aegis/2026-06-10|run diary]] and the graveyard.
- [x] Throttling gross exposure by trend breadth (count of instruments past the entry band) cuts the chop bleed - test, but pre-register as research-grade given thin evidence. **Supported 2026-06-10** (research-grade): the optimizer selected the throttle (breadth_norm 0.4) on top of the champion's exact params - held-out Sharpe +1.058 vs +1.008 unthrottled, 2023 bleed cut to -0.36 from -0.50 with the 2022 crash year intact (+0.84). It does NOT touch fast-crash years (2018 -1.55 vs -1.65 on extended data). One confirming run; re-test before promoting. See [[runs/aegis/2026-06-10|run diary]].
- [ ] A cross-sectional or vol-regime gross throttle reduces drawdown in chop but does *not* improve Sharpe - confirm vol-scaling is a drawdown tool, not a chop-alpha tool.
- [ ] No standalone regime filter (efficiency ratio / strength threshold) beats simply diversifying trend speed - the AQR null, worth confirming directly since the campaign already killed one trend-gate.
- [x] A fast signal used as a *turning-point gate* on the slow leg (scale the position down on fast/slow disagreement), not as an additive trading leg, reduces 2023-2025-style V-reversal bleed without the whipsaw the killed fast *leg* re-introduced - the operational form of Breaking Bad Trends on our thin book. **Killed as a convexity lever, pivots to drawdown - 2026-06-29** ([[runs/atalanta/2026-06-29#20260628T221359891740Z_atalanta_trend_turngate|turngate]]): the gate *correctly targets* the live worst split (a genuine reversal - it lifts that window -5.35% -> -2.81% and cuts its drawdown 13.45 -> 9.61 at gate 0.5), but it is convexity-*negative* in aggregate (held-out trend_convexity_payoff falls monotonically 0.424 -> 0.401 -> 0.358) because it also de-risks healthy mid-trend pullbacks - the AQR filter-null reproduced on our own book, even with the fast signal as a *modulator* not a trading leg. It cuts mean drawdown, so it survives only as a *drawdown* tool (the one-sided-mute hypothesis below), never the convex pole. gate=1.0 reproduced the champion bit-for-bit (clean nested test). Confirms the section's thesis: you cannot filter your way out of whipsaw without forfeiting convex capture.
- [x] A one-sided negative-signal mute (compress the most-negative readings toward zero, leave the positive tail uncapped) cuts held-out drawdown without materially lowering crisis-conditional return - Invesco's asymmetry, tested on the borrow-gated short leg where it bites hardest. **Supported and confirmed (interior peak), one caveat on the crisis clause - 2026-06-29** ([[runs/atalanta/2026-06-29#20260628T222637060832Z_atalanta_trend_shortmute|shortmute]]): capping only the IDTL short's deepest readings (longs untouched, freed gross -> cash) beats the champion, and a finer sweep + UPI + crisis-conditional confirmation brackets an **interior peak at `short_cap ~= 0.05`** - convexity 0.424 -> 0.453 (+6.8%), UPI 6.21 -> 7.02 (+13%), mean drawdown 8.58 -> 6.51, the worst whipsaw split's drawdown crushed 13.45 -> 2.82, return preserved. Long-only (`0.0`) is *worse* than `0.05` on convexity, so it is a cap, not a drop. The hypothesis's drawdown clause holds strongly; its "without lowering crisis-conditional return" clause is the caveat - `crisis_quarter_return` *decreases* monotonically as the cap tightens (champion 0.287 -> 0.05: 0.273, ~5%), because the IDTL short genuinely adds crisis-conditional return at full size. So the mute trades ~5% crisis return for large convexity/UPI/drawdown gains. The direct counterpoint to the killed turning-point gate above: a *symmetric* filter that touches the longs forfeits convex capture, but a *one-sided* mute that touches only the short's left-tail whipsaw improves the convex shape - Invesco's asymmetry, confirmed. **Promoted to the live `trend.yaml` at `short_cap=0.10` on 2026-06-29** (swap `keystone` -> `keystone_shortmute`; the cap peak moved 0.05 UCITS -> 0.2 on a US-ETF 2017-24 cross-instrument stress, so 0.10 is the robust middle, not the over-fit metric-max).
- [ ] A *state-conditioned* short cap - bind the mute only in panic states (trailing book drawdown plus elevated realized volatility, the Daniel-Moskowitz conditioning[^dmcrash]) and release it otherwise - preserves more crisis-conditional return than the static cap at equal reversal-window drawdown relief. Expected direction: the conditioned cap recovers most of the ~5% crisis-return give-up recorded for the static mute while keeping the whipsaw-split drawdown gains, because the short side's up-beta asymmetry exists only in the panic state, and outside it the full short is genuine crisis capture. Refuted if daily-bar detection of the panic state is too laggy to separate the states (the known detection-lag failure mode of coincident triggers). Mechanically distinct from the graveyard's symmetric turning-point gate (this touches only the short cap, never the longs) and from the killed fast leg (no new trading leg is added).
