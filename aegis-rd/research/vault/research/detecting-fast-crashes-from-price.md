# Detecting Fast Crashes from Price

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> No daily signal *predicts* a fast V-shaped crash; the fastest honest signals live inside the daily bar - a Yang-Zhang range volatility and the raw overnight gap react a day or more before close-to-close vol and weeks before a 252-day trend - so the realistic edge is a *coincident* throttle that fires on day 0-1 of the shock, and the binding design constraint is the false-positive bill in calm years, with de-risking to cash better evidenced than rotating to a haven - and the de-risk action now carries a named theoretical condition: an exit adds value only if the shock continues past the trigger, which a V-shaped rebound violates by construction. Tested, the ceiling held: the flagship in-bar triggers fired post-shock on the episodes that matter, which routes fast-crash coverage to *bought* gamma (a tail sleeve) rather than detection - this article is the measured wall any future crash-trigger proposal must climb.

A slow trend book is a *sustained-bear* instrument: a long-lookback core, sometimes with a haven leg, that harvests grinds like 2022. Its structural hole is the fast V-shaped shock. 2018 is the reference case - a year that stacked two fast reversals (February's volatility-ETP unwind, the Q4 selloff) on a rate shock where every major haven except the dollar fell,[^bespoke] so a slow core that entered the year long from 2017's melt-up was whipsawed before it could turn, and a haven rotation had nothing trending to rotate into. The question this article settles is narrow and falsifiable: is there a *daily-computable* signal that fires early enough in such a shock to cut the loss, and if so, what does "early" actually buy against a 252-day trend that lags by weeks?

The answer is sobering but usable. The early-warning literature, read honestly, says nothing on a daily horizon *predicts* a fast crash. What the data does allow is *faster detection* - and the speed gradient is real: the overnight gap and the daily high-low range register a shock before the day's close does, and any of these beats a 252-day trend by weeks. The edge on offer is reaction latency, not prediction, and the cleanest place to harvest latency is the open-high-low-close-volume bar itself.

## The prediction ceiling: price-based early warning does not time crashes

The cleanest test of the whole early-warning paradigm is Guttal and co-authors, who run the standard instability indicators across 115 years of the Dow and multiple indices.[^guttal] Their result is two-edged and decisive. Rising *variance* is a genuine leading signal: it preceded every major crash in the sample by at least three months with no false negatives. But it threw seven false alarms, and - critically - it "does not forecast *when* a crash might occur." The companion critical-slowing-down indicator (rising lag-1 autocorrelation), which works for ecological and climate tipping points, shows no usable trend before financial crashes; the authors conclude meltdowns are not forecastable critical transitions but stochastic shocks. The lesson transfers directly: a price-only signal that *leads* (rising variance) cannot time the crash and fires often and wrong, while a signal precise enough to act on does not lead. This is the same trap the whipsaw article found for regime filters - lagging, overfit, and whipsaw-prone - now shown to be a property of the price series itself.[^whipsaw]

That sets the ceiling. The realistic goal is not to predict the crash but to *react inside it faster than a 252-day trend does*, and to pay an acceptable false-positive cost for that speed. The rest of this article is a speed ranking of the signals a daily bar can produce, from the fastest (inside-the-bar range and gap) to the slowest.

## The fastest signals are inside the bar: range and gap

A close-to-close return throws away everything the session did between the prints. The daily bar does not. Two constructions extract a faster read of stress from the open, high, low and close that a single bar already contains, and they should be the first toolkit tried because they are the fastest signals daily data gives.

The first is a **range-based volatility estimator**. Parkinson uses only the high-low log range and is about five times more efficient than squared close-to-close returns; Garman-Klass adds the open and close for roughly seven times; Rogers-Satchell makes the estimate independent of drift.[^parkinson][^garmanklass] The efficiency gain is the point: a regime shift crosses a detection threshold *earlier in calendar time* with a range estimator than with close-to-close, because each bar carries far more information, so a short lookback suffices where close-to-close needs a long noisy one. But there is a caveat that is fatal if ignored and is exactly a fast-crash signature: all three estimators are computed from the *intraday* range and assume continuous trading, so they systematically *understate* volatility when the move arrives as an overnight gap - the jump from yesterday's close to today's open lands outside the day's high-low range and is never measured.[^rangevol][^yangzhang] Fast crashes are delivered largely through overnight gaps (March 2020 opened down roughly 7% repeatedly, tripping circuit breakers), so the fastest range estimators are blind to the fastest channel unless corrected. The fix is **Yang-Zhang**, the one estimator that adds an explicit overnight (close-to-open) variance term and is therefore robust to opening jumps while keeping eight-to-fourteen-fold efficiency.[^yangzhang][^rangevol] Yang-Zhang is the primary fast-vol trigger to try first.

The second is the **overnight gap** itself, read raw: the magnitude of the close-to-open move, `|Open_t / Close_{t-1} - 1|`. It is mechanically the *first* observation a daily bar offers of an overnight shock - you see the gap at the open, before the day's range or close has formed - which makes it at least coincident and plausibly the earliest daily read available. The return-decomposition literature establishes that overnight and intraday returns are distinct, often opposite-signed components, and that overnight information carries incremental downside-risk content, though it stops short of proving a gap *predicts* a crash rather than registering one.[^lps][^overnighttail] Two practical cautions. At the ETF and index level, idiosyncratic single-name earnings gaps wash out, so calm-year false positives are lower than the single-stock gap literature implies. But a dividend-adjusted (total-return) close series injects spurious gaps on ex-dividend days into a naive `Open / Close_{t-1}` comparison; the gap must be computed on a consistent adjustment basis or ex-dividend days screened, or the trigger will fire on dividends.

Both signals beat close-to-close realized vol on speed, and all of them beat the 252-day trend by weeks. Neither predicts; both react fast. That is the most the bar can give.

## Cross-asset detectors, and what to discard

A single instrument's bar misses the coordination that defines a risk-off day - the moment when everything moves together. The **financial turbulence index** of Kritzman and Li captures it: the squared Mahalanobis distance of the day's cross-sectional return vector from its historical mean and covariance, `FT_t = (r_t - mu)' * inv(Sigma) * (r_t - mu)`.[^kritzmanturb] Computed from a small cross-asset ETF return cross-section, it scores a day high when moves are jointly unusual in size *or* correlation, even if no single asset's move is extreme. Its one documented timing property is persistence: turbulence stays elevated for about two weeks after the first spike, so the usable play is react on day 0 and stay defensive through the cluster.[^poturb] The honest framing is that turbulence is a *coincident* detector, not a predictor, and its persistence backtests flag whipsaw on the sharp recovery (a turbulence throttle sat in cash through the COVID rebound); the originating authors market turbulence products, a conflict worth noting.[^poturb2] Corroboration independent of the originators now exists, with the failure mode included: a Hitotsubashi study replicating the index on a six-asset global universe confirms its spikes coincide with recognized stress events, documents that it stayed elevated straight through the post-COVID rebound (the opportunity-loss half of the trade, independently reproduced), and finds that only a *signed*, autocorrelation-cleaned extension beats the raw index and a static allocation in simulation - a refinement of the detector, not a validation of the raw throttle.[^kamauchi] Turbulence pairs naturally with Yang-Zhang: the range estimator times the equity leg's stress, turbulence times the cross-asset coordination, and a fast crash usually lights up both.

Two cross-asset constructions look promising and should be discarded. A **correlation spike** - rising average pairwise correlation across the book's ETFs - looks like a regime warning but is largely a statistical artifact: Forbes and Rigobon show measured correlation is biased upward exactly when volatility is high, and correcting the bias makes the apparent spikes of 1987, 1994 and 1997 vanish.[^forbesrigobon] A raw correlation gate fires *because* volatility already rose; it is a downstream symptom, not a lead. (Turbulence escapes this because it weights the full inverse covariance rather than reading a raw correlation level.) And a **volume spike** is not a fast-onset trigger at all. Karpoff's survey establishes that volume and volatility share a latent information driver and move together, and the Granger-causality evidence runs from volatility *to* volume, not the reverse, so volume cannot lead price; capitulation volume marks *bottoms*, identifiable in hindsight, not onsets.[^karpoff][^grangervol] Keep a volume z-score as a coincident confirmer if at all, never as the leading edge.

The much-cited **absorption ratio** - the share of variance in the top principal components, claimed to spike about a month before declines - fails this use on three counts: its lead claim rests on a single conflicted source, an independent replication found the ratio *decreasing* before crashes, and it runs on a 52-week weekly covariance window built for slow regime decay, not a one-week February-2018 unwind.[^kritzmanar][^poar] It is not a fast-crash trigger.

## Flight-to-safety days: a coincident risk-off flag, regime-hardened

The flight-to-safety literature supplies a different and more interpretable coincident signal: a daily flag for equities falling while havens rally. Baele, Bekaert, Inghelbrecht and Wei identify flight-to-safety days across 23 countries using only daily bond and stock returns, through a threshold rule that is cleanly computable from daily ETF closes: a day qualifies when the bond return exceeds a positive volatility-scaled threshold *and* the stock return falls below the symmetric negative one, `r_bond > k*sigma_bond AND r_stock < -k*sigma_stock`.[^baele] Calibrated at `k = 1.5` these days are about 0.8% of the sample yet carry an average bond-minus-equity differential of 4.26%; the published headline rounds to under 2% of days and a 2.7% differential.[^baele] On US ETF proxies the flag is `SPY < -k*sigma_SPY AND TLT > +k*sigma_TLT`, with `k` near 1.5 for rare-event flagging or nearer 1.0 for a more frequent sizing signal, and the volatilities can come from the same range estimators above rather than close-to-close.

The decisive property is that this flag is **coincident, not leading**. The paper's own evidence is unambiguous: the bond-minus-equity impact is already -3.16% on the *first* day of a flight-to-safety spell, and the day before an isolated one the impact is essentially zero - there is no measurable pre-move, because the flag is a same-day co-exceedance by construction.[^baele] Spells are short, 89% lasting three days or fewer. So it cannot front-run a crash; it confirms one as it starts, which is still useful for scaling exposure down once a cluster begins.

The flag inherits the weakness of its haven leg. A Treasury-up requirement breaks precisely in inflation shocks: in 2022, the first year since the late 1970s that stocks and bonds both fell, TLT declined *with* SPY for months, so a TLT-up flag would have stayed silent through the decade's worst equity drawdown.[^cheema][^statestreet] The fix the cross-asset evidence supports is to make the haven leg an *or* across the three havens whose failure regimes differ: `(TLT up) OR (UUP up) OR (GLD up)`. The dollar is the most regime-robust leg - it held in 2022 and spiked in the March-2020 dash for cash - gold is second, and Treasuries are the most fragile.[^cheema][^baurlucey] This is the complementarity the divergent-vs-defensive article found at the edge level, pushed into the trigger definition.[^divergent]

## Latency versus false positives, and what to do when it fires

Two design facts decide whether any of this helps. The first is that the relevant comparison is not trigger-versus-foresight but trigger-versus-the-slow-trend-already-running. A 252-day trend does not flip until a move is weeks old; a Yang-Zhang range spike, an overnight gap, a turbulence spike or a flight-to-safety flag fires on day 0-1. Against that baseline a coincident detector *is* an improvement in fast shocks, which is the whole point. The second fact is that the improvement is bought with false positives in calm years, and the whipsaw article's lesson is that a trigger-happy or lagging filter forfeits the convex payoff it was meant to protect.[^whipsaw] The turbulence throttle's documented failure - sitting in cash through the violent post-COVID recovery - is exactly this.[^poturb] Every trigger must therefore be judged on its calm-year drag and its re-entry whipsaw, not only on the crash it catches; the right metric is the held-out tradeoff between 2018-style protection and 2019/2021-style false-positive cost. The in-bar signals have an edge here too: because they are more efficient, they can run a shorter lookback and a higher threshold for the same detection speed, which is the lever that trades latency against false positives.

On the *action* once a trigger fires, the evidence now has a theoretical spine. Kaminski and Lo prove that under a random walk a simple exit-on-threshold rule *always* lowers expected return - a stop adds value only when post-trigger returns exhibit continuation (momentum) - and their empirical stop on US equities added on the order of 50-200 basis points per month during stopped-out periods (monthly data, 1950-2004/2008 working-paper samples, long-term Treasuries as the stop asset, rationalized by a regime-switching model with periodic flights-to-quality).[^kamlo] The condition is the point: a fast V-shaped rebound is by construction the regime where continuation fails, so the same stop that pays in a sustained slide subtracts value in the exact shock this article targets - the formal version of the re-entry whipsaw the tested throttles exhibited. Two further legs bound the trade: on individual US stocks with transaction costs, tight stops underperform buy-and-hold unless serial correlation is sufficiently high,[^loremorov] and when stop-loss and re-entry rules are judged jointly across nine asset markets, the volatility and excess-loss reduction is robust while the return enhancement is mixed and concentrated in secular bear markets.[^klement] One honest asymmetry in the anchor: Kaminski-Lo's stop asset was long duration, whose haven role is itself regime-fragile,[^cheema] and none of the sources establishes that rotating into a specific haven beats plain de-risking - so cutting gross exposure to cash remains the conservative default, and any haven tilt a separate, weaker bet to test on its own.[^poturb][^kritzmanar]

## Limitations

- The honest ceiling is low. The strongest neutral source says price-based early warning cannot time crashes and throws frequent false alarms;[^guttal] everything here is a *coincident* detector, and its value over the existing trend core is reaction latency, not prediction. If even a fast in-bar throttle whipsaws more than it protects on held-out calm years, the fast-crash hole may be uncoverable on daily data, which is itself a finding worth establishing.
- The Feb-2018 shock specifically may be structurally invisible. Its mechanism - a short-volatility-ETP unwind and dealer hedging - lives in positioning and options data a daily sleeve cannot see, so a daily trigger can react to the resulting move but never anticipate its cause.
- The range-estimator speed claims are strongly sourced (multiple peer-reviewed estimators plus the gap-understatement caveat), but the *gap-leads-crash* claim is weaker: it rests on a decomposition result plus mechanism. Treat it as a prior to test against Yang-Zhang, not a result.
- The *action* evidence is no longer unsourced but remains conditional. The stop-loss theorem says an exit pays only under post-trigger continuation, and that condition is unobservable at trigger time - in a fast V-shock it fails by construction.[^kamlo] The empirical stop-loss legs are monthly-frequency US equity samples with duration as the stop asset; the transfer to a daily, cross-asset book stopping into cash is ours to test. Cash-beats-rotation stays a prior, now with a mechanism behind it rather than no evidence at all.
- The turbulence detector's corroboration independent of its originators is a single non-refereed working paper co-authored by a multi-asset manager (mild COI, though not a turbulence-product vendor), and its positive result belongs to a *signed* extension, simulation-grade;[^kamauchi] the no-COI leg of the turbulence evidence remains thinner than the conflicted leg.
- Out of scope for a daily, price-only book: options chains and implied-volatility feeds, futures term structures, intraday and high-frequency data, order flow and ETP positioning, credit spreads, and any macro or fundamental series. Several of the strongest early-warning results depend on these and are untestable on daily bars alone.

## Strategy hypotheses this could seed

- [x] **Killed both halves** ([[runs/aegis/2026-06-10#20260610T205352247752Z_etf_aegis_v11_yzthrottle|v11]], [[runs/aegis/2026-06-10#20260610T205502094969Z_etf_aegis_v12_ccthrottle|v12]]): A Yang-Zhang range-volatility throttle (per-bar OHLC estimate on the equity leg, z-scored against a short lookback, scaling gross down past a threshold) cuts the held-out 2018 fast-crash loss and crosses its threshold earlier than a close-to-close realized-vol throttle at equal calm-year false positives - the test of whether the in-bar speed gain is real on our data. *Outcome: best 2018 improvement +0.01 Sharpe (vol-target design, fv_target x window grid on the pinned champion); the trigger is structurally post-shock (Feb-2018 cut lands 4-5 days after the bottom), and close-to-close beat Yang-Zhang at matched windows - the prediction-ceiling section called it.*
- [x] **Killed, with a finding** ([[runs/aegis/2026-06-10#20260610T211236206470Z_etf_aegis_v15a_gap_hold5|v15]]): An overnight-gap trigger (`|Open_t / Close_{t-1} - 1|` on an adjustment-consistent basis, ex-dividend days screened) fires on day 0 of the 2018 and 2020-Q1 gap-downs at least as early as the range throttle, and is the earliest daily read of a fast shock - confirm it leads close-to-close and watch the dividend-artifact false positives. *Outcome: the premise fails for 2018 - there were no 2018 gap-downs to fire on (zero >2% SPY gaps; Feb-5 gapped -0.73% and fell -3.48% intraday). Gap delivery is regime-specific: 2020 (27 days >2%) and 2022 (9) yes, 2018 no - the trigger even helps 2022 (+0.77 -> +1.01). At 1% the calm-year false-positive bill is fatal (composite 0.151 -> -0.025). This article's "fast crashes are delivered largely through overnight gaps" generalizes March-2020 too far.*
- [ ] A multivariate turbulence throttle (Mahalanobis distance of the ten-ETF return vector) adds protection beyond the single-leg range throttle by catching cross-asset coordination - test whether the two together beat either alone, and whether turbulence's re-entry whipsaw costs more than it saves.
- [ ] A daily flight-to-safety flag (`SPY < -k*sigma_SPY AND TLT > +k*sigma_TLT`, k near 1.5) is coincident, not leading: it fires on day 0-1 of the 2018 and 2020-Q1 drops but gives essentially zero lead - confirm it sizes but does not time; and replacing the TLT leg with an *or* across `TLT / UUP / GLD` restores firing in 2022.
- [ ] A volume z-score does not lead and adds nothing as a standalone fast trigger - the pre-registered negative control, since the price-volume evidence says volatility drives volume, not the reverse.
- [ ] Routing a fired throttle to cash beats rotating the throttled budget into UUP or short-duration havens on held-out fast-crash splits - the action control the trigger literature implies but does not prove.
- [ ] The stop-loss condition classifies our crash windows: measuring post-trigger continuation (the sign of the cumulative return over the 5-20 days after each fired trigger), the throttle's per-window value added is positive where continuation held (2008- and 2022-style windows) and negative where the window V-reversed (2018-Q4, 2020-Q1) - the Kaminski-Lo condition run as a held-out diagnostic.[^kamlo] Expected direction: per-window value added is monotone in the continuation measure. Refuted if the throttle's win/loss pattern is unrelated to post-trigger continuation, which would break this article's preferred explanation for why the tested triggers failed. A diagnostic over already-run throttles, not a new trigger; nothing here re-proposes a buried mechanism.
