# Convexity as the Axis of Strategy Diversification

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> A strategy portfolio is diversified along one dimension that matters - the sign of its convexity. Almost every return-generating strategy is short skew (paid to sell insurance); a rare few, trend chief among them, are long skew (paid to be convex). Allocation is budgeting between the two poles, not counting mechanisms. The roster this implies - how many sleeves and in what order - is worked out in [[the-tiered-strategy-roster]].

## One axis, not many labels

The conventional way to sort strategies is by mechanism: equity long/short, global macro, carry, statistical arbitrage, managed futures. For an allocator that buys *return streams* and weights them, the mechanism is nearly irrelevant. What decides whether two streams help each other is whether they make and lose money at the same time, and that is governed by the sign of each stream's convexity to the shocks that move all risk assets at once.

Lempérière and co-authors at CFM made the axis explicit. Across a broad set of "risk premium" strategies - equity, Fama-French factors, FX carry, short volatility, bonds, credit - the Sharpe ratio lines up almost linearly with the strategy's *negative* skewness, and barely at all with its volatility.[^lemp] Read literally, the claim is uncomfortable: you are not paid for bearing variance, you are paid for bearing *crash* risk. The strategies fall on a line because they are, economically, the same trade in different clothes. Each one sells insurance and collects a premium for it.

## Trend is the exception that proves the axis

The same study flags one clean outlier. Trend-following carries positive skewness *and* positive excess return at the same time; the Fama-French value factor and low-volatility investing do so too, though less sharply.[^lemp] That combination should be impossible for a risk premium, because it would mean being paid to hold something that also protects you. The authors' reading is that trend is a genuine market anomaly rather than a premium for risk.

The option analogy makes the mechanism concrete. Two decades earlier Fung and Hsieh showed that the returns of trend followers replicate a portfolio of lookback straddles - long-volatility positions on bonds, currencies, and commodities that pay off most in the best and the worst months for world equities.[^fh] Trend is structurally long gamma; carry and its cousins are structurally short gamma. The two poles are a single straddle seen from opposite ends. The trend follower is long the option; the carry seller wrote it. This is the same convex-versus-concave dichotomy that organizes [[divergent-vs-defensive-crash-resistance]], here grounded in the payoff algebra rather than in regime labels.

CFM later made the gamma explicit in price terms. A trend follower holding a position proportional to its accumulated trend has a cumulative P&L equal to one-half the difference between the squared price move over the whole trend horizon and the sum of squared daily moves - its profit is the realized excess of long-horizon variance over short-horizon variance, positive exactly when a sustained move dwarfs the day-to-day chop.[^cfmconv] Two design consequences fall out of that one identity. The convexity is a *multi-period* quantity, so it only shows up when the stream is measured over the move and a bar-by-bar regression reads a genuinely convex sleeve as flat. And it is *fragile to sizing*: any rule that scales the position down as the move grows - symmetric volatility targeting, or renormalizing gross exposure back to a fixed budget every bar - caps the long-horizon term that is the entire source of the gamma, dampening realized $\beta_2$ toward zero even when the signal is convex. Buying the convexity and then sizing it away is the quiet way to ship a long-gamma label on a flat stream.

## Why both sides persist

A return claim is only credible if someone is on the other side and cannot easily leave. Short-skew strategies persist because the demand for crash insurance is structural and price-insensitive: investors with drawdown limits, hedgers, and leveraged players all need protection and will pay for it, so the seller earns a premium in calm. Ilmanen put the general version plainly - across asset classes, buying insurance is poorly rewarded and selling it is paid, which is the cross-asset restatement of the skew axis.[^ins] The variance risk premium is the cleanest case: sellers of variance earn a positive return as compensation for jump and crash risk, because volatility spikes precisely when the market falls.[^vrp] Carry is the same bargain with a slower fuse - carry-trade crashes arrive when funding liquidity dries up and crowded positions unwind together, and VIX spikes coincide with the losses.[^bnp] Someone holds the bag through the unwind and is paid for it the rest of the time.

Trend persists for the opposite reason. It is long the insurance, and its edge is behavioral - under-reaction and herding into established moves - not a payment for bearing risk. That is also why trend "loses more often than it wins" yet keeps positive expectancy: a low hit rate with fat right-tailed payoffs is the signature of a long-gamma position, and it is exactly what a long straddle does.[^fh] An anomaly that happens to be convex is a rare and valuable thing, because the market usually charges for convexity rather than paying for it.

## Diversification is a convexity budget

If most strategies are short skew, then stacking several of them - however different their mechanisms look - just sells more of the same insurance. The book's hidden short gamma accumulates quietly through calm periods and then pays out all at once in the single regime where every seller is hit together. Diversifying by mechanism is how a portfolio ends up looking varied and behaving as one position. The redundancy is measurable even within a single convex family: adjacent trend-following lookbacks run 0.84 to 0.90 correlated, so layering many of them adds parameters without adding a return source.[^trendstruct] Counting strategies is not the same as spanning the skew axis.

Real diversification needs both signs of skew on the book. That turns net portfolio skew into the quantity to manage: the short-skew sleeve is the income engine that compounds in quiet markets, and the long-skew sleeve is the drawdown payer that buys the income engine time to recover when the premium it was collecting comes due. Ilmanen's prescription to harvest many low-correlated premia in parallel - value, carry, trend, volatility, defensive - is the same idea written as a shopping list.[^ilm] The sharper statement is that the list collapses to two poles plus one decision: how much net negative skew the portfolio can carry and still survive its worst week.

## The price of convexity, and how to pay less

Convexity is not free. The long-gamma sleeve bleeds in calm markets because that bleed *is* the premium the short-gamma sleeve is collecting; they are the two ledger entries of one transaction. The practical mistake is to buy convexity by renting it from the same market that prices the insurance.

Our own runs on the ten-ETF book make this concrete. An always-on short-term VIX-ETP floor could only move the 2018 drawdown at weights that destroyed every calm split, and the conclusion was explicit: the roll decay is an equilibrium premium, not something you carry for free.[[2026-06-11#20260611T084627805837Z_etf_aegis_v22_vixyfloor]] The external figure matches the experience - constant-maturity one-month VIX futures lose on the order of 30% a year as an equilibrium variance risk premium, a decay that will not close because it is the price of the convexity, not a market inefficiency.[^eraker] A mid-term VIX floor and an anti-beta floor failed the same way - the convexity shrank faster than its cost.[[2026-06-11#20260611T084651789718Z_etf_aegis_v23_vixmfloor]] The design problem, then, is not "buy convexity" but "buy convexity that is an anomaly, not a rented premium." Trend is one such source - long gamma without paying the straddle's full theta. The dollar behaved as another in our book: a positive-skew haven that paid in stress without the structural bleed of listed volatility, which is why a UUP floor dominated both the VIX and the anti-beta floors.[[the-dollar-as-dual-regime-haven]] The rule that generalizes: prefer convex *anomalies* (trend, certain havens) over convex *risk premia you must rent* (listed options, volatility ETPs), and size the short-skew income sleeve to the net skew the book can survive. This is the allocation-side complement to sourcing convexity cheaply, the question [[always-on-convexity-from-listed-instruments]] asks from the instrument side.

How many sleeves this axis implies, and the order in which to add them, is the roster question - the practical payoff of this argument, worked out in [[the-tiered-strategy-roster]].

## Limitations

The Sharpe-versus-negative-skew line rests principally on one study.[^lemp] Treat its sign and ordering as well-supported by the corroborating premium-specific work cited here, but the linear coefficient as suggestive rather than a law. Several of the supporting sources are AQR-affiliated (Pedersen, Ilmanen), which is a conflict of interest when the conclusion favors selling insurance as a paid premium - the academic carry-crash and variance-premium results stand independently, but the framing is not neutral.[^ins][^bnp]

The premium exists but its timing does not predict cleanly. The variance risk premium's forecasting coefficients are unstable out of sample, so a sleeve that tries to *time* when to sell or buy convexity rather than hold a budgeted exposure inherits that instability.[^pyun]

Carry's negative skew is asset-class-specific. It is strong in FX, present in commodities, and weak or absent in equity and bond carry.[^bnp][^carry] So "carry is short skew" is a tendency tied to where funding-unwind dynamics bite, not a universal property - a sleeve built from bond or equity carry may not carry the signature at all. How to source the concave pole around that constraint is the subject of [[carry-as-the-short-gamma-income-pole]], and the second concave mechanism - short-horizon reversal, and why it doubles the carry sleeve rather than diversifying it - is in [[short-horizon-reversal-in-small-cross-sections]].

Skew is hard to estimate. It is the third moment - defined, and its two pricing channels (systematic risk vs idiosyncratic preference) surveyed, in [[skewness-in-asset-returns]] - and it is dominated by a few tail observations, so a strategy's measured skew can flip sign across samples. A taxonomy that classifies a family by *measuring* its realized stream (build toward a role, then verify) must use tail-aware, regime-conditional estimators rather than a single full-sample moment; this is the open measurement problem in [[measuring-crisis-alpha]], and the operational gate framework that addresses it is [[verifying-strategy-family-membership]].

Haven convexity is regime-dependent. The dollar and bonds are not dependable diversifiers across the positive stock-bond-correlation regime of 2022, which is why the haven sleeve itself needs conditioning - see [[havens-at-regime-turning-points]].

## Strategy hypotheses this could seed

- [x] **Net-skew budgeting.** Build a composite of a trend sleeve (long gamma) and a short-horizon reversal / cross-asset carry sleeve (short gamma) sized so full-sample net skew is near zero, and test whether it beats either sleeve alone on held-out crisis-conditional return at matched volatility. **Refuted on our poles 2026-06-13:** the skew budget is *degenerate* here because the realized skew of the distribution-carry sleeve is ~0 (not negative) - so sizing the composite to net-zero skew drives carry's risk-share to ~100% and the composite collapses onto carry-only, losing trend's crisis protection (crisis-conditional return is monotone-decreasing in carry weight). Net-skew budgeting presumes a concave pole with materially negative *realized* skew; this universe's bond/credit/EM/FX distribution carry is ~symmetric (the article's own "carry's negative skew is asset-class-specific, weak or absent in equity and bond carry" limitation, measured). **Update - VRP re-test, same day:** with a properly-concave pole (the variance risk premium, `demeter.vol_carry`, qskew -1.18) **the budget balances and the construction works** - the skew-neutral weight is a genuine 40/60 (carry 30% of variance, net skew ~0, poles near-uncorrelated +0.01), and the composite beats both poles on compounded multi-window crisis return (+7.27% vs +4.68% / +5.72%) at half the drawdown. The net-skew-budgeting *mechanism* is supported once the concave pole is genuinely concave; the caveat is that the worst-decile DAILY tail stays trend's job (a short-gamma pole cannot add worst-day protection), so the budget pays *as a portfolio* (compounding + drawdown), not as worst-day insurance. See [[runs/floor/2026-06-13|run diary]].
- [ ] **Convex anomaly vs rented convexity.** Compare crisis-conditional payoff per unit of calm-period bleed for a trend sleeve against a listed-volatility floor on the same universe, as a like-for-like convexity-per-bleed frontier (extends the graveyard VIX-floor result rather than repeating it).
- [ ] **Skew as a family classifier.** Test whether a tail-aware skew estimate plus a convexity-vs-benchmark coefficient (both new Metrics) separate our existing Candidates into the predicted long- and short-gamma poles, and whether that separation predicts realized correlation in the next crisis window better than mechanism labels do.
- [ ] **Carry-skew placement check.** Verify on our universe that the concave sleeve's negative skew appears where theory says (FX and commodity proxies such as UUP, DBC) and is muted in bond/equity carry proxies, before trusting skew as that sleeve's verification signature.
