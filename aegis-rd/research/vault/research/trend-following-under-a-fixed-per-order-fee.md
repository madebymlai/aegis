# Trend Following Under a Fixed Per-Order Fee

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> When the binding execution cost is a fixed fee per order rather than a proportional one, a trend sleeve changes shape twice: the rebalancing control problem switches from smooth partial-adjustment to impulse control (trade rarely, and when you do, jump only part-way to an interior target while letting the book drift in between), and below a capital threshold the same fixed-fee arithmetic makes *buying* a managed-futures wrapper that rebalances futures internally beat self-replicating a multi-sleeve cash-ETF book at all.

Most trend-cost research assumes a proportional cost - a few basis points per dollar traded. A small retail account at a broker like Interactive Brokers faces a different cost shape: a flat minimum per order (EUR 1.25 on the IBKR Ireland Tiered schedule, charged whenever 0.05% of the order value falls below it).[^ibkr] That single change in the cost *function* - from linear-in-size to fixed-per-fill - inverts much of the received wisdom about how to run a trend sleeve, and the inversion is large enough to decide whether the sleeve is viable at all. This note works out the consequences. The convexity mechanics it relies on are taken as given from [[what-makes-a-trend-sleeve-convex]]; the new argument here is about *cost*, not shape.

The empirical anchor is our own. A daily-rebalanced, long-only UCITS-ETF trend book of up to eight names, sized inverse-volatility and run on a EUR 5,000 account, paid roughly EUR 938 a year in fees - about 422 fills at the EUR 1.25 floor - which is **18.8% of NAV per year**, turning a +5.7% gross return into roughly -13% net. Held-out convexity (a magnitude-aware, benchmark-free quarterly tail-payoff score) was *negative*, -0.038 at four names and -0.233 at eight: widening the universe made it worse. The instinct is to read this as "trend does not work at EUR 5k." The cost analysis below says the opposite: it indicts *that configuration* - daily rebalancing times many names, the worst corner of the design space for a fixed fee - not trend at small size.

## Under a fixed fee, only the count of fills matters

A proportional cost scales with how much you trade; a fixed fee scales with how *often*. This is the organizing principle, and it sorts every turnover lever into high- or low-leverage cleanly. Levers that cut the *number* of rebalancing events - trading on a slow clock, holding through small drifts, sticky signals that change state rarely - attack the fee directly. Levers that only shrink the *size* of each trade - precise inverse-vol scaling, smooth partial steps toward a target - barely touch a cost that is constant per order. Much of the classical transaction-cost-aware-momentum toolkit optimizes trade size and is therefore close to irrelevant here; the small-account problem is almost entirely a problem of fill *frequency*.

This also explains why the standard theoretical reference is the wrong one. Gârleanu and Pedersen's "aim in front of the target, trade partially toward the aim" is derived for *quadratic* costs and prescribes a smooth partial adjustment *every* period.[^gp] For a *fixed* cost the optimal policy is impulse control: change the portfolio only finitely often.[^korn] The sharp result is Holden and Holden's: with proportional costs you rebalance from outside a no-trade band to its nearest *edge*,[^leland] but with a *fixed or flat* cost element you rebalance to an *interior* point and never to the full target, precisely because crossing the whole gap is not worth paying the fixed fee for the last increment.[^holden] A design that trades every bar and snaps to the full target - which is what a naive target-weight strategy does - violates both halves of this at once.

## Cadence is most of the cure, and it is nearly free of convexity

The largest single lever is the rebalancing clock, and it is close to costless in the thing we actually care about. Trend convexity only appears when returns are *measured* over a horizon longer than both the signal's half-life and the rebalancing period; on the SG Trend index it is visible at quarterly and annual horizons and absent below a month.[^sepp] A signal with a roughly four-month half-life barely moves between monthly rebalances, so monthly capture of the quarterly-horizon tail is close to daily capture - you give up almost no convexity by slowing down. Hoffstein states the design rule directly: rebalance frequency should be a function of how fast the signal decays against implementation cost, so a slow signal *paired* with infrequent rebalancing is correct, not a compromise.[^hoffstein] The supporting evidence is that gross Sharpe for time-series momentum is similar at daily and weekly rebalancing and decays only slowly thereafter, and decays *faster* for fast signals - slow signals tolerate slow rebalancing with little loss.[^demystify]

The arithmetic is decisive at EUR 5k. The proven daily-times-eight book cost 18.8% of NAV. The same fee model run *monthly on four orthogonal sleeves* drops fills from about 422 a year to about 20, and fee drag to roughly 0.5-0.9% of NAV - a 20-to-30-fold reduction from one parameter. Going to quarterly halves it again at some cost in crisis responsiveness, since crises arrive fast and a slower clock is slower to engage them.[^mancrisis] Monthly is the sweet spot; quarterly is the cost-floor option.

## The hidden firehose: daily drift-correction is both a fill engine and anti-convex

There is a subtle implementation trap that makes the cadence lever invisible until you find it. Under target-percent rebalancing, emitting a finite target weight on *every* bar makes every bar a rebalancing point even when the target has not changed - the engine trims winners and tops up losers daily to hold the weights at target. A no-trade band on the *target* does not stop this: it only stops the target from jumping, while the daily drift-correction to the carried target keeps firing. The fix is to emit "no trade" (a held, drifting book) on non-event bars rather than re-asserting the same target. We verified this mechanism against the execution engine and the library maintainer's guidance; we have not yet measured the magnitude of the fill reduction, so treat the size of the win as a hypothesis and the mechanism as established.

Letting the book drift between trades is not merely cheaper, it is mildly convex. Daily drift-correction *sells* the position that just rose and *buys* the one that just fell - a small mean-reversion bleed against the trend. Holding instead lets winners run, which Hoffstein shows embeds a momentum tilt that amplifies the signal at no cost.[^hoffstein] So the cadence fix and the convexity goal point the same way: trade rarely, and drift in between.

## When you do trade, do not snap to the target

Impulse control prescribes the *shape* of the rare trade. Rebalance to an interior point, not the full target,[^holden] and an asymmetry follows from the convexity mandate rather than the cost: cut a position that the signal has turned against *in full* (preserve the de-risk that makes trend a lookback straddle, per [[what-makes-a-trend-sleeve-convex]]), but add to a strengthening position only *part-way*, which both saves fills and ramps into winners slowly the way pyramiding manufactures the right tail. The no-trade band itself is the practitioner form of this: Carver's position buffering trades only to the *edge* of a buffer and "reduces turnover without affecting performance,"[^carver] and Leland's band cuts turnover by roughly half versus calendar rebalancing.[^leland] Novy-Marx and Velikov find the single most effective cost mitigant across anomalies is a buy/hold spread - a stricter threshold to *establish* a position than to *keep* it[^novymarx] - which is the same idea as making entry harder than holding.

## The signal should be sticky, not twitchy

Endpoint-to-endpoint momentum (price now versus price *n* days ago) flips sign more often near zero than a smoothed estimate does, and under a fixed fee every spurious flip is a paid round trip. Two construction choices reduce the flips. First, hysteresis: gate on two thresholds (enter long above one, return to flat only below a lower one) so the state latches and only rare crossings cost anything - the Donchian breakout used as the base layer by many CTAs is exactly this, structurally hysteretic, parameter-insensitive, with a bounded-loss-in-chop, ride-the-breakout profile that is mechanically convex.[^donchian] Second, a smoother estimator: an exponential moving-average-crossover ensemble averaged over several speeds, or a least-squares slope of log price, changes sign less than the endpoint ratio. Baltas and Kosowski find a smoother trend rule cuts portfolio turnover by about 24% versus the raw sign rule with no significant Sharpe loss, and an efficient volatility estimator saves a further 13-25%.[^demystify] Both choices cut fills *and* steady conviction at the slow horizon where the convexity lives.

## Below a capital threshold, buy the convexity instead of building it

The fixed fee draws a line in capital. Modeling per-fill cost as `max(EUR 1.25, 0.05% x notional)`, the floor binds whenever an order is smaller than EUR 2,500; above that the cost converges to the 0.05% proportional rate. For the recommended slow, monthly, four-sleeve book - whose fill *count* is set by the signal, not by NAV - fee drag is about 0.6% of NAV at EUR 5k, 0.3% at EUR 10k, and about 0.1% by EUR 25-50k. The reframing matters: the failed daily-times-eight book genuinely needed roughly EUR 100k to dilute its EUR 938 fee below 1%, but the *well-built* book clears the floor at around EUR 10k and is essentially fee-efficient by EUR 25k. At EUR 5k you do not need more capital; you need fewer sleeves and a slower clock.

| EUR 50k | 0.05% dominates | ~20 | ~EUR 50 | **~0.10%** |

The recommended book's fill *count* is set by the signal, not by NAV, so the curve is just the EUR 1.25 floor amortized over a growing base. The contrast is the failed daily-times-eight book on the same fee model: 18.8% at EUR 5k, 9.4% at EUR 10k, 3.8% at EUR 25k, 1.9% at EUR 50k - a curve that genuinely needs roughly EUR 100k to fall below 1%. Same broker, same instruments, two cadence/breadth choices apart, and a ~30-fold gap in the small-account tax.

There is a cleaner move still. A UCITS managed-futures ETF rebalances a diversified long/short futures book *internally* at institutional cost, so its turnover never touches your account - you pay one entry ticket and a flat expense ratio. The iMGP DBi Managed Futures UCITS ETF (listed March 2025, EUR line LU2951555403, accumulating, 0.75% all-in) replicates a CTA across equities, bonds, commodities and FX, including the short leg a long-only cash-ETF book cannot take; the underlying strategy returned about +23% in 2022.[^imgp] At EUR 5k its 0.75% expense ratio is competitive with the DIY book's residual fee drag *once you add the sleeves real convexity needs*, and it delivers the breadth for free. This inverts our "widening the universe hurt" result: breadth hurt because each added cash-ETF name added daily fills, but inside a futures wrapper breadth is costless to the holder, so the right response to "breadth helps convexity but costs turnover" is to move the breadth inside a wrapper that rebalances for nothing. The honest caveats: it is swap-based (counterparty exposure), small and new (about EUR 60m AUM, so check the live spread at your size), and the +23% is the *strategy's* 2016-onward record, not the wrapper's short live history.

## The short leg, and why the fee floor decides how to take it

A long-only sleeve has a structurally asymmetric crisis profile: it owns the deflation-crisis quadrant (long duration and gold rallied in 2008 and 2020) but captures essentially none of the inflation-crisis quadrant, because it can only go to cash when bonds fall. 2022 is the proof - trend's banner year was a *short-bond* event, driven by short rates, long energy and a long dollar; Man Group attributes that year's positive bond contribution explicitly to *short* bond exposure, the opposite of the long-bond crisis alpha of prior crashes, and AlphaSimplex notes trend was structurally long bonds for decades and so suffers in short, sharp rate corrections.[^man2022][^alphasimplex] The short leg is the missing half of the convexity, which is why a long-only trend book realizes near-zero skew (the constraint is mapped in [[the-orthogonal-non-equity-trend-universe]]).

The fixed fee, plus the UCITS borrow constraint, dictates the wrapper. Genuine short borrow is not realistically available to EU retail for these ETFs. Buy-and-hold inverse ETFs decay from daily resetting, but the decay literature cuts in trend's favor: the constant-leverage drag is of order the realized variance over the holding period and bites in *choppy* markets, while in *sustained trends* daily-reset compounding *adds* to returns[^levetf] - and a trend-gated short is held only when the signal is short and trending, which is exactly when the decay helps rather than hurts. So the short leg is worth taking, but at EUR 5k the clean way is inside the managed-futures wrapper (real futures, no decay, sized by professionals); the defensible DIY version is a *single* -1x government-bond UCITS ETF, gated strictly on the slow signal and never a -2x for holding, where the decay-cost scales with the leverage times leverage-minus-one. Resurrecting a full DIY long/short cash-ETF book is the worst of both worlds - the fill floor plus our earlier net-negative long/short result on this substrate. This connects to the episodic-overlay logic in [[the-ucits-constrained-tail-sleeve]].

## Dominated execution choices at small size

A few broker-level choices are not trade-offs at this NAV, they are simply correct. Tiered pricing beats Fixed because the binding cost is the EUR 1.25 Tiered minimum rather than the EUR 3-4 Fixed minimum.[^ibkr] Accumulating share classes remove dividend-reinvestment fills and withholding-reclaim friction. Fractional shares do not lower the per-order floor, but they let the whole account deploy precisely across sleeves and avoid the forced lumpy rebalances a high share price would otherwise create. EUR-denominated lines avoid the per-conversion FX cost USD lines carry. None of these cut the *count* of fills, but each removes an incidental one.

Two choices are NAV-conditioned reversals - correct at scale, wrong here - and they share a tell: their cost is proportional, so it is cheap when amortized over a large base and ruinous under a flat fee. Volatility targeting reliably trims left-tail severity and lifts risk-adjusted return for equities, but its benefit for bonds, commodities and FX is negligible while its cost is a constant stream of rescaling trades;[^harvey] at a fixed fee that stream is pure waste, so vol-targeting belongs off (or coarsely band-gated) below roughly EUR 25k and reconsidered only once the floor stops binding. Overlapping-portfolio tranching - splitting the book into sub-portfolios rebalanced on staggered days - is the right cure for rebalance-timing luck at scale,[^hoffstein] but it *fragments* each rebalance into several smaller orders, every one of which hits the EUR 1.25 floor, so at EUR 5k it multiplies the fixed cost and should be avoided. Keep tranching's *drift* benefit, which is free in a hold-between-trades design, and discard its *frequency*.

The cleanest expression of the whole argument is a *minimum-order-size* gate: skip any rebalance whose traded notional is too small for the move to be worth the fee. This is the interior-point prescription[^holden] reduced to one rule - do not trade unless the move is worth a euro and a quarter - and it caps the worst small-account failure mode, paying a flat fee to shift a few euros of weight.

## Limitations

- The 18.8%, -0.038 and -0.233 figures are our own measurements on one universe and period (2018-2025, held-out rolling splits); they establish that the daily-many-names corner fails, not the precise viability of the recommended corner, which is a fee *projection* not yet run end-to-end.
- The impulse-rebalancing fill reduction is asserted from a verified mechanism, not yet measured; the order-of-magnitude claim must survive an A/B before it is trusted.
- "Slow is more convex" is contested and we do not silently pick a side. CFM shows that slowing a system, capping the forecast, and diversifying the pool each *reduce* convexity measured against a single underlying,[^cfm] and Man shows *faster* signals give better crisis alpha.[^mancrisis] Slow wins *here* on cost and horizon-fit, not on raw convexity-per-underlying; if NAV rises and the fee constraint relaxes, re-introducing a faster speed would raise convexity. The speed choice is NAV-conditioned, not absolute.
- The managed-futures ETF route carries swap-counterparty and new-fund risk, and its headline 2022 number is the underlying strategy, not the live UCITS wrapper.
- Skewness and tail-convexity estimates are statistically unstable by construction (few tail observations); treat all rankings as directional.
- Several load-bearing practitioner sources (CFM, Man, AQR) run or sell trend products; the cost-control results (Holden, Leland, Novy-Marx/Velikov, Baltas/Kosowski) are the better-insulated claims.

## Strategy hypotheses this could seed

- [ ] Impulse-control rebalancing - a strategy that holds (emits no-trade) between events, where an event is a cadence tick OR a no-trade-band breach OR a trend-state change, and on an event moves only part-way to an interior target (cut losers in full, add to winners partially) - cuts fills by roughly an order of magnitude *and* holds-or-raises net quarterly convexity versus the daily-rebalanced champion. Pre-register: net (post EUR 1.25) quarterly convexity payoff >= champion AND fills/yr strictly lower.
- [ ] Monthly (not daily) rebalancing of a four-month-half-life signal on four orthogonal sleeves lands fee drag below 1% of a EUR 5k NAV while holding quarterly convexity within noise of the daily book.
- [ ] Hysteresis state - a Schmitt-trigger on the trend score, or a new Donchian-breakout state indicator (enter on an N-day high, exit to flat on a shorter M-day low) - removes near-zero whipsaw, cutting fills and adding a bounded-loss convex shape versus the continuous sign gate.
- [ ] A smoother estimator (EWMA-crossover ensemble or log-price regression slope, drop-in for the endpoint momentum score) cuts state changes ~20% with no quarterly-convexity loss.
- [ ] A UCITS managed-futures ETF (iMGP DBi) beats the best DIY long-only cash-ETF book on net-of-fees quarterly convexity at EUR 5k - the buy-versus-build crossover.
- [ ] A single trend-gated -1x government-bond UCITS ETF adds the inflation-crisis convexity a long-only book lacks, net of daily-reset decay, when held only while its slow signal is short-and-trending. **PARKED 2026-07-03 ([[runs/atalanta/2026-07-03]]) - not testable live, and dominated in principle.** (1) The clean -1x US-Treasury UCITS this hypothesis assumes is **extinct**: Lyxor/Amundi E571/E572 were liquidated (LEI lapsed 2021, AT/CH/DE/LU-only distribution), and an IBKR account probe finds **no -1x US-Treasury line carried** - only -3x US-Treasury (UL3S/3TYS) and -1x EUR-sovereign (DXSV) inverse UCITS are tradable, and none is both US-Treasury and -1x. (2) A decay-physics pass makes the point moot regardless: drag ~= leverage^2 * 1/2 * variance, so a real **borrow-gated short (decay-free, exact underlying, holdable) dominates any inverse ETF**, and -3x is structurally wrong for a weeks-to-months hold (9x the -1x drag, worst in the high-vol bond-selloff it exists for). Verdict: the borrow-gated IDTL short (+ short_cap) stays the champion realization; a -1x **DXSV** EUR-sovereign sleeve is the only live fallback, reserved for a loss of IDTL borrow.
- [ ] Drop-one-name attribution on convexity-per-fill: only the minimal orthogonal set (global equity, long duration, gold, broad commodity) earns its EUR 1.25 at EUR 5k; each correlated extra sleeve lowers net convexity. Pairs with [[the-orthogonal-non-equity-trend-universe]].
- [ ] A minimum-order-size gate (skip any rebalance whose traded notional is below a small multiple of the EUR 1.25 floor) raises net convexity at EUR 5k by removing sub-economic fills, with no loss of crisis capture.
- [ ] Turning volatility targeting off (or coarsely band-gating it) holds quarterly convexity while cutting fills versus continuous inverse-vol rescaling, on a bond/commodity-tilted book at EUR 5k.
