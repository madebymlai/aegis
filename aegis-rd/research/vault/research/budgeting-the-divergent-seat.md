# Budgeting the Divergent Seat

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> "What is the best divergent payoff a UCITS/IBKR-IE book can own" is the wrong question. The newest work says why: hedge quality is not one number but several near-independent axes (conditional convexity, tail-event reliability, non-stress carry, drawdown persistence), and a divergent sleeve's worth is its *overlay capacity* - how much a risk budget can hold of it through the cycle, set by its left-tail conditional correlation rather than its standalone Sharpe. On both measures no single mechanism wins, which is why the seat's earlier selection sweep could not crown a challenger. But the answer is not to pile several convex instruments into one seat either: each sleeve must own a *distinct* instrument set, so the divergent seat cannot double-book a gold sliver or a long-VIX overlay it would be *buying while carry sells it*. The axes are instead covered *across* the roster's tiers - slow trend (reliability, persistence, positive carry), the tail sleeve (the single-day gap), carry (the concave side) - each sized by left-tail correlation. The one genuinely new, instrument-orthogonal line the divergent seat itself can budget in is a winter-cover complement (cross-asset value or economic trend) on its own signals, to pay down the trendless-winter bleed price trend cannot design away. And once the seat is budgeted, running it on a small account inverts twice under a fixed per-order fee: the rebalancing problem becomes impulse control (trade rarely, move part-way to an interior target, drift in between), and below a capital threshold the same fixed-fee arithmetic makes *buying* a managed-futures wrapper that rebalances futures internally beat self-replicating the book at all.

An earlier selection sweep (where-convexity-still-lives, retired; its surviving analysis now lives in [[what-makes-a-trend-sleeve-convex]] and [[is-the-trend-premium-decaying]]) answered the seat's selection question - among trend, bought convexity, cross-sectional and network momentum, macro-divergence and scarce assets, which single mechanism *wins* the divergent slot - and the answer was that none displaces slow trend outright: bought convexity is the tail tier's job, the concave income is carry's, and the convex non-trend systematics are things to combine with trend, not replace it. That verdict has a sequel the selection frame cannot see. If no single source wins, the seat is not a mechanism to be chosen but a *budget* to be sized, and the 2024-2026 literature has quietly rebuilt the vocabulary for sizing it. This article is that sequel, in two halves: first how to compose the seat (the budget), then how to run it when the binding execution cost is a fixed fee per order rather than a proportional one (the mechanics).

## Hedge quality is not one number

The selection frame implicitly ranks candidates on a single "how convex is it" scale. The sharpest recent theory says that scale does not exist. Noguer i Alonso and Al Fallouji, modelling a put sleeve and a trend sleeve together in a continuous-time CVaR framework, decompose hedge quality into four axes that do not move together: *conditional convexity* (how sharply the payoff bends in a shock), *tail-event reliability* (how often it actually delivers across events), *non-stress carry* (what it costs to hold in calm), and *drawdown persistence* (whether it keeps paying through a protracted decline).[^cvar] Trend and options sit at opposite corners of this space. The put has the highest conditional convexity (it reprices on jump impact) but the worst carry; trend has the highest tail-event reliability and the best carry but is late to the first shock, because its signal must cross zero before it is positioned. Their optimum is neither pure sleeve but an interior put-plus-trend hybrid - in their Monte Carlo the equal-weight hybrid cut tail-CVaR to 0.28 against 0.47 for trend-only and 0.63 for the put-only, while keeping skew near the trend leg's +1.2.[^cvar] The result matters less for its exact numbers - it is a stylized model - than for its shape: a payoff can be excellent on one axis and poor on another, so "best divergent payoff" is a category error the moment more than one axis binds. This is the theoretical form of the same fact our own metric already half-encodes, measuring the trend pole's intrinsic own-horizon skew rather than a single equity-beta convexity ([[verifying-strategy-family-membership]], [[what-makes-a-trend-sleeve-convex]]).

The four axes also explain, rather than merely assert, the division of labor the roster already runs. Trend's weakness is the *first* axis at short timescales (it sleeps through a one-day gap) and its strength is the *fourth* (it harvests protracted bears); the tail overlay is the mirror. Reading the seat as a single scalar hides that the two are complements on orthogonal axes, not rivals on one.

## A diversifier's worth is its overlay capacity, not its Sharpe

The second reframing is about sizing, and it is the more actionable of the two. The instinct is to rank convex sleeves by standalone Sharpe and hold the best. Quantica's overlay-capacity work inverts that. Under a fixed marginal-risk budget - "you may add at most +1% to a 100%-equity book's volatility" - the binding constraint on how much of a diversifier you can hold is its *correlation to equities in the left tail*, not its Sharpe.[^quanticastack] On that measure the ranking scrambles: an equity-market-neutral sleeve with a standalone Sharpe near 0.87 admits only a ~10-25% overlay because it is not negatively correlated when it matters, while trend at a standalone Sharpe near 0.40 admits a ~48-56% overlay and has held that capacity stable over the last decade, precisely because its left-tail correlation to equities is negative.[^quanticastack] Gold admits ~38-40%, and Treasuries have fallen from ~58% to ~34% as their crisis diversification eroded after 2022.[^quanticastack] Long-vol scores the highest raw convexity of all but the lowest deployable weight, because its negative carry makes any large allocation too expensive to run.[^quanticastack] The lesson is blunt: a divergent sleeve is worth what a risk budget can *hold* of it through the cycle, and a lower-Sharpe, reliably-crisis-negative sleeve beats a higher-Sharpe one that goes flat or positive in the drawdown.

Read together, the two reframings convert the seat from a selection into a construction. Hedge quality is multi-axis, so you want more than one source to cover more than one axis; overlay capacity is set by left-tail correlation, so you size each source by how much a budget can carry, not by its Sharpe. That is a budget, and it is why the honest answer to the issue's question is a small stack rather than a winner.

## Covering the axes across sleeves, not inside one seat

The reframings do not license stacking every convex instrument into the trend sleeve, and a hard book-design rule blocks the naive reading: each sleeve owns a *distinct* instrument set, both to keep the sleeves' realized streams orthogonal and to stop the book paying two spreads to hold offsetting positions. That rule turns "budget the divergent seat" into a question about the *roster*, not a shopping list for one sleeve. Priced on hedge-quality axis, overlay capacity, and the UCITS constraint, the axes are covered as follows - and only the last line is a genuinely new addition *inside* the divergent seat.

*Slow price trend is the backbone*, and remains so for the reason [[convexity-as-the-axis-of-strategy-diversification]] gives: it is the one convex payoff the market *pays* you to hold rather than charging you for, an anomaly rather than an insurance premium.[^lemp] That gives it the best non-stress-carry axis and the highest stable overlay capacity, and its durability now has fresh support - crowded momentum shows *lower* crash probability than uncrowded, the opposite of the crowding fear, because more participants reinforcing a directional move is stabilising for a trend position.[^crowd] It is UCITS-native either self-built from cross-asset ETFs or bought wholesale through a managed-futures UCITS ETF (the buy-versus-build arithmetic is worked out below; the universe in [[the-orthogonal-non-equity-trend-universe]]). Its known deficits - the trendless-winter bleed and the single-day gap - are what the other lines cover, but crucially *not all inside this seat*.

*Gold's left-tail convexity is real, and it is already budgeted elsewhere.* The 2025 evidence is that gold's negative equity correlation *rises* as risk assets sell off - convex in the left tail rather than merely uncorrelated on average - and unlike Treasuries it has kept that property since 2022, which is why its overlay capacity sits above duration's.[^gold][^quanticastack] But the book already owns that convexity twice over: the trend backbone holds gold (a UCITS physical gold ETC) *directionally* whenever gold trends, and the tail sleeve's physical fallback is a Swiss-franc-and-gold-led haven basket ([[the-ucits-constrained-tail-sleeve]]). Under the one-instrument-per-sleeve rule an always-on gold sliver bolted onto the divergent seat would double-book the tail sleeve's haven and the trend leg's own position, so gold does not earn a *separate* divergent-seat line - its convexity is captured where it already lives, and the design task is to size that existing exposure by its left-tail correlation, not to re-add it.

*The single-day gap is the tail sleeve's axis, and it is emphatically not a long-VIX overlay in the trend seat.* The physics is not negotiable: a single-asset trend P&L equals the long-horizon minus the short-horizon realized variance, a variance swap that pays the short-term realized variance as its entry cost, so a shock completing inside one day is paid by an option and slept through by trend.[^cfmvar] Covering it means buying convexity and paying a premium - and three constraints stack to keep that line out of the divergent seat. First and decisively, instrument-orthogonality: the book's variance exposure already belongs to the carry sleeve, which *sells* variance to harvest the risk premium ([[carry-as-the-short-gamma-income-pole]]), so a long-VIX overlay in the trend seat would be buying the very instrument carry is short, netting to a spread-paying wash and breaking the non-overlap rule. Second, cost: an always-on short-term VIX-ETP floor is a demonstrated dead end - its roll decay is an equilibrium premium too dear to carry at any crash-moving weight (see the hypotheses below). Third, UCITS: the cheapest gap instruments - VIX and VSTOXX ETNs - are unsecured notes, KID-blocked for EEA retail and out on tradability grounds, and OTC variance swaps are not retail-holdable.[^rebuffed] So the gap axis is delegated to the separate tail sleeve, cost-budgeted and sourced from the physical haven basket, not folded into the trend pole.

*A cross-asset value or economic-trend leg is the one genuinely new, instrument-orthogonal line the divergent seat can add.* It is orthogonal because it runs on a different signal (relative fundamentals or price-vs-fundamental dislocation), not on a convex instrument some other sleeve already owns. Cross-asset value is time-series trend's structural mirror - value and momentum run about -0.60 correlated within and across asset classes, so a value leg pays roughly when trend fades, by construction - and the corroboration is peer-reviewed rather than practitioner, which the vault's earlier LOIM read lacked.[^vamom] Economic (macro-fundamental) trend corroborates from the other side: it correlates only ~0.3-0.4 with price trend and is positive in the fundamental-catalyst drawdowns where price trend is starved.[^econtrend] The honest limits are three and each is load-bearing. Value covers *trendless-chop* winters but co-crashes in *acute liquidity* events - value, momentum, carry and defensive all broke down together in February-March 2020.[^styleslump] Much of what is sold as "global macro divergence" decomposes into trend plus equity beta once you regress it out, so a candidate must show residual non-trend, non-equity alpha before it earns a budget line.[^macroalpha] And the UCITS vehicles that exist blend carry into the same sleeve, which risks smuggling the concave pole into the divergent seat and eroding the low-correlation mandate the divergent seat owes the credit-carry pole - carry itself is a concave, co-crashing premium, not a divergent payer, so a carry-dominant "diversifier" fails the seat on shape.[^macroalpha]

## There is no free convexity, so the budget is the answer

The tempting objection is that a cleverly-engineered single sleeve could still dominate the budget. Every rigorous source in the sweep closes that door from a different side, and their agreement is the strongest claim here. Bouchaud's variance-swap identity says you *choose which premium you pay* - the whipsaw and chop bleed of harvested convexity via trend, or the option premium of paid-for convexity via long-vol - but never whether.[^cfmvar] The primary-grade decay evidence sharpens which premium is cheaper over time: index-option alphas have become statistically indistinguishable from zero over the past fifteen years, and the historical option overpricing was an intermediary-frictions phenomenon now eroding as households increasingly *sell* crash insurance through structured products - so the paid-for side is thinning while trend's harvested convexity, an anomaly rather than a friction, is the more durable premium.[^vrpdecay] And the live proof that no combination escapes the winter is a manager running the exact long-vol-plus-trend construction this article recommends: One River's Risk Responders posted its largest and longest drawdown ever in 2025, in a trendless tape with no panic, and its own research warns that shortening the models to defend the initial drawdown carries a meaningful long-term cost.[^oneriver] The winter cannot be designed out; its depth can be bounded by spreading the payoff across axes that fail at different times.

That is what a budget buys. The convergent recommendation across otherwise-competing houses is the same shape - harvest cheap convexity through slow-to-medium trend, and cap the residual with a small always-on paid-for overlay - stated by Man (trend plus a ~25% options overlay replicates put downside convexity without the carry), by CFM (a 10% defensive sleeve of short-term trend plus a liquid VIX-future long-vol leg), and by the quantified diversification-term-structure result that an equal blend of trend, bonds and gold reached a Sharpe of 0.72 against 0.49 for equity alone and beat any single divergent sleeve.[^manconvex][^cumulus][^termstructure] None of these is a silver bullet; all of them are budgets. One caveat separates their form from ours: these houses run the overlay *inside a single product*, so their VIX-future or options leg sits beside their trend leg in one book. A roster split into distinct sleeves under the one-instrument rule covers the same axes *across* sleeves - the gap-hedge instrument is the tail sleeve's, the short-variance premium is carry's - so the illustrations validate the budget principle while their specific long-vol instrument stays out of the divergent seat. The peer-reviewed capstone reads the same way: overlaying trend *and* an option tail hedge on a full-equity book improved every one of nine crisis episodes tested and added about 0.25% per month after factor controls, cutting max drawdown from roughly -34% to -19% - two axes covered, neither sleeve asked to be the whole answer.[^schwalbach]

## The fee floor inverts the seat's mechanics

The budget says what the seat should hold; the cost structure of a small account decides how it can be run. Most trend-cost research assumes a proportional cost - a few basis points per dollar traded. A small retail account faces a different cost shape: a flat minimum per order (EUR 1.25 on the IBKR Ireland Tiered schedule, charged whenever 0.05% of the order value falls below it).[^ibkr] That single change in the cost *function* - from linear-in-size to fixed-per-fill - inverts much of the received wisdom about how to run a trend sleeve, and the inversion is large enough to decide whether the sleeve is viable at all.

The empirical anchor is a daily-rebalanced, long-only UCITS-ETF trend book of up to eight names, sized inverse-volatility, run at EUR 5,000: fee drag came to **18.8% of NAV per year**, and held-out convexity (a magnitude-aware, benchmark-free quarterly tail-payoff score) was *negative* - -0.038 at four names and -0.233 at eight, widening the universe made it worse. The instinct is to read this as "trend does not work at small size." The cost analysis below says the opposite: it indicts *that configuration* - daily rebalancing times many names, the worst corner of the design space for a fixed fee - not trend at small size.

A proportional cost scales with how much you trade; a fixed fee scales with how *often*. This is the organizing principle, and it sorts every turnover lever into high- or low-leverage cleanly. Levers that cut the *number* of rebalancing events - trading on a slow clock, holding through small drifts, sticky signals that change state rarely - attack the fee directly. Levers that only shrink the *size* of each trade barely touch a cost that is constant per order. Much of the classical transaction-cost-aware-momentum toolkit optimizes trade size and is therefore close to irrelevant here; the small-account problem is almost entirely a problem of fill *frequency*. This also explains why the standard theoretical reference is the wrong one. Gârleanu and Pedersen's "aim in front of the target, trade partially toward the aim" is derived for *quadratic* costs and prescribes a smooth partial adjustment *every* period.[^gp] For a *fixed* cost the optimal policy is impulse control: change the portfolio only finitely often.[^korn] The sharp result is Holden and Holden's: with proportional costs you rebalance from outside a no-trade band to its nearest *edge*,[^leland] but with a *fixed or flat* cost element you rebalance to an *interior* point and never to the full target, precisely because crossing the whole gap is not worth paying the fixed fee for the last increment.[^holden] A design that trades every bar and snaps to the full target - which is what a naive target-weight strategy does - violates both halves of this at once.

## Cadence is most of the cure, and it is nearly free of convexity

The largest single lever is the rebalancing clock, and it is close to costless in the thing we actually care about. Trend convexity only appears when returns are *measured* over a horizon longer than both the signal's half-life and the rebalancing period; on the SG Trend index it is visible at quarterly and annual horizons and absent below a month.[^sepp] A signal with a roughly four-month half-life barely moves between monthly rebalances, so monthly capture of the quarterly-horizon tail is close to daily capture - you give up almost no convexity by slowing down. Hoffstein states the design rule directly: rebalance frequency should be a function of how fast the signal decays against implementation cost, so a slow signal *paired* with infrequent rebalancing is correct, not a compromise.[^hoffstein] The supporting evidence is that gross Sharpe for time-series momentum is similar at daily and weekly rebalancing and decays only slowly thereafter, and decays *faster* for fast signals - slow signals tolerate slow rebalancing with little loss.[^demystify]

The arithmetic is decisive at small size. The measured daily-times-eight book cost 18.8% of NAV. The same fee model run *monthly on four orthogonal sleeves* drops fills from about 422 a year to about 20, and fee drag to roughly 0.5-0.9% of NAV - a 20-to-30-fold reduction from one parameter. Going to quarterly halves it again at some cost in crisis responsiveness, since crises arrive fast and a slower clock is slower to engage them.[^mancrisis] Monthly is the sweet spot; quarterly is the cost-floor option.

One implementation trap hides the cadence lever until it is found. Under target-percent rebalancing, emitting a finite target weight on *every* bar makes every bar a rebalancing point even when the target has not changed - the engine trims winners and tops up losers daily to hold the weights at target, and a no-trade band on the *target* does not stop the daily drift-correction to the carried target from firing. The fix is to emit "no trade" (a held, drifting book) on non-event bars rather than re-asserting the same target. Letting the book drift between trades is not merely cheaper, it is mildly convex: daily drift-correction *sells* the position that just rose and *buys* the one that just fell - a small mean-reversion bleed against the trend - while holding lets winners run, which embeds a momentum tilt that amplifies the signal at no cost.[^hoffstein] So the cadence fix and the convexity goal point the same way: trade rarely, and drift in between.

## When you do trade: interior targets and sticky signals

Impulse control prescribes the *shape* of the rare trade. Rebalance to an interior point, not the full target,[^holden] and an asymmetry follows from the convexity mandate rather than the cost: cut a position that the signal has turned against *in full* (preserve the de-risk that makes trend a lookback straddle, per [[what-makes-a-trend-sleeve-convex]]), but add to a strengthening position only *part-way*, which both saves fills and ramps into winners slowly the way pyramiding manufactures the right tail. The no-trade band itself is the practitioner form of this: Carver's position buffering trades only to the *edge* of a buffer and "reduces turnover without affecting performance,"[^carver] and Leland's band cuts turnover by roughly half versus calendar rebalancing.[^leland] Novy-Marx and Velikov find the single most effective cost mitigant across anomalies is a buy/hold spread - a stricter threshold to *establish* a position than to *keep* it[^novymarx] - which is the same idea as making entry harder than holding.

The signal should be sticky, not twitchy. Endpoint-to-endpoint momentum (price now versus price *n* days ago) flips sign more often near zero than a smoothed estimate does, and under a fixed fee every spurious flip is a paid round trip. Two construction choices reduce the flips. First, hysteresis: gate on two thresholds (enter long above one, return to flat only below a lower one) so the state latches and only rare crossings cost anything - the Donchian breakout used as the base layer by many CTAs is exactly this, structurally hysteretic, parameter-insensitive, with a bounded-loss-in-chop, ride-the-breakout profile that is mechanically convex.[^donchian] Second, a smoother estimator: an exponential moving-average-crossover ensemble averaged over several speeds, or a least-squares slope of log price, changes sign less than the endpoint ratio. Baltas and Kosowski find a smoother trend rule cuts portfolio turnover by about 24% versus the raw sign rule with no significant Sharpe loss, and an efficient volatility estimator saves a further 13-25%.[^demystify] Both choices cut fills *and* steady conviction at the slow horizon where the convexity lives.

## Below a capital threshold, buy the convexity instead of building it

The fixed fee draws a line in capital. Modeling per-fill cost as `max(EUR 1.25, 0.05% x notional)`, the floor binds whenever an order is smaller than EUR 2,500; above that the cost converges to the proportional rate. For the recommended slow, monthly, four-sleeve book - whose fill *count* is set by the signal, not by NAV - fee drag is about 0.6% of NAV at EUR 5k, 0.3% at EUR 10k, and about 0.1% by EUR 25-50k. The reframing matters: the failed daily-times-eight book genuinely needed roughly EUR 100k to dilute its fee below 1%, but the *well-built* book clears the floor at around EUR 10k and is essentially fee-efficient by EUR 25k. At EUR 5k you do not need more capital; you need fewer sleeves and a slower clock.

There is a cleaner move still. A UCITS managed-futures ETF rebalances a diversified long/short futures book *internally* at institutional cost, so its turnover never touches your account - you pay one entry ticket and a flat expense ratio. The iMGP DBi Managed Futures UCITS ETF (listed March 2025, EUR line LU2951555403, accumulating, 0.75% all-in) replicates a CTA across equities, bonds, commodities and FX, including the short leg a long-only cash-ETF book cannot take; the underlying strategy returned about +23% in 2022.[^imgp] At small size its 0.75% expense ratio is competitive with the DIY book's residual fee drag *once you add the sleeves real convexity needs*, and it delivers the breadth for free. This inverts the "widening the universe hurt" finding: breadth hurt because each added cash-ETF name added daily fills, but inside a futures wrapper breadth is costless to the holder, so the right response to "breadth helps convexity but costs turnover" is to move the breadth inside a wrapper that rebalances for nothing. The honest caveats: it is swap-based (counterparty exposure), small and new (about EUR 60m AUM, so check the live spread at your size), and the +23% is the *strategy's* 2016-onward record, not the wrapper's short live history.

The wrapper now has a live year to judge (as of 2026-07-06), and it delivered the divergent shape: the EUR line printed roughly +24.7% over its first twelve months at a ~-6.8% maximum drawdown and ~0.20 equity beta, tracking its US parent (which runs ~9.1%/yr since 2019 at ~0.85 correlation to the SG CTA index) within about one percent.[^dbmflive] But the *build* case did not weaken. A neutral out-of-sample replication of the SG CTA index fails to beat a plain trend model and breaks down in stress,[^soderbargfee] and the closest pure-price-trend UCITS peer - Winton Trend, ~0.99% OCF - has run only about a 0.5 live Sharpe. So the wrapper buys breadth, the short leg, and single-manager-risk removal, not obviously better net convexity than a competent self-build, which is why the seat's build-vs-buy verdict is a pre-registered head-to-head against this exact EUR line (the hypotheses below), not a default to buy. One route the wrapper does *not* open for EEA retail: a capital-efficient "return-stacked" trend overlay - the US RSST/RSBT stacking ETFs are KID-blocked, and the only UCITS return-stacking ETFs stack government bonds, not managed futures, so any trend-on-top-of-core stack must be assembled by hand from a standalone trend sleeve plus a separate efficient-core holding.[^retstack]

## The short leg, and why the fee floor decides how to take it

A long-only sleeve has a structurally asymmetric crisis profile: it owns the deflation-crisis quadrant (long duration and gold rallied in 2008 and 2020) but captures essentially none of the inflation-crisis quadrant, because it can only go to cash when bonds fall. 2022 is the proof - trend's banner year was a *short-bond* event, driven by short rates, long energy and a long dollar; Man Group attributes that year's positive bond contribution explicitly to *short* bond exposure, the opposite of the long-bond crisis alpha of prior crashes, and AlphaSimplex notes trend was structurally long bonds for decades and so suffers in short, sharp rate corrections.[^man2022][^alphasimplex] The short leg is the missing half of the convexity, which is why a long-only trend book realizes near-zero skew (the constraint is mapped in [[the-orthogonal-non-equity-trend-universe]]).

The fixed fee, plus the UCITS borrow constraint, dictates the wrapper. Genuine short borrow is not realistically available to EU retail for these ETFs. Buy-and-hold inverse ETFs decay from daily resetting, but the decay literature cuts in trend's favor: the constant-leverage drag is of order the realized variance over the holding period and bites in *choppy* markets, while in *sustained trends* daily-reset compounding *adds* to returns[^levetf] - and a trend-gated short is held only when the signal is short and trending, which is exactly when the decay helps rather than hurts. So the short leg is worth taking, but at small size the clean way is inside the managed-futures wrapper (real futures, no decay, sized by professionals); the defensible DIY version is a *single* -1x government-bond UCITS ETF, gated strictly on the slow signal and never a -2x for holding, where the decay-cost scales with the leverage times leverage-minus-one. Resurrecting a full DIY long/short cash-ETF book is the worst of both worlds - the fill floor plus the earlier net-negative long/short result on this substrate. This connects to the episodic-overlay logic in [[the-ucits-constrained-tail-sleeve]].

## Dominated execution choices at small size

A few broker-level choices are simply correct at this size, not trade-offs: tiered pricing over fixed (the binding cost is the smaller per-order minimum), accumulating share classes (no dividend-reinvestment fills), fractional shares (precise deployment without lumpy forced rebalances), and base-currency lines (no per-conversion FX cost).[^ibkr] None of these cut the *count* of fills, but each removes an incidental one.

Two choices are NAV-conditioned reversals - correct at scale, wrong here - and they share a tell: their cost is proportional, so it is cheap when amortized over a large base and ruinous under a flat fee. Volatility targeting reliably trims left-tail severity and lifts risk-adjusted return for equities, but its benefit for bonds, commodities and FX is negligible while its cost is a constant stream of rescaling trades;[^harvey] at a fixed fee that stream is pure waste, so vol-targeting belongs off (or coarsely band-gated) below roughly EUR 25k and reconsidered only once the floor stops binding. Overlapping-portfolio tranching - splitting the book into sub-portfolios rebalanced on staggered days - is the right cure for rebalance-timing luck at scale,[^hoffstein] but it *fragments* each rebalance into several smaller orders, every one of which hits the per-order floor, so at small size it multiplies the fixed cost and should be avoided. Keep tranching's *drift* benefit, which is free in a hold-between-trades design, and discard its *frequency*.

The cleanest expression of the whole argument is a *minimum-order-size* gate: skip any rebalance whose traded notional is too small for the move to be worth the fee. This is the interior-point prescription[^holden] reduced to one rule - do not trade unless the move is worth the minimum - and it caps the worst small-account failure mode, paying a flat fee to shift a few euros of weight.

## Limitations

- The two budget reframings rest on a small number of sources each. The multi-axis hedge-quality result is one stylized continuous-time model[^cvar] and the overlay-capacity result is one manager's closed-form-plus-empirics with a clear conflict of interest (Quantica sells trend).[^quanticastack] They are conceptually strong and mutually reinforcing, but neither is a replicated empirical law; treat "size by left-tail correlation, not Sharpe" as a well-motivated design principle, not a proven theorem.
- Left-tail conditional correlation is itself a few-observation tail estimate and therefore unstable ([[skewness-in-asset-returns]]), so a budget sized on it must be built with wide error bars and revisited on a slow cadence, not re-optimised on every new drawdown.
- The one-instrument-per-sleeve rule is what makes this a roster problem rather than a single-sleeve stack, and it is the binding constraint on the whole argument: it is why gold (already a trend leg and a tail haven) and long-variance (already carry's short-side) cannot be re-added inside the divergent seat, and why the only new internal line is a signal-orthogonal value/economic-trend complement. Relax that rule and the article collapses back into the naive "stack every convex instrument in one seat" it argues against.
- The budget's components still interact even across sleeves. A value/economic-trend complement, the existing directional gold exposure, and the tail sleeve can displace each other under a fixed book-vol target (the roster's own allocator-displacement caveat), so combined convexity-per-bleed must be measured jointly on held-out data, never assembled from standalone convexity numbers.
- This article's budget half is built on the retired selection sweep's verdict; if that verdict were wrong and some non-trend source did dominate on every axis at once, the budget would collapse back to a single mechanism. Nothing in the sweep suggests that, but the whole argument is downstream of "no single winner."
- **The budget is smaller in practice than in principle on a constrained substrate.** The two off-sleeve levers this article proposes have both been tested and neither is currently deployable under a UCITS/IBKR-IE substrate: left-tail-correlation sizing of existing legs is a clean null, and the winter-cover value/economic-trend complement is substrate-blocked for lack of a clean instrument (see the hypotheses below for both). The construction argument stands as roster design, but its *new-line* ambition is presently blocked by the substrate, not the economics - itself the strongest case for a broader, futures-capable substrate ([[the-orthogonal-non-equity-trend-universe]]).
- The 18.8%, -0.038 and -0.233 figures are our own measurements on one universe and period (2018-2025, held-out rolling splits); they establish that the daily-many-names corner fails, not the precise viability of the recommended corner, which is a fee *projection* not yet run end-to-end.
- The impulse-rebalancing fill reduction is asserted from a verified mechanism, not yet measured; the order-of-magnitude claim must survive an A/B before it is trusted.
- "Slow is more convex" is contested and we do not silently pick a side. CFM shows that slowing a system, capping the forecast, and diversifying the pool each *reduce* convexity measured against a single underlying,[^cfm] and Man shows *faster* signals give better crisis alpha.[^mancrisis] Slow wins *here* on cost and horizon-fit, not on raw convexity-per-underlying; if NAV rises and the fee constraint relaxes, re-introducing a faster speed would raise convexity. The speed choice is NAV-conditioned, not absolute.
- The managed-futures ETF route carries swap-counterparty and new-fund risk, and its headline 2022 number is the underlying strategy, not the live UCITS wrapper.
- Skewness and tail-convexity estimates are statistically unstable by construction (few tail observations); treat all rankings as directional.
- Several load-bearing practitioner sources (CFM, Man, AQR, Quantica) run or sell trend products; the cost-control results (Holden, Leland, Novy-Marx/Velikov, Baltas/Kosowski) are the better-insulated claims.

## Strategy hypotheses this could seed

- [x] **Sizing existing convex legs by left-tail conditional correlation (overlay capacity) beats equal-risk and Sharpe weighting on combined held-out convexity-per-bleed.** The direct test of the Quantica reframing, respecting instrument-orthogonality by re-weighting exposures already held rather than adding instruments. **Tested via the robust tail-risk-parity proxy and NULL, 2026-07-07** ([[runs/atalanta/2026-07-07#atalanta_tailparity|2026-07-07]]): inverse-Expected-Shortfall leg sizing left held-out worst-split drawdown flat while shaving convexity - no dose Pareto-improves the baseline. Drawdown here is a signal/whipsaw property, not a leg-sizing property, so left-tail-correlation sizing plausibly still bites *across* roster tiers (trend vs carry vs tail) or on a broader book, not inside a thin single sleeve.
- [x] **A cross-asset value / economic-trend UCITS leg, decomposed clean of equity beta and the credit-carry pole, lifts the divergent seat's trendless-winter return at near-zero correlation to both poles.** Pre-register a residual non-trend, non-equity alpha gate and a realized-skew >= 0 gate before testing the held-out improvement; reject any carry-dominant vehicle on shape. **SUBSTRATE-BLOCKED as a clean instrument, 2026-07-07** ([[runs/atalanta/2026-07-07]]): the economics are well-supported (Asness et al. value/momentum; AQR macro momentum; Lombard Odier), but no clean EEA-retail UCITS instrument exists - the only UCITS value ETFs are long-only equity-factor funds (~1.0 beta, fail the non-equity gate), no pure cross-asset value or macro-momentum UCITS ETF exists for EEA retail, and self-building either overlaps the roster's existing exposures or hits the shorting/fixed-fee-floor walls that killed [[runs/atalanta/2026-07-06#atalanta_cs_equity_momentum (v1) - the convex non-trend complement|cross-sectional momentum]]. Re-entry: a broader, futures-capable substrate, or a carry-blended macro/ARP UCITS vehicle that clears the decomposition gate.
- [ ] **The single-day-gap and long-variance axes stay in the tail and carry sleeves, not the divergent seat (a boundary, not a run).** Instrument-orthogonality, the cost-killed always-on VIX-ETP floor ([[runs/aegis/2026-06-11#20260611T084627805837Z_etf_aegis_v22_vixyfloor|v22]]), and the carry sleeve's ownership of the short-variance premium together fix this; the divergent seat's job is the slow-crisis and trendless-winter axes only. Recorded so any future "add a long-vol overlay to trend" proposal must first clear the non-overlap rule and the prior cost kill.
- [ ] Impulse-control rebalancing - a strategy that holds (emits no-trade) between events, where an event is a cadence tick OR a no-trade-band breach OR a trend-state change, and on an event moves only part-way to an interior target (cut losers in full, add to winners partially) - cuts fills by roughly an order of magnitude *and* holds-or-raises net quarterly convexity versus the daily-rebalanced champion. Pre-register: net (post fee-floor) quarterly convexity payoff >= champion AND fills/yr strictly lower.
- [ ] Monthly (not daily) rebalancing of a four-month-half-life signal on four orthogonal sleeves lands fee drag below 1% of a EUR 5k NAV while holding quarterly convexity within noise of the daily book.
- [ ] Hysteresis state - a Schmitt-trigger on the trend score, or a new Donchian-breakout state indicator (enter on an N-day high, exit to flat on a shorter M-day low) - removes near-zero whipsaw, cutting fills and adding a bounded-loss convex shape versus the continuous sign gate.
- [ ] A smoother estimator (EWMA-crossover ensemble or log-price regression slope, drop-in for the endpoint momentum score) cuts state changes ~20% with no quarterly-convexity loss.
- [ ] A UCITS managed-futures ETF (iMGP DBi) beats the best DIY long-only cash-ETF book on net-of-fees quarterly convexity at small size - the buy-versus-build crossover.
- [ ] A single trend-gated -1x government-bond UCITS ETF adds the inflation-crisis convexity a long-only book lacks, net of daily-reset decay, when held only while its slow signal is short-and-trending. **PARKED 2026-07-03 ([[runs/atalanta/2026-07-03]]) - not testable live, and dominated in principle.** (1) The clean -1x US-Treasury UCITS this hypothesis assumes is **extinct**: Lyxor/Amundi E571/E572 were liquidated (LEI lapsed 2021, AT/CH/DE/LU-only distribution), and an IBKR account probe finds **no -1x US-Treasury line carried** - only -3x US-Treasury (UL3S/3TYS) and -1x EUR-sovereign (DXSV) inverse UCITS are tradable, and none is both US-Treasury and -1x. (2) A decay-physics pass makes the point moot regardless: drag ~= leverage^2 * 1/2 * variance, so a real **borrow-gated short (decay-free, exact underlying, holdable) dominates any inverse ETF**, and -3x is structurally wrong for a weeks-to-months hold (9x the -1x drag, worst in the high-vol bond-selloff it exists for). Verdict: the borrow-gated IDTL short (+ short_cap) stays the champion realization; a -1x **DXSV** EUR-sovereign sleeve is the only live fallback, reserved for a loss of IDTL borrow.
- [ ] Drop-one-name attribution on convexity-per-fill: only the minimal orthogonal set (global equity, long duration, gold, broad commodity) earns its per-order fee at small size; each correlated extra sleeve lowers net convexity. Pairs with [[the-orthogonal-non-equity-trend-universe]].
- [ ] A minimum-order-size gate (skip any rebalance whose traded notional is below a small multiple of the per-order floor) raises net convexity at small size by removing sub-economic fills, with no loss of crisis capture.
- [ ] Turning volatility targeting off (or coarsely band-gating it) holds quarterly convexity while cutting fills versus continuous inverse-vol rescaling, on a bond/commodity-tilted book at small size.
