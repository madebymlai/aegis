# Momentum Crashes Outside Equities

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> The canonical momentum crash is a short-leg event - past losers rip in a post-bear rebound - but that mechanism is specific to long-short equity books; outside equities the crashes come in three other shapes (a commodity long-leg bubble-ride into a deflationary bust, a currency carry liquidity-spiral unwind, and trend-following's own reversal whipsaws), and the fixes that demonstrably work in equities do not transfer cleanly, with each fix carrying an out-of-sample cost the equity literature understates.

The momentum-crash literature has one canonical picture, and it is an equity picture. Daniel and Moskowitz show that a long-short equity momentum strategy crashes when, following a market decline, the past-loser decile rebounds violently: in July-August 1932 the loser decile returned +232% while winners gained only 32%, and in March-May 2009 losers rose +163% against +8% for winners.[^dm] The mechanism is the short leg. After a bear market the losers are high-beta, distressed names whose equity is a deep out-of-the-money call option on the firm; the momentum portfolio is short that optionality and long low-beta defensive winners, so when the market rebounds it has a conditionally large negative beta and gets run over. The optionality "is only present in bear markets," and it lives in the loser portfolio.[^dm] This is a clean, well-replicated story - and it is the wrong story for most non-equity momentum crashes. The interesting question is what the right stories are, and whether the equity fixes survive the translation.

## Three mechanisms, not one

Daniel and Moskowitz themselves run the optionality test on bonds, currencies, and commodities and find the same loser-leg convexity in the long-short factor portfolios.[^dm] But the factor portfolio is not how most capital is exposed to non-equity momentum, and the directional and carry versions crash by different routes. Separating them is the whole game.

**Commodities: the long-leg bubble-ride - but only if you were long-only.** The defining commodity-momentum event of the modern record is 2008, and which crash it was depends entirely on construction. A long-only momentum or index book rode crude and the broad complex up through a speculative blow-off and then over the top into the deflationary bust. The PowerShares DB Commodity Index fund (DBC) gained over 100% from its 2007 inception to its mid-2008 peak and then fell more than 60%; the iShares S&P GSCI trust (GSG) more than doubled and then lost over 71% of its peak value within months; the S&P GSCI total-return index drew down roughly 67% July-2008 to early-2009.[^commetf][^dblci] Commodity index funds "continued to rally during the first half of 2008, long after the bear market in U.S. stocks had already started... when risky assets started to be sold off across the board in the second half of 2008, commodity funds provided no shelter."[^commetf] The crash here is a long-leg phenomenon: the book was long the trend into a top, and the top was the macro regime flipping from inflation-fear to deflation. There is direct evidence the run-up itself was partly a momentum-and-flow bubble - Singleton documents that index and managed-money flows had predictive power for 2008 oil returns, consistent with momentum-style positioning amplifying the move.[^singleton]

The correction that matters is that *time-series* momentum - the trend construction CTAs actually run - did the opposite, and this is where conflating the two constructions is most costly. By the time the complex topped, the 12-month trend signal had already flipped many contracts short, so the trend book was short into the bust and profited on the way down. Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen document it in the primary: "time series momentum profits are large in October, November, and December of 2008... Leading into this period, time series momentum suffers losses in the third quarter of 2008, where the associated price moves caused the TSMOM strategy to be short in many contracts, setting up large profits that were earned in the fourth quarter of 2008 as markets... fell further. ... TSMOM suffers sharp losses when the crisis ends in March, April, and May of 2009."[^mop] So the trend book's bad period was not riding the bubble over the top - it was the March-2009 reversal at the bottom, six months later and the opposite direction. The long-leg bubble crash belongs to the long-only book and the long leg of a cross-sectional spread; the trend book takes a reversal loss instead. A momentum-gated *haven* basket that rode long DBC and oil into H2-2008 was experiencing the long-only version, not the trend version - the gate was fully invested and trending up into the peak.

**Currencies: the carry liquidity spiral.** The classic currency crash is the carry unwind, and its mechanism is neither short-leg rebound nor long-leg bubble - it is a funding-liquidity spiral. Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen show carry returns are negatively skewed conditional on the interest-rate differential: high-rate "investment" currencies (AUD, NZD) carry the most negative skew, low-rate "funding" currencies (JPY) the most positive, and a higher rate differential predicts more negative future skewness.[^bnp] This is the FX analogue of the short-leg convexity that [[relative-vs-absolute-momentum-in-small-universes]] identifies as the source of equity momentum's catastrophic tail - a written-option payoff that pays a premium in calm and detonates in the unwind. The skew is "roughly comparable to the magnitude of skewness of the stock market index" (the equity index log return is skewed about -0.41 weekly, -0.88 quarterly) and crucially "cannot easily be diversified away," because crashes are correlated across similar-rate currencies.[^bnp] The driver is that a shock that loses money for speculators is amplified when they hit funding constraints and unwind, depressing prices, raising volatility and margins, and forcing further unwinds; a rising VIX coincides with carry losses and position reductions.[^bnp] This is the Brunnermeier-Pedersen liquidity-spiral made visible in FX.

A distinction the literature insists on, and that matters here: currency *momentum* is not currency *carry*. Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling and Schrimpf find cross-sectional FX momentum of up to 10% a year that is "very different from the popular carry trade... largely unrelated to carry trade returns,"[^menkhoff] and Burnside, Eichenbaum and Rebelo make the sharper point for our purposes: the 2008 crisis "cannot be used as an example of the kind of rare disaster" that rationalizes carry, because "momentum made money during the financial crisis."[^ber] So the FX crash is a carry phenomenon, not a momentum phenomenon - the momentum factor in FX did not share it. This is the first sign that "momentum crash" is the wrong label for several of these events.

**Bonds and trend-following: reversal whipsaws at turning points.** Bond momentum's bad days come at policy turns, but they are reversal events, not loser-rebounds. Time-series momentum in bonds is real but weak as a cross-sectional rank (Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen put the cross-sectional momentum Sharpe across 10 government-bond markets at 0.06, statistically zero, against 0.63 for commodities),[^amp] and the within-curve duration-bucket version that does work produces *positively* skewed returns and hedges rather than crashes.[^lsm] What actually hurts a long-rates trend book is a sharp reversal when the market reprices the policy path - the same reversal-sign whipsaw that [[trend-following-in-whipsaw-regimes]] frames as the structural premium a trend sleeve pays, not a fixable defect. March 2023 is the cleanest recent case: a quick, large upheaval in short-rates and government-bond yields gave the trend-following industry one of its worst months in years, and on the SG Trend Index it ranked as the second-worst five-day drawdown since 2000.[^quantica][^man2023] The mechanism Quantica describes is "a classic trend reversal shock with nowhere to hide" - books that were positioned for the prior rate trend were caught when it snapped, with no short-loser leg involved at all.[^quantica]

## What conditions each crash

The conditioning variables differ by mechanism, which is why a single risk model cannot cover them.

For the equity factor, the conditioning is bear-market-plus-high-volatility-plus-rebound, and Daniel and Moskowitz show it is *partly* forecastable: the crashes cluster in "panic states" following declines when volatility is high, and the loser leg behaves like a written call on the market in exactly those states.[^dm] For commodities, the conditioning variable is the term structure - the backwardation/contango cycle and its associated roll yield - layered on the macro inflation/deflation regime. Momentum and roll-yield (term-structure) signals in commodities are distinct and low-correlated, and the term-structure state carries independent information about which contracts are about to keep rising versus rolling down a contango curve.[^fuertes2010][^degroot] For carry, the conditioning is funding liquidity: the TED spread and VIX predict carry losses, and the level of carry-trade activity (crowding) itself endogenously creates the skew, because a larger built-up position has more to unwind.[^bnp] For trend-following at turning points, the conditioning is the disagreement between fast and slow signals, which flags the moments when reversal risk is highest.[^garg]

The crowding channel deserves emphasis because it is shared across carry and trend but absent from the textbook equity story. Brunnermeier-Nagel-Pedersen show carry skew is endogenous to position size: "gains on carry trades lead to further build-up of carry trade activity, which then also increases the potential impact... of an unwinding."[^bnp] AQR's century study finds the post-2008 trend environment was marked by higher within- and across-market correlations - a "risk-on/risk-off" regime that reduced the number of independent trends and lowered risk-adjusted returns.[^aqrcentury] Crowding and correlation are state variables for these crashes in a way they are not for the 1932/2009 equity rebounds.

## What demonstrably helped, and what it cost

The equity literature's headline fix is volatility scaling. Barroso and Santa-Clara show that scaling momentum to constant volatility lifts the Sharpe ratio from 0.53 to 0.97, cuts the worst one-month return from -78.96% to -28.40%, and the maximum drawdown from -96.69% to -45.20%, while dropping excess kurtosis from 18.24 to 2.68 and left-skew from -2.47 to -0.42.[^barroso] Daniel and Moskowitz's dynamic version (scaling by forecast mean and variance) roughly doubles the static Sharpe.[^dm] These are large, robust numbers - *for equities*. The question is whether they transfer.

They mostly do not. Harvey and co-authors run volatility targeting across 60+ assets and find the Sharpe benefit is concentrated in risk assets (equities and credit) that exhibit a leverage effect - a negative return-volatility relation that makes vol-scaling behave like added momentum - and is "negligible" for bonds, currencies, and commodities.[^harveyvol] For non-equity momentum, vol-scaling's robust benefit is therefore not higher Sharpe but a thinner left tail and lower vol-of-vol; it is drawdown control, not crash alpha - the same drawdown-tool-not-chop-alpha verdict reached for trend sleeves generally, and a reminder (see [[measuring-crisis-alpha]]) that a metric rewarding crash *gains* will rank these overlays differently from full-sample Sharpe. Worse, in commodities specifically the equity-style vol-management fails out of sample: Wang and co-authors (in the Journal of Futures Markets) find that the Moreira-Muir volatility management that works in-sample on the 12-month commodity momentum and market portfolios "fails to improve real-time performance," because commodities lack the negative risk-return relation vol-management needs.[^volcomm] This is the honest cost: a fix that is close to free-and-effective in equities is, in commodities, an out-of-sample illusion, and in bonds and FX a tail-trimmer that does nothing for Sharpe.

The fix that fits the *commodity* mechanism is a term-structure / carry filter, and it has the strongest non-equity evidence - but it is not a crash hedge, it is a return enhancer with its own out-of-sample drag pattern. Combining momentum with a roll-yield (term-structure) signal - buy backwardated winners, avoid or short contangoed losers - raised the risk-adjusted return of a long-short commodity book from roughly 10% a year (momentum-only) to about 21% over 1979-2007, with the two signals low-correlated, and the gain survives conservative transaction costs (de Groot and co-authors report net returns roughly doubling, from an insignificant 3.98% to 8.42% a year for the term-structure-enhanced version).[^fuertes2010][^degroot][^cxo] The reason this matters for crashes is structural: a long-short factor that *shorts the contangoed losers* has near-zero or even positive skew (Fuertes and co-authors), which is why the commodity-momentum factor was "largely left unaffected" by 2008-09 while the long-only index lost 60-70%.[^thesis][^fuertes2015] The cost ledger: the term-structure filter does nothing for a *long-only* book in a long-leg bust (it cannot short the contango), it adds turnover, and the underlying momentum edge has decayed structurally in recent years on its own merits.[^gcard]

The third fix, speed diversification, is the one with cross-asset support but the subtlest cost. Garg, Goulding, Harvey and Mazzoleni show that blending fast and slow time-series-momentum signals beats either alone and that fast-slow disagreement is itself a turning-point signal; the intersection of the two signal directions carries predictive information, including predictably negative returns when both are negative.[^garg] Faster signals act as a "low-cost insurance layer," cushioning drawdowns after abrupt shocks and de-risking quickly when trends fragment.[^speedrep][^man2023] But the diversification is shallower than it looks: adjacent lookbacks are 0.84-0.90 correlated, so the medium-term layer is largely redundant and the efficient form is a short-plus-long barbell, not a dense ladder.[^barbell] And the cost shows up precisely in trend-friendly years: the fast leg whipsaws when the slow leg would have ridden a clean trend, so a faster blend that protects 2023-style reversals gives back in 2014/2022-style strong-trend years - the standalone Sharpe of fast trend is materially lower than slow.[^graham][^speedrep] Speed diversification is real, but it is a turning-point insurance you pay for in the smooth years.

## The honest cost ledger, summarized

Three fixes, three out-of-sample bills:

- *Volatility scaling* costs almost nothing in equities (it raises Sharpe there) but buys only tail-trimming in bonds/FX and is an out-of-sample mirage in commodities; treating it as crash alpha outside equities overstates it.[^harveyvol][^volcomm]
- *Term-structure / carry filters* enhance long-short commodity momentum net of costs and flatten its skew, but do nothing for a long-only book in a long-leg bust, add turnover, and ride a decaying base edge.[^degroot][^gcard]
- *Speed diversification* dampens turning-point reversals (bond-policy turns, the 2009 and 2023 whipsaws) but gives back trend capture in strong-trend years, and the diversification across adjacent speeds is mostly redundant.[^garg][^barbell]

None of these touches the long-leg commodity crash, which is the one most relevant to a long-only book: when you are long the trend into a macro-regime flip, there is no risk-management overlay short of an absolute exit that helps - vol-scaling cuts size after the vol spike (too late), term-structure filters cannot short, and speed diversification only changes how fast you turn, not whether the turn is into a bust. The general lesson is the one the equity literature's success obscures: the canonical crash and its canonical fix are an equity special case, and the non-equity crashes are governed by term structure, funding liquidity, and reversal timing - different state variables, different and costlier remedies.

## Limitations

- The "commodity momentum factor barely crashed in 2008-09" claim rests on long-short factor results (Fuertes; the Vaasa thesis reading of Daniel-Moskowitz). A long-only commodity-momentum *gate* (hold the trending commodities, no short leg) is structurally exposed to the long-leg bust and is *not* protected the way the long-short factor is - the protection comes entirely from shorting contango. This is the single most important caveat for any long-only application and is flagged in-text.
- The carry-crash mechanism is from one primary source (Brunnermeier-Nagel-Pedersen) plus the corroborating Burnside-Eichenbaum-Rebelo and Menkhoff et al. on the momentum-vs-carry distinction; the skewness and liquidity-spiral results are well-replicated, but the exact 1986-2006 sample limits direct read-through to post-GFC FX microstructure.
- Vol-management's commodity OOS failure is a single peer-reviewed study (Wang et al., JFM) building on the Cederburg-style critique; the direction is consistent with Harvey et al.'s asset-class finding, so confidence is medium-high, but it is not multiply replicated for commodities specifically.
- Much non-equity evidence is monthly-cadence long-short factor research on futures. A daily long-only ETF book has different turnover, different whipsaw, and (critically) no short leg - so the factor-level skewness numbers do not transfer to a long-only implementation. The hypotheses below are written to respect that gap.
- The trend-following post-mortems (2009, 2015-16, 2023) are practitioner and index sources, several CTA-affiliated (COI); the SG Trend prints are public index values, but the attribution narratives (which markets, which reversals) are manager commentary.

Falsifiable claim: in the 2008 window the commodity gate's loss comes with a positive (long) entering position and a momentum signal that was still positive at the pre-crash peak, i.e. the loss is riding the trend over the top, not a short-leg rebound. This is a *diagnostic*, not a fix, and is mechanically distinct from the graveyard's fast-vol / overnight-gap *detection* triggers (those try to time the entry to the crash; this only classifies the crash's shape after the fact). - [x] **Term-structure / carry filter on a long-only commodity-momentum leg**: gate the commodity sleeve not only on price momentum but on a roll-yield / curve-state proxy computable from daily ETF OHLCV - hold a commodity ETF long only when it is both trend-positive *and* its curve proxy is non-contango (e.g. the front-vs-deferred spread implied by comparing a front-month-heavy ETF like USO against a longer-dated or optimized-roll ETF like USL/DBC over the lookback). Falsifiable: the curve-filtered long-only commodity leg has a shallower 2008-style long-leg drawdown than the price-momentum-only leg, at the cost of forfeited upside in backwardated bull runs (the honest out-of-sample bill). - [x] **Cross-asset crash-shape separation in the haven basket**: within the haven sleeve (TLT/GLD/UUP/DBC/XLE), test whether the *commodity-linked* members (DBC, XLE) contribute long-leg drawdowns at macro-regime flips while the *duration/dollar* members (TLT, UUP) contribute reversal whipsaws at policy turns - i.e. the basket holds two structurally different crash mechanisms that a single vol or breadth control cannot jointly hedge. Falsifiable: the worst-drawdown attribution in the 2008-H2 and a rate-reversal window (e.g. 2013 taper or 2023) loads on different members with different entering-position signs. The 2023 window could not test the duration-reversal half (the gate had already evicted TLT). See [[runs/aegis/2026-06-11]].**
- [ ] **Within-curve duration momentum as the positively-skewed exception.** The one documented momentum family with *positive* skew is duration-bucket momentum inside the Treasury curve.[^lsm] Test it on our substrate: a long-only rotation among curve-segment ETFs (SHY/IEF/TLT by trailing momentum, daily OHLCV) against holding TLT statically. Expected direction: the rotation shows a shallower drawdown and flat-to-positive skew in the duration-crash windows (2013 taper, 2022) because within-curve momentum steps down the curve at the policy turn instead of crashing with the long end. Refuted if the rotation inherits TLT-like negative skew or loses the 2022 window - which would mean the Fed staff result does not survive ETF-grade, daily-cadence implementation. A new lever from a cited no-COI source; not a detection trigger, short leg, signal blend, or floor, so mechanically distinct from everything in the graveyard.
