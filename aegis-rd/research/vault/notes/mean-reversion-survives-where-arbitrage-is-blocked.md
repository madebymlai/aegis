# Mean Reversion Survives Where Arbitrage Is Blocked

> [!abstract] One-line takeaway
> Mean reversion earns net of realistic costs only where the trade that would close the gap is itself blocked - structurally, by an access or short-sale friction that keeps capital from correcting an observable anchor, or informationally, by a triggering flow the market has not yet learned to anticipate. Reversion that lacks either kind of blockage - the naive statistical form traded on price history alone - has been competed down to the cost line or below over the last two decades, and a small, long-only, whole-share EU-retail book inherits that decay in full while capturing only a narrow slice of what still works.

"Mean reversion" names three different bets that happen to share a sign convention. One buys a security against an *observable* fair value - a fund's own net asset value, a dually-listed twin's other listing, a sibling share class with identical cash flows - and waits for the market price to converge to something you can already compute. A second buys or sells only when a *specific, identified* cause of the move is non-fundamental - a redemption-forced fund sale, a rating-triggered mandate exit, an index reconstitution, an ETF creation/redemption imbalance - and bets that cause will unwind. A third infers non-fundamental displacement purely from *price history*: last week's loser bounces, a stock's residual against its sector snaps back, two historically co-moving names diverge and reconverge. The first two conditions on something outside the price series; the third does not. That difference turns out to be the whole story of why some reversion still pays and most does not.

This article asks which constructions in each family still clear realistic costs today, for a feasibility profile that matters concretely here: a modest EU-retail UCITS-eligible book trading whole shares through IBKR-style fixed per-order minimums, no derivatives, no shorting as a default posture, and a realistic cadence of weekly to monthly. That profile rules out plenty of what the literature calls "arbitrage" - most of it needs to short one leg, hold a futures position, or turn over daily - and it changes which findings are actually informative. The synthesis: anchored reversion survives specifically where a structural friction (short-sale cost, capital control, wrapper segmentation) stops the correcting trade from happening quickly, and that friction is durable enough that a patient, unlevered, long-only buyer can be the marginal corrector rather than needing to short. Flow-conditioned reversion survives specifically where the triggering flow cannot be scheduled or anticipated - once a flow becomes calendar-predictable, competing capital arrives and the premium is arbitraged toward zero, sometimes within a decade. Statistical reversion, having neither kind of blockage, has decayed furthest and fastest; the equity-market version of that decay is already documented in depth in [[short-horizon-reversal-in-small-cross-sections]], and this article treats it briefly, adding only what that article does not cover.

## Anchored reversion: paid where the correcting trade is blocked

Closed-end funds are the cleanest laboratory. A CEF's net asset value is public every day; its market price frequently is not equal to it, and the price-NAV gap has been documented as mean-reverting since the 1970s.[^pontiff] Lee, Shleifer and Thaler's canonical account treats the discount as a sentiment phenomenon: retail-held CEFs and retail-held small stocks move together, discounts narrow when small caps rally, and new funds get launched precisely when sentiment (and hence discounts) is favorable.[^lst] That reading has never gone unanswered. Berk and Stanton show that a fully rational model - managers with observable-with-noise skill, long-term fee contracts they can't be forced to renegotiate, and the option to leave once their ability is revealed as good - mechanically reproduces all four stylized facts Lee, Shleifer and Thaler catalogued: funds launch near NAV, drift into discount, the discount varies across funds and over time, and price converges to NAV at termination.[^berkstanton] Neither camp has retired the other; a 2025 nonlinear time-series study still finds CEF discounts following regime-switching trend breaks that neither a pure sentiment nor a pure rational-fee model fully explains.[^trendbreaks] What is not disputed is that the price-NAV gap is tradable: parametrically modeling the history of a fund's premium (not just its current level) and buying the highest-expected-return quintile against the lowest earned an 18.2% annualized return with a Sharpe ratio of 1.918 in a U.S. sample first tested out of sample from 1998, against a 14.9%-return, 1.519-Sharpe naive discount-sort benchmark - neither figure stated net of transaction costs.[^ppw] A 2021 Copenhagen Business School thesis running the same family of strategies on nine Swedish CEFs, explicitly net of modeled and market-impact costs, still delivered 7.4-14.6% annualized, with the mean-reversion-specific slice of that return estimated at 2.2-9.3%; this is a single, unpublished, small-sample source and should be weighted accordingly.[^aggeborn]

The reason this premium has not been arbitraged away after five decades of documentation is the same reason it can be captured by an unlevered long-only buyer rather than requiring a short: the correcting trade is structurally impeded. When U.S. regulators relaxed short-sale constraints under Regulation SHO for a randomized subset of stocks, funds holding more of the deregulated names saw their premiums move differently from funds holding fewer - and separately, funds that actually experience heavier short selling see their premiums decline significantly over the following five days, exactly the signature of a constraint binding until it doesn't.[^altpeterson] Across single-country CEFs, premiums and discounts still forecast future share-price returns (though only weakly forecast NAV returns) in a 2021 VAR study, and the authors tie the effect explicitly to capital controls, political risk and information asymmetry between local and foreign holders - frictions that block the textbook short-the-fund/long-the-portfolio arbitrage in segmented markets.[^berggrun] That is the mechanism this article's thesis needs: a friction that is durable (capital controls and short-sale costs don't disappear because a paper got published) and does not require the retail side of the trade to short anything - buy the discount, hold, let the constrained side of the market be whoever is forced to sell into it or unable to correct it. The access catch for a EU-retail book is regulatory rather than economic: many U.S.-domiciled CEFs lack a PRIIPs KID and are therefore unavailable to EU retail accounts on brokers like IBKR without professional-client status, which narrows the tradable universe to UK/EU investment trusts and funds that do carry a KID before any of the discount economics above can be tested.

Dual-class shares with identical cash-flow rights are a second anchor, and one where the correcting trade is already the shape our feasibility profile needs. Schultz and Shive's intraday study of 100 U.S. dual-class pairs (1993-2006, 3,687 episodes where one class's bid exceeded the other's ask by at least 1%) finds the gaps are corrected mostly by *one-sided* trades - buying the cheap class or selling the expensive one - not by long-short arbitrage, and that it is usually the more liquid class whose price moves out of line first.[^schultzshive] Long-short arbitrage playing "a minor part" is, from a long-only retail perspective, the finding that matters: most of the correction comes from exactly the trade our book can place. Marquette and Williams took this further than most academic papers bother to and ran the strategy on a real retail brokerage account against two live names (Blockbuster, Lennar), confirming it was logistically executable outside a backtest - a small, anecdotal, but genuinely feasibility-relevant test.[^marquettewilliams] A 2025 study of U.S. and Canadian dual-class firms (2012-2022) finds the voting premium's volatility is driven by the *magnitude* of news flow about the controlling shareholder, not by its sentiment direction, and explicitly does not confirm the relative-illiquidity explanation for the premium - a genuine disagreement with the liquidity-based reading of where the gap comes from, even though both papers agree the gap itself is real and moves.[^delabruslerie]

Cross-listed shares (ADRs against their home-market twin) round out the anchored family but transfer less cleanly to our cadence. The gap is real and mean-reverting - a study of Mexican ADR pairs finds an average convergence half-life of 3.1 business days (median 1.1 days) using a nonlinear threshold model, with liquidity positively correlated to convergence speed - but a half-life measured in days means most of the documented profit accrues to whoever monitors intraday, not to a weekly or monthly rebalancer arriving after the gap has typically already closed.[^koumkwa] The mechanism that keeps *some* gap alive between snapshots is the same access friction as the CEF case: pairs-trading between the ADR and the local share is itself the main price-correcting force, and that mechanism requires holding (or shorting) both legs simultaneously, in two markets, often in two currencies - a structurally two-sided trade our no-derivatives, long-side-first profile is poorly suited to run at any horizon.[^alsayed]

## Flow-conditioned reversion: paid until the flow is anticipated

The classic identified-flow story is Coval and Stafford's asset fire sales: mutual funds in the bottom decile of capital flows are forced to shrink existing positions regardless of price, the stocks they dump lose an average 10.1% in the forced-sale quarter (t = -11.52), and prices rebound 7.7% over the following months as the selling pressure dissipates - a strategy shorting the anticipated sellers and buying the anticipated buyers earned average annual abnormal returns "well over 15%."[^covalstafford] This is the strongest possible case for a structural counterparty: a fund facing redemptions has no price discretion, and Coval and Stafford explicitly note the front-running incentive their own finding creates.

That measure has since been challenged on methodological grounds specific to how the field computes it. Wardlaw shows that the standard flow-pressure variable used across "a large and rapidly growing literature" is mechanically a function of the stock's own realized return during the outflow quarter - meaning it is not actually orthogonal to fundamentals - and that after removing this contamination, outflows produce only a "fairly negligible" quarterly return decline with *no* subsequent reversal, undoing many of the field's established results.[^wardlaw] This is a serious critique of the measurement, not a replication failure of Coval and Stafford's specific classification, and the two sources disagree on how much of the "fire sale reversal" literature survives contact with the corrected variable. A partial answer: Cespa, Keswani and Urzúa re-ran the fire-sale-reversal test on European data using Wardlaw's own corrected flow-to-stock measure and still found a significant price reduction followed by a reversal, plus an increased probability of subsequent takeovers, concentrated where liquidity is low and fire-sale intensity is high.[^cespa] The honest read is that the mechanism likely survives Wardlaw's correction outside the specific U.S. large-cap setting the critique targeted, but the magnitude claimed by the original 2007 study should not be taken at face value without re-measurement on the corrected variable.

Fallen angels - investment-grade bonds downgraded to high yield - are the credit-market analogue, and here the two sides of the counter-lane disagree even more directly about the *mechanism*, not just the magnitude. Ben Dor and Xu attribute post-downgrade underperformance and up-to-two-year reversal to insurer capital-charge rules and mandates that force immediate disposal.[^bendorxu] Ambrose, Cai and Helwege test this directly by splitting downgrades into those where the stock market reacted (information-driven) and those where it didn't (plausibly forced-selling-only), using NAIC insurer transaction data. For the "clean," uninformative subset, the average price decline in a two-week window around the downgrade is a statistically insignificant -0.45% (four-agency sample, t = -0.67); for the informative subset it is -10.19% to -10.84%, highly significant. Their conclusion is blunt: "the majority of the price reaction in the case of fallen angels reflects negative information and price pressures are negligible in magnitude, if not exactly zero."[^ambrose] This is a genuine, unresolved disagreement between two credit-market microstructure papers using different samples and periods, and it matters for anyone building a fallen-angel strategy: the live evidence that the *combined* effect (information plus whatever pressure remains) is tradable comes from fund performance rather than either academic paper - iShares' FALN ETF has returned 6.1% annualized since its 2016 inception, roughly 2 percentage points ahead of the broad high-yield category, while a CAIA industry report finds fallen angels enter their benchmark index priced about 150 basis points cheaper than high-yield peers on average, against roughly 65 basis points of average transaction cost and a narrow ~250-bond, ~$150bn universe with thin post-Dodd-Frank dealer inventory.[^caia][^morningstar] Both of those sources have a commercial interest in the conclusion (an index/ETF promotional report and a fund-performance write-up), so treat the net-of-cost magnitude as directionally right but not independently verified.

Index reconstitution supplies the article's cleanest demonstration of *decay through anticipation*, and it is worth dwelling on because it directly tests this article's central claim. The classic S&P 500 addition effect - buy what an index fund is about to be forced to buy - averaged 7.4% in the 1990s. By the 2010s it had fallen to under 1%, statistically indistinguishable from zero, *despite* a large increase in the share of market assets tracking the index over the same period; the deletion effect shows the same pattern, from -16.1% in the 1990s to -0.6% in the 2010s.[^greenwoodsammon] Greenwood and Sammon estimate the "demand multiplier" - the price impact per dollar of forced index-fund buying - fell by a factor of roughly twenty, from about 6.7-6.75 in the late 1990s to 0.30-0.37 in the last decade, and attribute the decline mainly to the growth of index migrations (stocks moving between S&P tiers, which halves the net demand shock) and to markets becoming structurally better at absorbing a scheduled, publicly announced flow. This is a flow that used to be non-fundamental and forced exactly like a fire sale, on a fixed announced calendar - and once enough capital learned the calendar, the premium it paid was competed away almost completely. Not every paper agrees the effect is dead: Kumar, Lawrence, Prakash and Rodríguez re-measure S&P 500 additions and deletions with outlier correction and a cleaner separation of "true" changes from index migrations, and find a *permanent* price response survives for both.[^kumar] This is a live methodological dispute, not a settled counter-lane finding, and it is the single disagreement in this article most consequential to the "scheduled flows get arbitraged away" half of the thesis.

ETF creation and redemption flows are the newest identified-flow signal and the one most directly usable by our profile, because the underlying data (daily shares outstanding) is public. Brown, Davies and Ringgenberg formalize why: authorized participants correct violations of the law of one price between an ETF and its basket by creating or redeeming shares, and because the ETF and its underlying assets can respond differently to a non-fundamental demand shock, the resulting flow is itself a signal of that shock. Empirically, a portfolio short the highest-flow ETFs and long the lowest-flow ETFs earns 1.1-2.0% per month at a one-month horizon, with the predictability persisting out to three- and six-month horizons in a 2007-2016 U.S. sample.[^bdr] The long-only half of that construction - buy the ETFs under the heaviest redemption pressure and hold for the multi-month reversal window - is directly implementable at monthly cadence without shorting.

The month-end rebalancing-flow trade is the plainest expression of "identified, unscheduled-enough-to-still-pay" reversion for a long-only book: hold whichever of stocks or bonds most underperformed the other over the month, into the last handful of trading sessions, ahead of the calendar-driven rebalancing flow from large multi-asset funds, then sell into that flow. A single practitioner backtest reports a 20-year CAGR of 7.9% with a Sharpe near 0.78 (best year 2008) - a single, pre-cost, un-replicated source, but the mechanism (fixed-weight multi-asset funds rebalance on a schedule that is itself public knowledge, so front-running it is exactly a scheduled flow) sits closer to the index-reconstitution decay case than to the fire-sale survival case, and should be expected to decay for the same reason as the index effect once enough capital anticipates the specific calendar dates.[^eom]

The pattern across this whole lane: **scheduled, calendar-public flows get arbitraged toward zero as soon as enough capital learns the calendar** (index reconstitution, and probably eventually month-end rebalancing); **unscheduled, idiosyncratic flows resist this** because no outside capital can pre-position without first knowing *which* fund will face redemptions or *which* issuer will get downgraded (fire sales, fallen angels, ETF flow pressure). That distinction, more than the structural-versus-behavioral label usually applied to "forced selling," is what predicts whether a flow-conditioned premium survives contact with the market that keeps discovering it.

## Statistical reversion: the crowd got there first, mostly

Pairs trading is the founding statistical construction: Gatev, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst's distance method reported roughly 11% annualized excess returns trading the top pairs by historical co-movement.[^ggr] Do and Faff's extension to 2009 confirmed a steady decline - the top-20-pairs portfolio's mean monthly excess return fell from 0.86% (1962-88) to 0.37% (1989-2002) to 0.24% (2003-09) - and their follow-up work concludes the distance method is largely unprofitable after 2002 net of costs, attributing roughly 70% of the decline not to more competitors sharing the same opportunities but to a genuine worsening of the underlying arbitrage economics (a rising share of pairs that diverge and simply never converge).[^dofaff] An open replication extending the same methodology to 2020 on CRSP data reports the profitability has "essentially disappeared."[^ggrrepl] Commodity futures are the one pocket where this has not fully happened: a technical-rule pairs strategy across metals, energy and agricultural spreads (1990-2016) still shows out-of-sample Sharpe ratios as high as 1.7-2.0 for Brent-WTI in its earlier subperiods, decaying but not vanishing by the end of the sample, with break-even costs still above conservative estimates.[^fuertes] That pocket requires futures access and typically a short leg, which sits outside this book's construction, and it would need to be re-expressed through commodity ETC pairs (still two-sided) to become even theoretically reachable here.

The residual-reversal / statistical-arbitrage lineage that generalizes pairs trading to a broad cross-section shows the same shape. Avellaneda and Lee's PCA- and sector-ETF-based mean-reversion strategies earned an average Sharpe of 1.44 (PCA) and 1.1 (ETF) from 1997-2007, but both degrade sharply after 2002 - to 0.9 and a comparable figure respectively in the 2003-2007 subperiod - even before accounting for the fact that the strategy's edge concentrated specifically in the pre-2003 years.[^avellanedalee] A 2026 practitioner replication of the same framework on 2015-2020 S&P 500 data across six implementations finds the underlying signal is genuinely still present at the gross level (+2.2% to +3.5% annualized across five of six variants) but survives realistic costs only inside a narrow envelope: sub-10-basis-point fees and a holding period of roughly ten to twenty trading days; outside that joint constraint the edge disappears, and trades held past three weeks are actively destructive.[^tapiero] That is a single, non-peer-reviewed source, but its cost-and-horizon sensitivity finding is consistent with everything else in this article: a signal this thin cannot survive both wide holding windows and retail-scale transaction costs simultaneously, which effectively excludes it from a monthly-cadence book regardless of what a sub-10bps institutional desk can still extract from it.

Yield curve slope and curvature reversion is worth naming even though it is the clearest access exclusion in this article. A foundational 1964-2000 U.S. Treasury study finds duration-matched, cash-neutral spread and curvature trades outperformed a comparable bond-index benchmark by up to 5.9 times on a risk-adjusted, pre-cost basis - but the authors themselves report that "market efficiency has improved... and the scope for excess returns has diminished since the late 1980s," a decay signal built into the origin paper rather than added by a later critic.[^curveyc] The construction requires simultaneous long and short duration-matched Treasury exposure, which in practice means futures or swaps; it is genuinely inaccessible to a no-derivatives, long-side-first mandate and there is no evidence it survives a long-only re-expression.

For the equity cross-section specifically - close-to-close reversal, industry/factor-residual reversal, VIX-conditioned timing, and the ETF operational-shorting evidence that ties non-informational demand to a next-day wrapper-price reversal without forecasting the underlying basket - the deep treatment already exists in [[short-horizon-reversal-in-small-cross-sections]] and this article adds nothing to it beyond noting that the pattern is identical: naive reversal sits below the cost line, a conditioned or residual version clears it but comes almost entirely from a single conflicted source cluster, and a floor-sized universe is breadth-starved for a cross-sectional bet regardless of construction.

## What discriminates a tradable dislocation from a repricing

Four variables recur across all three lanes as the discriminant between a gap that will close in your favor and one that reflects a genuine repricing.

**Access and short-sale constraints** determine whether an anchored gap can persist long enough to be observed and traded at all, rather than being closed same-day by professional arbitrage capital. The Reg SHO evidence on CEF premiums, and the ADR literature's finding that convergence speed rises with market efficiency and liquidity, both point the same direction: where shorting is cheap and unrestricted, gaps close within days; where it is not, they persist for the weeks or months a slower book needs to act.[^altpeterson][^koumkwa]

**Funding and liquidity stress**, proxied by the VIX, is already established in depth as the discriminant for statistical reversal's *size* at a point in time.[^nagel] The same state variable plausibly widens anchored discounts too - country CEF discounts move with political and macroeconomic risk in the segmented-market evidence above - but this article has not found a direct test of VIX-conditioned CEF discount trading and flags that as untested rather than assumed.

**Whether the triggering flow is scheduled or unscheduled** is this article's own synthesized addition to the conditioning-variable list, and it is the axis that separates the two flow-conditioned outcomes above. A calendar-public, repeated flow (index reconstitution, and by the same logic eventually month-end rebalancing front-running) gets discovered, priced, and competed away - Greenwood and Sammon document this happening to completion over about two decades. An idiosyncratic, hard-to-forecast flow (which specific fund faces redemptions, which specific issuer gets downgraded next) resists this because outside capital cannot pre-position without already knowing the answer, and the evidence for these mechanisms - Coval-Stafford, its Wardlaw correction, and the Cespa-Keswani-Urzúa European replication - holds up across decades and a serious methodological challenge in a way the scheduled-flow evidence does not.

**Whether the move carries information** is the same distinction the fallen-angel dispute turns on directly: Ambrose, Cai and Helwege find price pressure vanishes once the informative downgrades are stripped out, meaning most of what looks like "forced-selling reversal" in the pooled sample is actually the market correctly pricing bad news, not overreacting to a technical flow. This is the identical logic already established for equity short-horizon reversal weakening after earnings announcements in the sibling article, transplanted into a credit-market setting with a sharper identification strategy.

These four conditions are themselves specific instances of the general three-condition framework already developed in [[when-conditioning-pays]] - state persistence relative to the acting horizon, a clean mapping from the conditioning variable to the trade, and a bounded cost of misclassification. A short-sale constraint is a persistent state (it does not flip week to week); an unscheduled flow's non-fundamental character is, by construction, imperfectly observable in real time, which is exactly what Wardlaw's critique demonstrates when the standard proxy for it turns out to be contaminated by the very return it is supposed to predict. Applying that general framework here does not just describe this article's finding after the fact - it would have predicted in advance that scheduled flows decay and unscheduled ones do not, because a scheduled flow is a state whose persistence is common knowledge, which is precisely the condition under which competing capital arrives fastest.

## Who pays, and whether it lasts

For anchored reversion, the counterparty is retail or sentiment-driven capital on one side and structurally constrained institutions - segmented-market investors facing capital controls, short-sale-cost-averse holders - on the other, and both are permanently present by construction rather than fading as smart money learns about the trade. That looks structural, but "structural" here specifically means "as durable as the regulatory and wrapper friction that creates it," which is why the debate between Lee-Shleifer-Thaler's behavioral account and Berk-Stanton's rational-fee account has run for over three decades without resolution: both frictions and rational fee structures are durable, and price data alone cannot fully separate a persistent behavioral mispricing from a persistent, correctly priced cost of illiquidity.

For flow-conditioned reversion, the payer is a mandate-constrained institution with no price discretion - a redemption-driven fund, an insurance desk facing capital charges, an index tracker. That mandate constraint is itself structural (funds do not change their redemption rules to avoid paying a liquidity premium), but the *premium's durability* depends entirely on whether other capital can see the trade coming. When it can (index reconstitution), the premium was arbitraged from 7.4% to statistically zero inside two decades even as the amount of index-tracking capital rose. When it can't (which specific fund melts down, which specific bond gets cut), the evidence survives a serious methodological correction and a second, independent, European sample two decades after the original study. This asymmetry - not the structural-versus-behavioral label usually attached to forced selling - is the most decision-relevant finding in this article.

For statistical reversion, the payer was historically retail and uninformed order flow, plus market makers holding inventory they didn't want; the arrival of cheap algorithmic liquidity provision and thousands of imitators pushed the compensated version of the trade down toward, and in liquid equities below, realistic transaction costs. Do and Faff's own decomposition finds most of pairs trading's decline came from the underlying arbitrage economics genuinely worsening (more pairs diverging permanently rather than reconverging), not merely from more traders splitting a fixed opportunity - the clearest evidence in this article of a premium that looks behavioral and decaying at its source, rather than structural and merely more crowded.

## Limitations

The CEF discount debate between the sentiment account and the rational-fee account is genuinely unresolved as of the most recent evidence reviewed here (2025), and a strategy that trades the discount should expect returns closer to whichever model is more locally correct for a given fund's fee structure and manager tenure than to the pooled historical average.[^trendbreaks]

Wardlaw's critique targets the flow-pressure measure used broadly since the mid-2000s; it is not a direct replication failure of Coval and Stafford's original 2007 classification, which used a related but not identical construction. Treat the fire-sale reversal claim as likely to survive in some form - supported by the post-critique European replication - but with the *magnitude* genuinely uncertain until re-measured on the corrected variable in the original U.S. sample.

The fallen-angel price-pressure-versus-information dispute between Ben Dor-Xu and Ambrose-Cai-Helwege is unresolved and uses different samples, periods and identification strategies; a strategy built on this mechanism should expect the combined, live fund-level track record (which blends information and whatever pressure remains) rather than either paper's isolated theoretical premium.

Kumar, Lawrence, Prakash and Rodríguez directly dispute Greenwood and Sammon's "index effect is dead" conclusion on methodological grounds and find a permanent price response survives once outliers and index migrations are handled differently. This is the single most consequential open disagreement for the "scheduled flows decay" half of this article's thesis, and it should be treated as unresolved rather than as confirming evidence for either side.

Several headline figures in this article are explicitly gross or pre-cost (Patro-Piccotti-Wu's 18.2%, the yield-curve strategy's 5.9x, the EOM rebalancing 7.9% CAGR) while others are explicitly net (Aggeborn's Swedish CEF strategies, the corrected fallen-angel price-pressure test). These are not comparable numbers and should not be read as a ranked list of expected returns; each is conditioned on the universe, period and cost treatment stated alongside it.

## Strategy hypotheses this could seed

- [ ] **CEF discount-quintile long-only strategy, PRIIPs-filtered universe.** Restrict to CEFs and UK/EU investment trusts carrying a PRIIPs KID and tradable whole-share on IBKR; rank monthly by discount-history-implied expected return (Patro-Piccotti-Wu's construction) and hold the top quintile long-only against the fund's own trailing discount mean; test net of IBKR's fixed per-order minimum at monthly cadence. Refuted if the long-only leg (no short benchmark leg) fails to clear costs once the access-filtered universe shrinks the opportunity set.
- [ ] **Dual-class gap accumulation strategy.** For any dual-class pair in the tradable universe where the cheaper class trades at a gap versus the pricier class exceeding a calibrated band, buy only the cheaper class (no short leg, per Schultz-Shive's finding that one-sided trades do most of the correcting) and hold to convergence or a maximum period; benchmark against buying the pair indifferently. Refuted if concentrating purchases at gap-open dates does not outperform indifferent-timing accumulation net of costs.
- [ ] **Fallen-angel information filter.** Using public rating-action data, classify downgrades to high yield by whether the equity (if listed) reacted negatively around the announcement (informative) or did not (mandate-only, per Ambrose-Cai-Helwege's split); test whether a UCITS-accessible high-yield or fallen-angel ETF position entered only after uninformative downgrades shows a different reversal pattern than one entered after informative downgrades. Refuted if the split shows no difference, which would favor the pure forced-selling account over the information account (or vice versa).
- [ ] **Index-effect boundary test on a less-followed index.** Given the S&P 500 addition/deletion effect has decayed to statistically zero, test whether a smaller-AUM, less-passively-tracked index (a European small/mid-cap index, or an index with recent methodology changes) still shows a tradable reversion around reconstitution at whole-share, monthly-or-slower cadence net of costs. Refuted if the effect is already zero there too, which would suggest the decay generalizes beyond the S&P 500 rather than being specific to its uniquely deep passive-AUM base.
- [ ] **ETF flow-pressure long-only overlay.** Build a monthly public-data ETF-flow signal (shares-outstanding change, matching Brown-Davies-Ringgenberg's construction) restricted to UCITS-accessible ETFs; buy the bottom-decile (heaviest redemption-pressure) ETFs monthly and hold for the multi-month reversal window their evidence documents, taking only the long leg since shorting is excluded. Refuted if the long-only half of the long-short signal does not clear the fixed per-order cost floor at monthly rebalancing.
