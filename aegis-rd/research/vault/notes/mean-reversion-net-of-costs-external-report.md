# Mean Reversion as an Alpha Family — What Survives Net of Costs

*External due-diligence report. Sole context: the client brief on mean-reversion constructions for a retail, EU-accessible, long-side-first, weekly-to-monthly-cadence portfolio sleeve. All evidence below was gathered independently via web search and primary-source retrieval; nothing in this report draws on any other document.*

---

## 1. Answer

**The family does not survive net of costs as a single thing — it survives in three very unequal pieces, and the client's three-way split is directionally right but the boundaries are in the wrong place.**

- **Unconditioned statistical reversion (short-horizon reversal, RSI2/IBS-style oscillators) is the weakest of the three, not a middle case.** At the daily-to-weekly cadence where the edge is largest, it has been shown to fail realistic cost tests out-of-sample even before considering retail-specific frictions ([Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz 2018](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~afrazzin/pdf/Trading%20Cost%20of%20Asset%20Pricing%20Anomalies%20-%20Frazzini,%20Israel%20and%20Moskowitz.pdf) find short-term reversal's break-even capacity is $9.5B, an order of magnitude below momentum's $52–103B, at *institutional* trading-desk costs an order of magnitude cheaper than retail's; an independent 2019–2024 out-of-sample replication posts net Sharpe **−0.15** at 7bp round-trip after a positive net Sharpe of +0.34 in-sample — [equity-statarb decay case study](https://github.com/andrewzhang615-star/equity-statarb)). The classic form has "vanished entirely in most regions" per Robeco's own quant desk ([Blitz, van der Grient & Honarvar 2023](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4575689)). Where it still shows a positive net number, it requires large-cap universes and turnover-minimizing execution most retail brokers cannot replicate ([de Groot, Huij & Zhou 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbankfin.2011.07.015), 30–50bp/week net — but this is a pre-2018, large-fund-scale result, not confirmed since). At weekly/monthly retail cadence with fixed per-order commissions, the arithmetic is close to fatal: per-trigger edges of 35–38bp ([Pagonidis 2013](https://www.naaim.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/00V_Alexander_Pagonidis_The-IBS-Effect-Mean-Reversion-in-Equity-ETFs-1.pdf)) are comparable to or smaller than round-trip minimum commissions on small tickets.

- **Anchored reversion is the strongest surviving construction, and closed-end fund (CEF) discounts are the best-evidenced case.** Multiple independent studies, spanning 1996 to 2025, find discount mean reversion earns 7–18% annualized *net of transaction costs*, at monthly rebalancing cadence, in a market structurally too small and too retail-dominated for large institutional capital to fully compete away ([Aggeborn & Leander 2021](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/68332054/1148327_Exploiting_Discount_Mean_Reversion_and_High_Discounts_Isak_Aggeborn_Leander_2021.05.17.pdf), 7.4–14.6% net annualized on the Swedish CEF market; [Patro, Piccotti & Wu](https://www.gc.cuny.edu/sites/default/files/2021-07/Exploiting-Closed-End-Fund-Discounts_CUNY-talk.pdf), 18.2% annualized, Sharpe 1.92, US CEFs 1998–2011; discount persistence itself confirmed as recently as 2025, [Durmaz, Kim, Lee & Sun](https://doi.org/10.1515/snde-2024-0123)). This is the construction that best fits the client's cost lens: long-only, monthly, whole-share, and — via UCITS-domiciled or closed-end-fund-equivalent vehicles — EU-accessible.

- **Flow-conditioned reversion is real, recent, and the closest thing to a "genuine repricing filter," but it is event-driven rather than always-on, and mostly a *long-only, buy-after-the-shock* strategy for a retail account rather than a symmetric long-short one.** Fire-sale reversion is the best-replicated case: the original Coval-Stafford (2007) finding has been independently confirmed with European data, Chilean pension-fund natural-experiment data, and Chinese margin-call data, all post-2018 ([Aragon & Kim 2023](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v149y2023i3p578-609.html), 3–7% annual premium buying stocks exposed to future fire-sale risk; [Bian, He, Shue & Zhou 2018](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25040/w25040.pdf), 5pp reversal within 30–40 trading days). ETF-flow-conditioned reversion is the newest and best-fitted-to-EU-ETF-access construction: ETF creation/redemption flows predict a 1.1–2.0%/month return reversal persisting to 3–6 months ([Brown, Davies & Ringgenberg, "BDR" 2020](https://repository.arizona.edu/bitstream/handle/10150/661294/BDR_2020_ManualCites_V3.pdf?sequence=3)).

- **The conditioning variable the client suspects is where the alpha lives is real, but it is not one thing.** Volatility state (VIX) conditions the *magnitude* of statistical-reversal profits; turnover conditions their *persistence* ([Nagel 2012](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1573164); [NBER w30917](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30917/w30917.pdf)). Order imbalance is a genuine discriminator in the data but is not retail-accessible in real time and its documented profits are "unlikely to be much larger than brokerage commissions for individual investors" even before retail's higher cost structure ([Chordia & Subrahmanyam 2004](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/36-00.pdf)). Volume-based conditioning, intuitively plausible, *fails replication* in liquid large caps — high-volume moves revert *more*, not less ([equity-statarb](https://github.com/andrewzhang615-star/equity-statarb), citing Campbell-Grossman-Wang 1993).

**Confidence: medium.** High confidence on the *ranking* (anchored > flow-conditioned > statistical, inverted from a naive reading of "everyone can compute the reversion math") — this is corroborated by four independent, methodologically distinct literatures (asset-pricing trading-cost studies, CEF-puzzle studies, fire-sale studies, ETF-microstructure studies) that never cite each other yet converge on the same shape. Lower confidence on precise net-of-cost magnitudes at *this specific client's* cost structure, because almost none of the academic literature models a fixed per-order minimum commission on whole shares — it models proportional institutional cost curves. The report flags this gap explicitly in Open Questions.

## 2. Evidence

### 2.1 Unconditioned statistical reversion: a liquidity-provision wage, increasingly claimed by the wrong bidder

The canonical framing — Nagel's "reversal strategies are a proxy for the returns to liquidity provision" — still holds as a *mechanism*, but the *return to that mechanism* has been shrinking and concentrating exactly as the client suspected.

**Capacity/cost evidence.** [Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2018), "Trading Costs of Asset Pricing Anomalies"](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~afrazzin/pdf/Trading%20Cost%20of%20Asset%20Pricing%20Anomalies%20-%20Frazzini,%20Israel%20and%20Moskowitz.pdf) used ~$1.05 trillion of live institutional trades (1998–2013, 21 developed markets) to measure real transaction costs. Size, value, and momentum survive at $52–103B (US) break-even fund sizes, extending to $122–1,807B with cost-aware portfolio construction. Short-term reversal (STR) is the outlier: break-even size only **$9.5B US / $13B global**, and even optimized construction only reaches **$17B** — smallest of every anomaly tested, at costs an order of magnitude below what a retail broker charges. Their conclusion: STR is "most constrained by trading costs" of the four anomalies studied.

**Decay evidence, specifically for reversal.** [McLean & Pontiff (2016), "Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.12365) (97 predictors) find average post-publication decay of 58%, but flag that decay is *larger* for "predictors that can be constructed with only price and trading data" — i.e., reversal-type signals decay faster than fundamentals-based ones. [Falck, Rej & Thesmar (2021), "Why and How Systematic Strategies Decay"](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3845928) find publication year alone explains 30% of the variance in Sharpe decay across 72 factors, with overfitting proxies adding another 15% — arbitrage-related variables add almost nothing, suggesting *overfitting*, not crowding, is the primary decay driver, a distinction the client's due-diligence should track.

**A rigorous, labeled-as-such independent replication.** An open-source project explicitly designed to test "does short-horizon residual reversal in US equities still survive realistic execution?" ([equity-statarb, GitHub, 2026](https://github.com/andrewzhang615-star/equity-statarb)) — not peer-reviewed, but methodologically serious (leak-safe CRSP data, pre-registered Phase 2 hypotheses, deflated Sharpe correction, a genuinely sealed 2019–2024 out-of-sample test) — found: in-sample (2000–2018) net Sharpe @7bp = **+0.34** (breakeven 11.3bp); out-of-sample (2019–2024) net Sharpe = **−0.15** (breakeven fell to 4.2bp). Their volume-conditioning hypothesis was *rejected by the data* — in liquid large caps, high-volume days revert **more**, contradicting a common practitioner heuristic (citing Campbell, Grossman & Wang 1993).

**The disagreement.** [de Groot, Huij & Zhou (2012), "Another look at trading costs and short-term reversal profits"](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbankfin.2011.07.015) reach the opposite headline: restricting to large-cap US stocks and using turnover-minimizing portfolio construction, they report reversal profits of **30–50bp/week net of costs**, and up to 20bp/week net in Europe. This is pre-2018 and institutional-scale, so it does not resolve the client's question, but it establishes that universe restriction (large caps only) and execution sophistication (delay-based rebalancing) are the two levers that separate "STR is dead" from "STR survives" — both levers a retail account can only partially pull.

**Short-sale costs are the dominant, retail-relevant limit to arbitrage — and cut the other way from what "STR is dead" implies.** [Muravyev, Pearson & Pollet (2025), "Anomalies and Their Short-Sale Costs," *Journal of Finance*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.13501) test 162 published anomalies: average long-short return is a significant **0.14%/month before** short-sale costs, falling to an insignificant **−0.01%/month after** adjusting for stock borrow fees — and the effect is concentrated entirely in the **short leg** (12% of stock-months carry borrow fees >1%/year, and excluding them alone erases the anomaly). This is the single most important cross-cutting finding for the client's brief: it means the profits in the broad academic reversal literature are disproportionately a *short-selling* phenomenon. Since the client's mandate is long-side-first with no shorting infrastructure implied, this reframes the question — the client should not expect to capture textbook "STR" returns at all, because the textbook STR return *is* the short leg.

**The retail/practitioner corner (IBS, RSI2).** [Pagonidis (2013), "The IBS Effect: Mean Reversion in Equity ETFs"](https://www.naaim.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/00V_Alexander_Pagonidis_The-IBS-Effect-Mean-Reversion-in-Equity-ETFs-1.pdf), the best-cited practitioner-academic paper on daily reversal, finds average next-day return **+0.35%** when Internal Bar Strength < 0.20 vs **−0.13%** when IBS > 0.80, in US equity index ETFs since the early 1990s, delivering **>30% annual gross** before costs. Two facts materially limit its relevance to this client: (1) the effect **"only seems to exist in the U.S."** — international equity index ETFs (France, Australia, Germany, Spain, Switzerland, Taiwan, UK) "either do not exhibit it at all, or do so to a much smaller degree," which is a serious caveat for a UCITS-eligible, EU-accessible mandate; (2) a practitioner follow-up ([Jonathan Kinlay, 2019](https://jonathankinlay.com/2019/07/the-internal-bar-strength-indicator/)) documents a statistically flagged deterioration in the SPY IBS strategy's equity curve since ~2015, penetrating the 95% Monte Carlo bootstrap confidence band (though not yet the 99% band at time of writing).

**Practitioner disagreement on the whole RSI2/oscillator family.** Named practitioner Michael Harris ([Price Action Lab, 2018](https://www.priceactionlab.com/Blog/2018/08/rsi2-trading-strategy/)), author of *Fooled by Technical Analysis*, argues RSI2-based strategies fail his randomness tests: performance is acutely sensitive to exact overbought/oversold thresholds that "cannot be determined ex ante," with wildly different drawdowns (e.g., −24% vs +13% YTD) from small threshold changes — his conclusion is that RSI2 is not exploiting a structural anomaly but curve-fit to specific historical conditions. Against this, more recent vendor/blogger walk-forward tests report the opposite: 75% of out-of-sample windows profitable with a walk-forward efficiency ratio of 0.81 ([alphactor.ai, 2025](https://alphactor.ai/blog/mean-reversion-strategy) — note this is vendor-published content promoting a backtesting product, lower credibility), and an independent quant blogger's rigorous IS/OOS/Monte-Carlo-permutation-test battery on QQQ confirms a "consecutive down days" reversal effect holds out-of-sample with statistical significance ([QuantPTrader, 2026](https://quantpt.substack.com/p/rsi2-and-rsi3-on-qqq-120-conditions)), while finding **no** significant overbought/short-side edge on the same instrument. This is a genuine, unresolved practitioner-vs-practitioner disagreement (see §3).

### 2.2 Anchored reversion: where a genuine fair-value anchor still buys a premium

**Closed-end fund discounts — the strongest surviving anchored construction.** The foundational result is [Pontiff (1996), "Costly Arbitrage: Evidence from Closed-End Funds," *QJE*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1540-6261.00506) (arbitrage costs, not noise-trader risk, are the primary reason discounts persist). Post-2018 confirmations and refinements:
- [Aggeborn & Leander (2021), CBS thesis](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/68332054/1148327_Exploiting_Discount_Mean_Reversion_and_High_Discounts_Isak_Aggeborn_Leander_2021.05.17.pdf): Swedish CEF market, daily rebalancing on new information, **7.4–14.6% annualized abnormal returns net of transaction cost**, with the discount-mean-reversion component alone estimated at 2.2–9.3%. Explicitly incorporates a linear market-impact cost model (Li et al. 2019: ~43bp trade-day impact per 10% of ADV traded).
- [Patro, Piccotti & Wu (CUNY working paper)](https://www.gc.cuny.edu/sites/default/files/2021-07/Exploiting-Closed-End-Fund-Discounts_CUNY-talk.pdf): a parametric model using the *history* of premium innovations (not just the current discount) on US CEFs 1998–2011 delivers **18.2% annualized, Sharpe 1.918**, vs. 14.9%/Sharpe 1.52 for the naive Thompson/Pontiff quintile-sort benchmark — evidence that discount *path*, not just level, carries information, and that "no evidence of rational learning" (returns did not decay between the first and second half of their sample).
- [Durmaz, Kim, Lee & Sun (2025), "Trend Breaks and the Persistence of Closed-End Fund Discounts"](https://doi.org/10.1515/snde-2024-0123): the discount puzzle persists into 2025-vintage literature; discounts fluctuate around time-varying (regime-switching) trends rather than converging away, evidence the mispricing is structural, not a temporary pre-2018 artifact.
- Practitioner corroboration at real scale: Saba Capital's Boaz Weinstein has run an activist campaign since at least 2023 against BlackRock-managed CEFs trading at "gaping discounts," estimating the market-wide discount pool at **~$60B across a $500B CEF market** ([Bloomberg 2023](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-06-07/saba-s-boaz-weinstein-sees-value-in-closed-end-fund-arbitrage); [CNBC 2024](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/05/17/blackrock-boaz-weinstein-closed-end-funds.html)). Note the caveat below (§3): this is an *activist* strategy (board seats, forced conversions) the retail client cannot replicate directly, though it corroborates that discounts remain wide and real capital still finds them worth attacking.

**ADR arbitrage — persistent premium, but for structural reasons that block retail too.** [Patnaik, Shah & Stigler](https://macrofinance.nipfp.org.in/PDF/22_4sl_Shah-ADR.pdf) and the companion [NIPFP working paper](https://www.nipfp.org.in/media/medialibrary/2013/04/wp_2010_71.pdf) show Indian ADR premiums have a half-life of **27 months**, vs. ~0.5 months for Mexico/Brazil ADRs — the difference is Indian capital controls that permit ADR-to-local-share conversion only up to the original issue size, mechanically breaking the arbitrage once headroom is exhausted. This is precisely the "access constraint preserves a premium institutional capital cannot reach" case the client's sub-question asks about — but it cuts against tradability for *this* client too, since the premium is uncapturable by anyone lacking FII registration to trade the domestic Indian leg, retail or institutional. It is evidence *of* the phenomenon, not a *tradable instance* of it for a EU retail account. [Rösch (2021), *J. Financial Economics*](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finana/v142y2021i1p195-213.html) finds ADR-vs-home-share deviations persist ~12 minutes on average in liquid developed-market ADRs — an intraday, institutional/HFT-arbitraged phenomenon, out of scope for a daily-or-slower retail mandate.

**Dual-class share gaps — a genuine, long-only-friendly retail case.** [Schultz & Shive (2010), *J. Financial Economics*](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v98y2010i3p524-549.html): using TAQ data on 100 US dual-class pairs (1993–2006), abnormal returns from buying the cheap class and selling the expensive class "easily survive trading costs from bid-ask spreads," but the authors explicitly caveat they "are unable to say if they survive all implementation costs." The mechanically important finding for this client: **"long-short arbitrage plays a minor part in eliminating gaps, and one-sided trades correct most of them"** — i.e., ordinary (not necessarily short-selling) buyers of the cheap share class are doing most of the correcting, meaning a long-only version of this strategy (buy the discounted class, do not short the premium class) captures the majority of the mechanism. Practitioner replication ([Wesley Gray / Alpha Archetype, 2011](https://alphaarchitect.com/dual-class-shares-a-first-class-strategy/)) reports 82% of trades (3,025/3,687) profitable and a mean 9.75% return on trades where the spread exceeds 10%, average 213-day holding period — a cadence that fits the client's weekly-to-monthly mandate well, though it explicitly warns "trading this strategy in an intra-day fashion is absolutely suicidal for the retail investor" and recommends picking a favorite diverging pair and holding rather than trading every signal.

**Yield-curve slope and futures basis — largely out of the client's practical reach.** [Chua, Koh & Ramaswamy (2006), *Journal of Fixed Income*](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3488&context=lkcsb_research) find cash-neutral, duration-matched yield-curve mean-reversion trades outperformed benchmark bond/equity indices by 5.1–5.9x risk-adjusted (1973–2000), surviving realistic transaction costs, but explicitly note "market efficiency appeared to have improved... and the scope for excess returns has diminished" over their sample — and their strategies are constructed as duration-neutral long/short bond legs, which is a derivatives-adjacent construction outside the client's "no derivatives, no leverage" constraint. [Rossi, Zhang & Zhu (2025), "Short-Term Basis Reversal"](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5250499) find a robust weekly reversal pattern in the front-to-second futures contract spread (~10% annualized, t=3.91) across a broad commodity/equity-index futures universe — but this is explicitly a futures-market construction, out of scope per the brief.

### 2.3 Flow-conditioned reversion: the client's preferred conditioning variable, well-evidenced post-2018

**Fire sales — the best-replicated flow-conditioned effect.** [Coval & Stafford (2007), *J. Financial Economics*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2006.09.007) is the foundational identification: mutual-fund-flow-forced selling causes stock prices to drop and then rebound **7.74% over months 4–12** post-shock (t=4.43); combined long-short strategies earned "average annual abnormal returns well over 15%." Post-2018 independent replications, spanning different markets and identification strategies, all find the same reversal pattern:
- [Aragon & Kim (2023), *J. Financial Economics*](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v149y2023i3p578-609.html): an *ex-ante* fire-sale-risk exposure measure (stocks disproportionately owned by funds likely to face future outflows) earns **3–7% annually** buying high-exposure vs. low-exposure stocks — this reframes fire-sale reversion as a standing, holdable risk premium rather than an event-reactive trade, which may fit a monthly-rebalance retail cadence better than event-chasing.
- [Bian, He, Shue & Zhou (2018), NBER w25040](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25040/w25040.pdf): Chinese margin-call-driven fire sales during the 2015 crash; stocks disproportionately held by near-margin-call accounts underperform by ~5pp over 10–15 days, then the gap "closes once liquidity returned to the market" within 30–40 trading days.
- A quasi-natural experiment in Chilean pension-fund forced selling (2007 regulatory shock) finds a clean, fundamentals-unrelated 4% price decline followed by reversion only after **4–6 months** ([ScienceDirect / Elsevier](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1042957316300146)).
- A direct Coval-Stafford replication using post-2017 data confirms the **~20-month** reversion window still holds, and finds firms with better accounting quality suffer smaller fire-sale discounts in the first place ([Mercado, Rubio & Scapin, Warwick WRAP repository](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/177621/7/WRAP-Role-accounting-quality-mutual-fund-fire-sales-2023.pdf)) — a genuinely post-2018 out-of-sample confirmation of the original mechanism.

**ETF creation/redemption flows — the best-fit flow-conditioned construction for a UCITS-ETF-heavy EU portfolio.** [Ben-David, Franzoni & Moussawi (NBER w20071), "Do ETFs Increase Volatility?"](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20071/revisions/w20071.rev0.pdf) find ETF arbitrage/flow-driven price pressure on underlying stocks reverses over 5–20 days; for S&P 500 stocks, a one-standard-deviation ETF-mispricing shock predicts **−0.344%** over the following month, and the reversal is stronger for stocks with higher ETF ownership. The more directly tradable construction: [Brown, Davies & Ringgenberg ("BDR"), Arizona repository, 2020](https://repository.arizona.edu/bitstream/handle/10150/661294/BDR_2020_ManualCites_V3.pdf?sequence=3) show ETF flows (creations minus redemptions) are a public, observable signal that predicts *future* returns on both the ETF and its underlying basket — a portfolio short high-flow ETFs / long low-flow ETFs earns **1.1–2.0%/month** at one-month horizon, with predictability persisting to 3- and 6-month horizons (2007–2016 sample). Because ETF share counts (a proxy for flow) are publicly disclosed, this is a genuinely retail-implementable signal at monthly cadence, and translatable to a long-only tilt (favor recently-outflow-heavy ETFs, avoid recently-inflow-heavy ones) without requiring shorting.

**Index rebalancing — a slow, long-only-friendly residual, but most of the edge is already claimed.** [SSRN, "The Avoidable Costs of Index Rebalancing"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4099610): in the year following an S&P 500 index change, discretionary deletions beat additions by **22% on average**; delaying reconstitution trades by 3–12 months captures up to **23bp/year**. This is explicitly a long-only-compatible, monthly-cadence construction — buy recent forced-out index deletions, wait for the mechanical selling pressure to abate. However, a companion paper on institutional calendar rebalancing ([SSRN, "The Unintended Consequences of Rebalancing"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5122748)) finds the *predictable* piece (a 17bp next-day equity decline following overweight-driven institutional selling) is already the target of front-running strategies — and 17bp is smaller than a typical retail round-trip cost, meaning this particular sub-flavor does not survive the client's cost lens even though it is real and measured ($16B/year aggregate cost to institutional rebalancers).

### 2.4 Conditioning variables: what actually discriminates a bounce from a break

- **Volatility/stress state (VIX).** [Nagel (2012), "Evaporating Liquidity," *Review of Financial Studies*](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1573164): reversal-strategy returns and conditional Sharpe ratios rise strongly and predictably with VIX; even industry-portfolio reversals (which have low unconditional returns) produce high Sharpe ratios in high-VIX regimes. This is the strongest, most replicated conditioning variable in the literature and directly supports the client's belief (a) that unconditioned reversal profits concentrate in stress periods — a genuinely checkable, weekly-cadence-compatible signal for a retail account (VIX is public, free, daily).
- **Volatility vs. turnover as differential conditioners.** [NBER w30917, "Reversals and the Returns to Liquidity Provision"](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30917/w30917.pdf): stock-level volatility predicts *faster, initially stronger* reversals (inventory-risk compensation), while *low* turnover predicts *more persistent, ultimately stronger* reversals (longer inventory-unwind duration) — these are different mechanisms with different tradeable implications: high-volatility names revert fast (needs daily monitoring, poor retail fit); low-turnover names revert slowly over weeks-to-months (better retail fit, but likely also less liquid/higher spread cost, a tension the client should weigh explicitly).
- **Order imbalance.** Real and replicated across markets ([Chordia, Roll & Subrahmanyam](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/28-00.pdf); [Andrade, Chang & Seasholes 2008, Taiwan](http://moya.bus.miami.edu/~sandrade/Andrade_Chang_Seasholes_JFE2008.pdf), where a zero-cost imbalance-sorted portfolio earns 52bp in week one, cumulating to 145bp over ten weeks) but two practical problems for this client: (1) it requires real-time order-flow/margin-account data not published to EU retail brokers in most jurisdictions; (2) even where measurable, [Hasbrouck-style individual-stock studies](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/36-00.pdf) find imbalance-based trading profits are "unlikely to be much larger than brokerage commissions for individual investors" — i.e., the signal is real but was already too small for retail cost structures even *before* accounting for the fact retail cannot observe it in real time.
- **Volume — a claimed discriminator that fails replication in the direction commonly assumed.** The intuitive heuristic ("low-volume moves are noise, high-volume moves are informed and won't revert") is contradicted by the equity-statarb replication: in liquid large caps, **high-volume moves revert more, not less** ([citing Campbell, Grossman & Wang 1993](https://github.com/andrewzhang615-star/equity-statarb)). Pagonidis similarly finds the IBS effect is *stronger*, not weaker, after high-volume days for US ETFs.
- **Presence/absence of news and earnings.** The equity-statarb project's pre-registered test found an earnings-announcement exclusion filter *helps* (earnings-window reversal signals are actually inverted — earnings moves drift per post-earnings-announcement-drift, not revert), consistent with the fire-sale and ETF-flow literatures' shared logic that a *reversal* signal should specifically exclude information-driven moves to work.
- **Factor-adjustment of the move.** [Da, Liu & Schaumburg](https://academicweb.nd.edu/~zda/Reversal.pdf) show a residual-return-based reversal strategy (stripping out cash-flow-news-correlated component using analyst forecasts) roughly quadruples the risk-adjusted return of naive reversal (1.34%/month alpha vs. 0.33% for standard reversal, 1982–2009 large-cap US sample) and survives transaction-cost analysis — direct evidence that "removing the informational component of the move" is exactly the kind of conditioning that separates tradable displacement from real repricing, operationalizing the client's own hypothesis.

### 2.5 Edge existence — who pays, and why they keep paying

- **Unconditioned statistical reversion:** the payer is, structurally, a capital-constrained financial intermediary bearing inventory risk in stress periods ([Nagel 2012](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1573164)); the *receiver* of that structural premium today is disproportionately proprietary/HFT market-making capital, not retail. [Biais, Declerck & Moinas (BIS working paper)](https://www.bis.org/publ/work563.pdf) show proprietary traders (fast and slow) systematically place *contrarian* marketable orders after price moves and are consistently profitable doing so — including through the 2010 Greek crisis, i.e., they do not withdraw when liquidity provision is most valuable. This is a structural edge, but one already claimed by better-capitalized, faster participants at the frequencies where it is largest; it is why the daily/weekly retail-accessible slice has decayed (§2.1) even though the macro mechanism (Nagel) remains theoretically sound.
- **Closed-end fund discounts:** the payer is retail-sentiment-driven demand mispricing (the original Lee-Shleifer-Thaler noise-trader story), sustained because the CEF market's small individual fund sizes make it uneconomic for large institutional arbitrage capital to scale into ([Pontiff 1996](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1540-6261.00506)); Kohl (2013), cited within [Aggeborn & Leander](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/68332054/1148327_Exploiting_Discount_Mean_Reversion_and_High_Discounts_Isak_Aggeborn_Leander_2021.05.17.pdf), adds an "optionality premium" explanation — investors demand compensation for holding a portfolio they did not personally select. This is durable *because* it is structural to fund size, not a behavioral fad expected to fade — the strongest "structural, not behavioral" answer in this report.
- **Fire sales:** the payer is a forced seller — a mutual fund facing redemptions, or a margin account approaching a call — a structural feature of the open-end fund and margin-lending industries that is not going away. The buyer/liquidity-provider is whoever is willing to hold through the temporary dislocation; the fact this has replicated across the US, Europe, Chile, and China, in different decades and regulatory regimes, is strong evidence the mechanism is structural rather than a single-episode artifact.
- **ETF flow reversal:** more ambiguous. Authorized Participants mechanically correct NAV gaps (a structural, low-risk arbitrage business), but the underlying flows that create the mispricing in the first place trace to retail/investor demand shocks whose persistence depends on whether retail flow-chasing behavior itself persists — a genuinely open, partly-behavioral question (see §4).
- **Index rebalancing:** the payer is the index-fund industry's mechanical, calendar-announced trading — a structural, permanent feature of index investing's growth — but most of the predictable edge around the *announcement-to-effective-date* window is already claimed by dedicated index-arbitrage desks; only the slower, multi-month "buy recent forced-out deletions" residual survives for a slower participant.
- **A surviving-but-unexplained construction, flagged per the brief's instruction:** dual-class share gaps ([Schultz & Shive 2010](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v98y2010i3p524-549.html)) lack a clean "who's forced, who's constrained" story — the paper's own finding that ordinary one-sided trades (not big arbitrageurs) do most of the correcting suggests this may be closer to a limited-attention/limited-scale inefficiency than a structural liquidity-provision premium, which is a genuine reason for caution about its durability even though the historical numbers are attractive.

### 2.6 The retail cost frontier: what a fixed-minimum, whole-share account can actually clear

Concrete cost data for a EU-domiciled retail account (Interactive Brokers, the most commonly cited reference broker in this space): US shares/ETFs, **$0.005/share, $1 minimum** per order (fixed plan) ([IBKR US pricing](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/en/pricing/commissions-stocks-europe.php)); Western European shares/UCITS ETFs, **€3/£3 minimum per trade** (fixed plan) or 0.05% of trade value with a €1.25 minimum (tiered plan) ([IBKR Europe pricing](https://www.interactivebrokers.eu/en/pricing.php); [detailed fixed-vs-tiered comparison](https://www.bankeronwheels.com/ibkr-fixed-vs-tiered/)). Worked through: a €2,000 UCITS ETF round-trip at the €3 fixed minimum costs **~30bp**; the same round-trip on a €500 ticket costs **~120bp** — confirming the brief's assumed 10–80bp range is realistic for mid-size tickets but degrades sharply for small ones, which mechanically pushes a small account toward *fewer, larger tickets* and *away from daily rebalancing*.

This cost structure directly determines which of the constructions above survive arithmetically:

- **Survives:** CEF discount mean reversion (explicitly monthly-rebalance in the literature — [Aggeborn & Leander](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/68332054/1148327_Exploiting_Discount_Mean_Reversion_and_High_Discounts_Isak_Aggeborn_Leander_2021.05.17.pdf) use daily *signal* evaluation but the underlying discount mean-reversion half-life is measured in months, so a monthly-rebalance retail approximation captures most of the effect); ETF-flow-conditioned tilting (BDR's 1–6 month predictability horizon is a natural fit for monthly rebalancing); fire-sale/index-deletion buying (multi-month to multi-quarter holding periods per §2.3, inherently low-turnover).
- **Does not survive:** daily-cadence statistical reversal (IBS, RSI2 flips). Per-trigger edges of 35–38bp ([Pagonidis](https://www.naaim.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/00V_Alexander_Pagonidis_The-IBS-Effect-Mean-Reversion-in-Equity-ETFs-1.pdf)) are comparable to or smaller than round-trip minimum commissions on anything but the largest tickets, and the strategy requires near-daily monitoring and trading — the opposite of what a fixed-minimum-commission cost structure rewards. This is true *even where the underlying statistical effect is real* (Pagonidis's own data, the QuantPTrader OOS replication) — it is a pure arithmetic-of-cadence problem, not a "does the edge exist" problem.
- **Ambiguous / needs live-cost verification:** dual-class share arbitrage (attractive average-holding-period of 213 days fits well, but requires positions in specific paired securities that may not always be available as UCITS-wrapped or liquid-enough-for-whole-share EU listings — flagged as an open question in §4).

## 3. Disagreements

1. **Does short-term statistical reversal survive costs at all, even restricted to large caps?** *Academic camp is split.* [Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2018)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~afrazzin/pdf/Trading%20Cost%20of%20Asset%20Pricing%20Anomalies%20-%20Frazzini,%20Israel%20and%20Moskowitz.pdf) and the independent [equity-statarb](https://github.com/andrewzhang615-star/equity-statarb) out-of-sample replication say no (or barely, at institutional cost levels far below retail's). [de Groot, Huij & Zhou (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbankfin.2011.07.015) say yes, given a large-cap-only universe and delay-based turnover reduction. **The evidence favors "no, or only marginally, and shrinking"** — the de Groot et al. result is pre-2018 and has not been independently reconfirmed since, while three independent post-2018 studies (Frazzini et al.'s capacity analysis, the equity-statarb OOS test, and Blitz et al.'s cross-region finding that the classic effect has "vanished entirely in most regions") all point the same direction. This is the client's belief (a) essentially confirmed, but more starkly than the brief's framing suggests — it is not "competed down to the market-maker cost frontier," it looks closer to gone at any cost frontier above institutional.

2. **Is the RSI2/IBS retail-practitioner strategy family a real, robust effect or a curve-fit one?** Named practitioner Michael Harris ([Price Action Lab 2018](https://www.priceactionlab.com/Blog/2018/08/rsi2-trading-strategy/)) argues it is statistically indistinguishable from random given its extreme sensitivity to un-knowable-in-advance parameter thresholds. Against this, a rigorous independent quant blogger's IS/OOS/Monte-Carlo-permutation battery ([QuantPTrader 2026](https://quantpt.substack.com/p/rsi2-and-rsi3-on-qqq-120-conditions)) finds the *consecutive-down-days* variant of the same family holds up out-of-sample with statistical significance on QQQ, while finding *no* significant overbought/short-side signal — i.e., even the pro-RSI2 evidence is asymmetric and partial, not a blanket confirmation. Multiple vendor-published "still works" claims ([QuantifiedStrategies.substack](https://quantifiedstrategies.substack.com/p/larry-connors-rsi-strategy-still), [alphactor.ai](https://alphactor.ai/blog/mean-reversion-strategy)) should be weighted lower — they are marketing content for backtesting/education products, not independent research, and are labeled as such here per the brief's evidentiary rules. **Unresolved; genuinely split even within the practitioner camp**, and this report did not find a peer-reviewed academic paper directly adjudicating RSI2/IBS specifically (only the related, more rigorous Pagonidis 2013 paper on IBS, which itself flags post-2013 US-specific deterioration and non-existence outside the US).

3. **Does academic-anomaly decay stem primarily from arbitrage capital crowding in, or from statistical overfitting in the original studies?** McLean & Pontiff's (2016) framing implies capital-driven, publication-informed trading. Falck, Rej & Thesmar (2021) directly test this and find overfitting proxies explain far more of the cross-sectional variance in decay than arbitrage-related proxies do. **Evidence favors overfitting as the larger factor** for the broad anomaly zoo, but this report notes the two mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and reversal-specific evidence (real capacity constraints documented by Frazzini et al., real crowding documented in equity factor microstructure by [Zooming In on Equity Factor Crowding, arXiv 2001.04185](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.04185)) suggests reversal in particular may be more capital-crowding-driven than the average anomaly in the McLean-Pontiff sample, which skews toward accounting-based signals.

4. **Do closed-end fund and dual-class arbitrage strategies survive "all implementation costs," or only quoted bid-ask spreads?** [Schultz & Shive (2010)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v98y2010i3p524-549.html) explicitly hedge their own claim on this point for dual-class shares. The CEF literature is more confident (Aggeborn & Leander model market impact explicitly and still find 7.4–14.6% net), but neither literature models a retail fixed-per-order-minimum commission structure — both model proportional/bps-based institutional-style costs. **This is a genuine gap this report cannot resolve from existing evidence** rather than a disagreement between sources; flagged as the top item in Open Questions.

## 4. Open questions

1. **No study in this literature models a fixed-per-order-minimum, whole-share retail cost structure directly.** Every net-of-cost figure found (CEF discount arbitrage, dual-class gaps, index-deletion buying) uses either a proportional bps cost model or an institutional live-trading dataset. A follow-up round should specifically search for retail-broker-cost-aware backtests of CEF discount strategies (the strongest anchored construction) to confirm the 7–18% net figures survive a €3–€1 minimum-per-trade structure at realistic (not idealized) position sizes for a small account.

2. **Does the ETF-flow-conditioned reversal signal (BDR 2020, 1.1–2.0%/month) hold on UCITS-domiciled EU ETFs**, where creation/redemption disclosure regimes and AP structures may differ from the US sample the paper uses? This is directly load-bearing for the client's EU-accessible mandate and was not found addressed in the literature searched.

3. **Is the dual-class share gap construction durable, or a shrinking historical artifact?** The evidence base (Schultz & Shive 2010, Alpha Archetype 2011) is over a decade old with no post-2018 replication found in this round. Given the "who's on the other side" answer is weaker/more ambiguous than for CEFs or fire sales (§2.5), and given dual-class share structures have themselves become less common in new IPOs in some markets, a follow-up should specifically search for a 2020s-vintage replication or decay study.

4. **How much of the CEF discount premium is now claimed by activist capital (Saba/Weinstein-style campaigns) rather than being available to a passive discount-buyer?** If activist funds are increasingly forcing discount-narrowing outcomes (tender offers, conversions) rather than waiting for organic mean reversion, the *character* of the return (event-driven, lumpier) may differ from the smooth mean-reversion return modeled in the academic backtests. This report found the activist angle (Bloomberg/CNBC) but did not find a study quantifying how much of measured CEF arbitrage returns since 2023 are attributable to activist catalysts versus organic discount mean reversion.

5. **Order-imbalance and cross-stock price-pressure conditioning variables (Andrade-Chang-Seasholes) were demonstrated on Taiwan margin-account data — does an equivalent, EU-observable proxy exist** (e.g., published short-interest data, ETF flow data as an imbalance proxy) that could operationalize this conditioning variable for a retail account without requiring exchange-level order-flow feeds?

6. **This report reached what it judges to be theoretical saturation** after two full WIDE→NARROW rounds plus targeted fills: independent literatures (asset-pricing/trading-cost, CEF-puzzle, fire-sale, ETF-microstructure, order-imbalance/liquidity-provision, retail-practitioner blogs/forums) were converging on the same shape (anchored > flow-conditioned > statistical) rather than surfacing new schools of thought, and the final round's new queries (yield curve, retail cost frontier, IBS robustness, short-sale-cost literature) returned confirmatory rather than contradictory evidence. The main residual uncertainty is not "what does the field believe" but "does the field's belief translate arithmetically to a specific small retail account" (Open Question 1) — which is a modeling exercise, not a further literature search, and is explicitly out of scope for this knowledge-survey assignment.
